# Patience as Competitive Advantage

**Source:** [[Nomad Partnership Letters]] — developed across multiple
letters from 2001 onwards; most directly stated in the 2005 and 2013
letters.

## Summary

Sleep's central claim is that patience is not a personality virtue
that produces better investment outcomes incidentally; it is a
structural competitive advantage that most investors cannot access
because of institutional constraints. The investor who can genuinely
hold a position for five to ten years is competing in a different
and far less crowded market than the investor who must show results
quarterly.

Sleep identifies three linked components that make patience a
competitive structure rather than merely a temperamental preference:
a patient investor base, a genuinely long-duration analytical
framework ([[Destination Analysis]]), and the deliberate avoidance
of trading activity as an end in itself.

## The mechanism

Most institutional investment managers face a structural mismatch
between their time horizon and the time horizon of the businesses they
own. A quarterly benchmark comparison forces portfolio decisions that
are often irrational from a multi-year perspective: selling positions
that have underperformed for twelve months even when the underlying
thesis is intact; buying positions that have recently outperformed
even when they have already been fully valued; avoiding high-quality
businesses that are temporarily depressed because being wrong for
a full year is a career risk.

The investor who has a genuinely patient investor base — one that
will not redeem capital because of a bad quarter or even a bad year —
faces none of these pressures. They can hold a position that is
temporarily cheap while the thesis develops, add to positions during
price dislocations without career risk, and avoid buying inferior
positions merely to look active. Each of these freedoms translates
directly into better investment outcomes.

Sleep's observation from the 2005 letter: the "aggregate patience
of its investor base" is one of the most important structural features
of any investment fund. He and Zakaria selected their partners
accordingly — the Nomad Partnership was primarily held by charities
and endowments whose investment mandates were genuinely long-duration
and whose governance structures insulated them from short-term pressure
to redeem.

## The equity yield curve

Sleep uses the analogy of a bond yield curve to make the patience
argument quantitative. A bond yield curve shows that longer-duration
bonds typically offer higher yields, compensating investors for
accepting the uncertainty of longer time horizons. Sleep argues
that equities have an analogous structure: the expected return from
a ten-year holding period in a quality business is higher than
the expected return from a one-year holding period, not merely
because more time allows compounding but because the market's short-
term volatility is noise relative to the business's long-term
intrinsic value growth.

The practical consequence: investors with short time horizons are
structurally accepting a lower expected return than investors with
long time horizons, even when holding the same securities. A fund
that forces short-horizon decisions through quarterly benchmarking
is selling cheap options on long-run returns to investors who can
afford to hold them.

## Inactivity as value-added

The 2013 interim letter makes the point explicitly: "the decision
not to do something is still an active decision; it is just that the
accountants don't capture it." Sleep and Zakaria report with
satisfaction that their purchase and sale transactions had "all but
ground to a halt" by 2013.

This is counter-cultural in investment management, where activity
is often perceived as evidence of diligence. Sleep's claim is that
when the portfolio contains the businesses you want, at prices you
are satisfied with, and the underlying theses remain intact, then
doing nothing is the correct investment decision — not a failure of
initiative. The paradox is that the most active management decision
may be the explicit, ongoing decision not to trade.

The 2012 annual letter describes the same discipline at the holding
level: Sleep and Zakaria routinely attempt to "kill" their investments
— asking whether, if they did not already own the position, they would
buy it today at the current price. If the answer is yes, inactivity
is warranted. If the answer is no, it is not inactivity but analytical
drift that is being preserved.

Munger is quoted directly in the 2013 interim letter: "you make your
real money sitting on your assets." The observation is not novel —
it is central to Buffett's framing and to Fisher's [[When to Sell]]
analysis — but Sleep's letters give it the most systematic treatment.

## The patient investor as management support

A secondary argument in the letters: patient investors enable the
managements of the businesses they own to make better decisions.
A founder-managed business whose shareholders demand quarterly earnings
growth will, eventually, make investments that serve the quarterly
metric rather than the long-run business. A business whose shareholders
are willing to hold through multi-year investment cycles — Amazon's
fulfilment infrastructure build-out, Costco's international expansion,
AirAsia's route development — can make those investments without being
penalised in ways that affect management's ability to retain staff
and credibility.

Sleep is not merely making a claim about investor returns. He is
making a claim that the composition of a company's shareholder base
affects the quality of its management decisions, and that a patient
shareholder base is a genuine (though hard-to-quantify) contribution
to the businesses it owns.

## Fisher's parallel: the three-year rule

Fisher's framework in [[Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits]] contains a
practical patience mechanism that complements Sleep's structural argument
without replicating it. Fisher's three-year rule: when a purchase fails
to perform within three years — not because anything specific has changed
the investment thesis, but simply because the market has not yet recognised
the value — he would sell. The rule served as a backstop against holding
indefinitely out of stubbornness, and as a discipline of regular
reconsideration. He violated it exactly once in his career.

The three-year rule is a different kind of patience argument from Sleep's.
Sleep argues that the patient investor earns a structural premium because
she faces less competition at long time horizons. Fisher's argument is more
specific: patience is warranted for as long as the investment thesis is intact,
subject to a three-year test of whether the thesis is operationally correct.
If the thesis is correct but the market has not recognised it within three
years, Fisher's response is to re-examine the thesis rather than simply
to extend the hold further. This is a more exacting standard than Sleep's
near-permanent framework, but it is also more protective against the
specific failure mode of holding a quietly failing thesis under the cover
of patience.

## Contradictions

Sleep's argument that patience is structural sits in tension with
Fisher's more conditional approach in [[When to Sell]]. Fisher
identifies three valid reasons to sell, including the discovery of
a clearly better opportunity. Sleep's framework effectively reduces
this to two conditions (thesis breakdown, quality deterioration) by
treating [[Reinvestment Risk]] as so substantial that "clearly better
opportunity" is almost never a reliable guide. The tension is examined
in [[Selling vs Holding Forever]].
