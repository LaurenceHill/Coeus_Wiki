# Selling vs Holding Forever

**The tension:** Fisher identifies three and only three valid reasons
to sell a position. Sleep argues that the portfolio should tend toward
"near permanent" holdings where the design goal is to never sell.
These frameworks produce different decisions from the same evidence.

## Buffett's position

Before the Fisher vs Sleep tension, a third voice is useful as context.
Buffett's own stated position, from the 1988 letter: "Our favourite holding
period is forever." This is not a vow but a disposition — the revealed
preference of an investor who has found that exiting quality businesses
is almost always a mistake in retrospect. The 1987 letter provides the
complementary framing: Berkshire holds three "permanent" common stock
positions — Capital Cities/ABC, GEICO, the Washington Post — which it
would not sell even if they appeared far overpriced, treating them like
controlled businesses rather than tradeable securities.

Buffett's willingness to hold indefinitely rests on two conditions: the
prospective return on equity of the underlying business remains satisfactory,
and management remains competent and honest. These are roughly Fisher's
first two reasons to sell — in negative form. What Buffett explicitly rejects
is Fisher's third reason: the discovery of a clearly better opportunity.
"We are quite content to hold any security indefinitely, so long as \[those
two conditions hold]." This convergence with Sleep's position, reached
independently by a different route, strengthens the case against using
Fisher's third reason as a trigger.

## The two positions

**Fisher's three reasons to sell** (from _Common Stocks and Uncommon
Profits_, summarised in the wiki as [[When to Sell]]) are: first,
the original analysis was wrong and the business was never as good as
assessed; second, the business has genuinely deteriorated and no longer
meets the criteria that justified purchase; third, a clearly better
opportunity is available and the proceeds from the sale would, even
after taxes and friction, produce a better return in the alternative.

Fisher regards these as necessary and sufficient: any sale motivated
by a reason other than one of these three is likely to be a mistake.
He is explicit that selling because a stock has risen a lot, or because
of macroeconomic anxiety, or to realise a tax loss, or because the
investor is uncertain, is almost always wrong.

**Sleep's near-permanent holding** position, developed across the
2008–2013 Nomad letters and articulated in the 2021 postamble, argues
that a portfolio built around [[Scale Economics Shared]] businesses
should effectively never be sold, unless one of those businesses
deteriorates or the thesis breaks down. The language Sleep uses —
"near permanent" — is deliberate. He is not arguing for an absolute
prohibition on sales; he is arguing that the correct decision, under
almost all circumstances, is to do nothing.

Sleep explicitly excludes Fisher's third reason. The discovery of a
"clearly better opportunity" is not, in his framework, a valid reason
to exit a quality compounder, because the expected cost of being wrong
about the alternative (the [[Reinvestment Risk]]) is too large. The
investor who sells a great business at fair value to buy a different
business at a discount is assuming they can identify the discount
reliably, that the discount will close, and that the compounding of
the original business would not have exceeded the alternative's
recovery. Sleep's argument is that these assumptions are rarely
satisfied in practice.

## Where the conflict is real

The substantive disagreement is over Fisher's third reason. Sleep is
making a claim about the reliability of comparative valuation judgements:
that the investor who believes they have found something "clearly better"
is very often wrong, and that the realistic expected cost of acting on
that belief — selling a high-quality compounder and reinvesting in
something else — exceeds the realistic expected benefit. Fisher's
framework gives the investor the benefit of the doubt on their
comparative judgement; Sleep does not.

This is partly empirical and partly psychological. Empirically, the
distribution of investment returns is highly skewed: a small number
of positions produce most of the gains over a career, and identifying
the substitutes for those positions in advance is very hard. A framework
that permits selling the great position (Fisher's third reason) risks
systematically cutting those winners and replacing them with something
less good. Psychologically, the temptation to find a "clearly better"
alternative is especially strong after a position has risen substantially,
because the investor then faces the anchoring problem of believing the
original position is now "expensive" relative to where it was bought.

## Where the conflict dissolves

On Fisher's first and second reasons, the two frameworks agree fully.
A position where the original analysis was wrong should be exited,
and a position in a business that has genuinely deteriorated should
also be exited. Sleep's "kill" test — asking whether he would buy the
business today at the current price — is operationally equivalent to
Fisher's first two reasons: a no answer indicates either that the
original thesis was flawed or that the business has since changed.

Both frameworks also agree that selling due to price movement alone
is almost always wrong. Fisher's insistence that a risen stock price
is not a reason to sell is Sleep's near-permanent holding in different
language.

## The failure mode on each side

Fisher's framework, applied with insufficient discipline, creates the
risk of excessive portfolio turnover. An investor who is always looking
for a "clearly better opportunity" will find one — or convince themselves
they have found one — with sufficient frequency that the portfolio
turns over constantly, incurring taxes, friction, and [[Reinvestment Risk]] repeatedly. The third reason is the one most susceptible to
rationalisation.

Sleep's near-permanent framework, applied without discipline, creates
the risk of [[Denial, Anchoring, Drift]] — staying in a deteriorated
position long past the point where the evidence warrants exit, because
the psychological disposition is toward holding. The "near permanent"
framing can become an excuse to avoid the hard work of re-evaluating a
thesis that is quietly failing.

The practical synthesis suggested by the wiki's reading of both sources:
hold until Fisher's first or second reasons apply; treat Fisher's third
reason with extreme scepticism, as Sleep advises; and use Sleep's
kill test regularly to guard against drift.
