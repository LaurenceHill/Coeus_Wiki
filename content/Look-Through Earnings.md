# Look-Through Earnings

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1980 letter,
"Non-Controlled Ownership Earnings" section (first canonical
formulation); reinforced in 1981 and 1984; 1990 letter names the
framework explicitly; 1991 and 1992 letters provide the worked tables
and extend the concept to individual investors; 1996 and 1997 letters
provide updated totals ($1,522M and $1,930M respectively); 1998 letter
skips the calculation (General Re acquired at year-end, making a
meaningful figure impractical).

## Summary

The principle that the value of a business to its owner is the
business's earnings, regardless of whether those earnings are
distributed as dividends, retained at the operating company, or
captured under accounting consolidation rules. The 1980 letter is the
first place in the modern letters series where the principle is
stated cleanly:

> The value to Berkshire Hathaway of retained earnings is not
> determined by whether we own 100%, 50%, 20% or 1% of the businesses
> in which they reside. Rather, the value of those retained earnings
> is determined by the use to which they are put and the subsequent
> level of earnings produced by that usage. This is true whether we
> determine the usage, or whether managers we did not hire — but did
> elect to join — determine that usage. (It's the act that counts, not
> the actors.)

The 1980 letter pairs the abstract principle with a memorable image:
*"if a tree grows in a forest partially owned by us, but we don't
record the growth in our financial statements, we still own part of
the tree."* The 1981 letter reinforces the principle by noting that
Berkshire's non-recorded ownership earnings — its share of the
undistributed earnings of GEICO, Washington Post, RJR, General Foods,
and others — already exceeds reported operating earnings, and that
the gap will widen rather than close.

## Why it matters

GAAP accounting treats less-than-20%-owned holdings as if only the
dividends matter. The implication is that an owner with a 1% stake in
a company that retains all earnings reports zero income from the
holding, even when the underlying business is compounding. Buffett's
counter-claim is that the accounting picture is wrong about value, and
that the investor should reason about the underlying earnings rather
than the reported ones.

The principle has three operational consequences. The first is
selection: a fractional owner should evaluate retained earnings the
same way a controlling owner would — by what they are spent on and
what return they earn. The second is patience: if the manager is
deploying retained earnings well, the value compounds inside the
business and shows up in market price over time, but the timing is
unpredictable. The third is reporting honesty: an annual report that
ignores look-through earnings systematically understates the
underlying economics and trains shareholders to think about the wrong
quantity.

## The retained-earnings test

The 1980 and 1981 letters together formulate a test that becomes a
cornerstone of Buffett's later writing: a dollar of retained earnings
should produce *at least* a dollar of subsequent market value. The
1981 letter states it directly: *"if a group of non-controlled
companies is selected with reasonable skill, the group result should
be quite satisfactory."* The 1981 letter also gives the failure mode:
*"market recognition of retained earnings also will be unevenly
realized among companies. It will be disappointingly low or negative
in cases where earnings are employed non-productively, and far
greater than dollar-for-dollar of retained earnings in cases of
companies that achieve high returns with their augmented capital."*

The test is not "did the company earn money" but "did the retained
portion produce value commensurate with its retention." A company
that earns 14% but retains earnings invested at 6% is destroying
shareholder value in the retained portion even though reported
earnings rise. Look-through earnings is the corrective lens.

## How this links to share buybacks

The 1980 letter contains a pause-for-commercial about share buybacks:
*"if a fine business is selling in the market place for far less than
intrinsic value, what more certain or more profitable utilization of
capital can there be than significant enlargement of the interests of
all owners at that bargain price?"* The buyback endorsement follows
directly from look-through earnings logic — if retained capital can
buy more of the business at sub-intrinsic prices, the retained dollar
becomes worth more than a dollar in subsequent market value, almost
mechanically. The 1980 letter cites GEICO's reduction of share count
from 34.2 million to 21.6 million in two years as a worked example.
GEICO's retained earnings, in this period, were doing more for
Berkshire as a fractional owner than they would have done as
distributed dividends.

## The 1984 dollar-retention test

The 1984 letter sharpens the retained-earnings test into a single
diagnostic that becomes Buffett's recurring tool for distinguishing
businesses that should retain from businesses that should distribute.
The condition is exact: *"unrestricted earnings should be retained
only when there is a reasonable prospect — backed preferably by
historical evidence or, when appropriate, by a thoughtful analysis of
the future — that for every dollar retained by the corporation, at
least one dollar of market value will be created for owners. This
will happen only if the capital retained produces incremental
earnings equal to, or above, those generally available to
investors."* The test is to be applied prospectively before retention
and reviewed retrospectively after retention; the unit of failure is
the *incremental* dollar, not the average dollar.

The 1984 letter then offers the bondholder analogy as a way of
forcing the test into intuitive form. *"Imagine that an investor
owned a $100 perpetual bond that, at his option, could either pay
out $10 in cash annually, or alternatively, plow back the $10 at
8% forever."* The analogy makes the retention question sharp: the
bondholder who lets the issuer reinvest at 8% when 12% is available
elsewhere is destroying value at the rate of the spread, regardless
of how thoroughly the issuer's annual report explains the
reinvestment. The 1984 letter notes that in inflationary periods the
analogy gets darker still: an issuer reinvesting at 8% when the
investor needs 12% just to preserve real value is destroying value
*and* the destruction is hidden inside reported earnings.

The 1984 letter also identifies the *multi-divisional schizoid CEO*
failure mode the test is designed to catch. *"Many corporate
managers reason very much along these lines in determining whether
subsidiaries should distribute or retain earnings. At that level,
the thinking can be quite sophisticated. But distribution at the
parent company level is usually a different story. Here, managers
seldom put themselves in the shoes of their shareholder-owners."*
Subsidiary A is told to send cash up because the parent can deploy
it better than A can. The same parent then retains the same cash
without ever asking whether *its* shareholders could deploy it
better than the parent can. The asymmetry is invisible to the
manager and corrosive to the shareholder; the dollar-retention test
is the device that surfaces it.

The 1984 letter's most-quoted compressed formulation closes the
section: *"Restated as a Pro-Am Golf Tournament: regardless of how
inept the amateur player may be, the team's score will be quite
respectable because of the dominant skills of the professional."*
A great core business camouflages losses on incremental capital
deployed elsewhere. Look-through earnings, applied with the
dollar-retention test, separates the professional's contribution
from the amateur's so the latter can be addressed rather than
hidden. The 1984 letter ends the section with the line that has
become the most-cited summary of the entire framework: *"If
earnings have been unwisely retained, it is likely that managers,
too, have been unwisely retained."*

## The 1990–1992 formalisation

The 1990 letter is the first time the *name* "look-through earnings"
appears explicitly in the corpus. It introduces the formal definition
and the worked table: Berkshire's share of undistributed operating
earnings of major investees, less a hypothetical tax, added to
reported operating earnings. The 1990 calculation yields approximately
$590 million of look-through earnings for 1990, against $371 million
of reported operating earnings. The gap — roughly $220 million —
represents earnings that were being compounded inside the businesses
of Berkshire's investees (GEICO, Coca-Cola, Capital Cities/ABC,
Washington Post, Wells Fargo, and others), not visible in Berkshire's
GAAP statements, but accruing to Berkshire's account.

The 1990 letter ties the framework to the 15% annual intrinsic-value
target: *"I've told you that over time look-through earnings must
increase at about 15% annually if our intrinsic business value is
to grow at that rate."*

The 1991 letter extends the concept to individual investors in an
important passage that has no precedent in the earlier letters:
*"We also believe that investors can benefit by focusing on their own
look-through earnings. To calculate these, they should determine the
underlying earnings attributable to the shares they hold in their
portfolio and total these. The goal of each investor should be to
create a portfolio (in effect, a 'company') that will deliver him or
her the highest possible look-through earnings a decade or so from
now."* This is a direct instruction to apply the framework to
personal investment decisions, not merely as an analytical tool for
understanding Berkshire. The logic is the same: focus on the
underlying earning power, force yourself to think about long-term
business prospects, and ignore GAAP as the governing quantity.

The 1991 letter also identifies the right scoreboard: *"It's true,
of course, that, in the long run, the scoreboard for investment
decisions is market price. But prices will be determined by future
earnings. In investing, just as in baseball, to put runs on the
scoreboard one must watch the playing field, not the scoreboard."*

The 1992 letter provides the most concrete statement of the
quantitative target: look-through earnings of $604 million in 1992
must reach more than $1.8 billion by the year 2000 for Berkshire to
meet the 15% annual goal. This gives the framework a specific
horizon and a specific hurdle, making it actionable rather than
aspirational. The 1992 letter also adds a caveat against using
long-term thinking as cover for underperformance: *"Just as you
should be suspicious of managers who pump up short-term earnings by
accounting maneuvers, asset sales and the like, so also should you
be suspicious of those managers who fail to deliver for extended
periods and blame it on their long-term focus."* Long-term orientation
does not waive the requirement to deliver; look-through earnings is
the tool for evaluating delivery without being misled by accounting.

## The 1996–2002 figures

The 1996 letter reports look-through earnings of $1,522 million (up from
approximately $1,030 million in 1994, the prior figure given). The 1997
letter extends the table: look-through earnings of $1,930 million, composed
of $743 million from major investees' undistributed earnings (American
Express, Coca-Cola, Disney, Freddie Mac, Gillette, Washington Post, Wells
Fargo) less a $105 million hypothetical tax, added to $1,292 million of
reported operating earnings. The 1998 letter omits the look-through
calculation because the General Re acquisition closed on December 21 —
neither a historical nor a pro-forma figure would have been meaningful —
and the calculation resumes in 1999.

The 1999 letter resumes with look-through earnings of $1,926 million —
essentially unchanged from 1997, reflecting some weakness in investee
results during the intervening years. The 2000 letter reports $2,415 million,
composed of $740 million from major investees (American Express, Coca-Cola,
Freddie Mac, Gillette, M&T Bank, Washington Post, Wells Fargo) less a $104
million hypothetical tax, added to $1,779 million of reported operating
earnings. The jump from 1999 to 2000 reflects the eight major acquisitions
completed in 2000 which substantially expanded Berkshire's operating earnings
base. The 2001 and 2002 letters do not include look-through tables; during
years of major portfolio restructuring, a comparable prior-period figure would
be misleading.

## What this principle is not

It is not an argument that all retained earnings produce equivalent
value. The 1980 letter is explicit that retention is good only if
deployment is good. *"We have owned 100% of businesses whose reported
earnings were not worth close to 100 cents on the dollar to us even
though, in an accounting sense, we totally controlled their
disposition."* The principle says that ownership percentage is the
wrong filter; it does not say that retention is universally
beneficial. Where retention is bad, distribution is preferable —
either as dividends or as buybacks. The 1981 letter's Matthew 25
parable of the talents makes the symmetric point: high-return
businesses should retain, low-return businesses should distribute,
and the inflation environment perversely forces the inverse on weak
businesses (see [[Inflation Tax]]).

It is also not an argument that the look-through quantity is reliably
measurable. Buffett's 1981 estimate that look-through earnings on four
holdings (GEICO, General Foods, RJR, Washington Post) would total
"well over $35 million in 1982" is offered with a caveat that the
calculation is approximate. The principle is more important than the
arithmetic; the arithmetic only matters insofar as it forces the
investor to reason about underlying economics rather than reported
ones.

## Where this connects to Sleep

Sleep's [[Destination Analysis]] is structurally analogous.
Both authors are arguing that the right unit of analysis for a
business is its long-run economic engine — what cash it will throw
off, where, to whom — rather than its current GAAP earnings. Sleep
applies the framing to the businesses themselves; Buffett applies it
to the ownership stakes. The two are different applications of the
same disciplinary move: refuse to be governed by the accounting
quantity when the underlying economic quantity is what determines
return.
