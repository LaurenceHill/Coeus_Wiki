# Free Cash Flow Over Earnings

**Source:** [[Invent and Wander]] — 2004 shareholder letter (the entire
letter is devoted to this argument). [[The Bezos Letters]] (Anderson,
Chapter 13, "Measure What Matters") covers the same material in
secondary commentary.

## Summary

Bezos's stated headline financial metric for Amazon is free cash flow per
share, not earnings per share. The 2004 letter opens with the definition:
_"Our ultimate financial measure, and the one we most want to drive over
the long-term, is free cash flow per share."_ Free cash flow is defined in
the same letter as net cash provided by operating activities less purchases
of fixed assets, including capitalised internal-use software and website
development — the items that appear on the statements of cash flows.

The decision to report and manage against this metric rather than GAAP
earnings is explicit and deliberate. The 1997 letter states the trade-off
directly: _"When forced to choose between optimising the appearance of
our GAAP accounting and maximising the present value of future cash
flows, we'll take the cash flows."_ This preference is not incidental — it
follows directly from Amazon's [[Long-Term Thinking]] posture and its
willingness to invest at rates that depress reported earnings while
compounding underlying business value.

## Why GAAP earnings mislead in high-reinvestment businesses

GAAP earnings are an accounting construct that can diverge materially from
the cash a business generates. In Amazon's case, the divergence is
structural: heavy investment in fulfilment infrastructure, technology, and
new businesses shows up as current-period expense or depreciation under
GAAP, while the cash return from those investments arrives over subsequent
years. A management team optimising for near-term reported earnings would
invest less aggressively than the underlying economics justify. A management
team optimising for free cash flow per share can invest with the long run in
mind, accepting near-term GAAP losses as the cost of building a larger
future cash engine.

The more pointed version of the same argument: earnings can be managed
in ways that free cash flow cannot. Revenue recognition timing,
depreciation schedules, and accounting for capitalised versus expensed
costs all create discretion for management to improve the reported earnings
line without improving the underlying business. Free cash flow is harder to
flatter, because it requires the business to actually collect cash — from
customers, faster than it pays it out — rather than record a favourable
accounting entry.

Bezos's related instruction to managers: focus on metrics that point toward
genuine customer outcomes and economic substance, not toward metrics
that can be satisfied by process compliance or accounting choice. This is
the financial-reporting application of the broader principle that processes
and metrics must serve the underlying goal rather than displacing it —
a theme that also appears in [[Day 1]]'s sceptical view of proxies and in
[[Customer Obsession]]'s warning against proxy obsession.

## The anecdote-and-data discipline

The 2004 letter and Anderson's Chapter 13 together surface a second
measurement principle that operates alongside the free-cash-flow preference:
when data and anecdote conflict, the anecdote usually contains signal about
a flaw in the measurement, not an exception to the data.

Bezos's formulation, from a 2018 forum at SMU: _"What I have noticed is
that when the anecdotes and the data disagree, the anecdotes are usually
right. There's something wrong with the way you're measuring it."_ This
is an unusually specific claim. It inverts the default assumption of
data-first management: rather than dismissing the anecdote when it
conflicts with the metric, Bezos uses the conflict as a signal that the
metric may be measuring the wrong thing or measuring the right thing in the
wrong way.

The two principles connect: choosing free cash flow over GAAP earnings
is itself an instance of questioning the default metric because it conflicts
with better evidence about what actually matters. The same reasoning that
led Bezos to reject GAAP earnings as the headline metric should, by his
own logic, lead any manager to interrogate their own chosen metrics when
they stop pointing toward genuine value creation.

## The relationship to working backward

[[Working Backward]] is structurally dependent on the free-cash-flow
preference. Bezos's method of starting from the desired customer
experience and working backward to the required capabilities almost always
implies a period of investment ahead of revenue. The Kindle required
hardware engineering Amazon didn't have. AWS required infrastructure
investment that preceded commercial demand. Prime required subsidised
shipping that depressed per-order economics for years before the flywheel
effects arrived.

In each case, the decision to make the investment looks clearly negative
under a short-term GAAP earnings lens and clearly positive under a
long-term free-cash-flow lens. A CEO accountable to quarterly earnings
per share would have structural difficulty authorising any of these bets.
A CEO accountable to long-run free cash flow per share can authorise them
and explain clearly why. This is the organisational function of the metric
choice: it aligns the headline financial objective with the investment posture
that the working-backward and [[Customer Obsession]] principles require.

## Parallel in Buffett — and a precision note

Buffett's [[Look-Through Earnings]] is one analogous move: GAAP
accounting treats retained earnings of minority-owned businesses as if they
produce no income for Berkshire, so Buffett corrects by reasoning about
underlying earnings regardless of accounting treatment. Both he and Bezos
are refusing to be governed by the accounting quantity when the underlying
economic quantity is what determines return.

Buffett's [[Owner Earnings]] concept (1986 letter Appendix) is the more
directly comparable formulation. Owner earnings adjusts GAAP earnings by
adding back non-cash charges and subtracting maintenance capital
expenditure — the amount the business must reinvest just to maintain its
competitive position and unit volume. The discipline is explicitly the same
as Bezos's: reject GAAP, focus on cash economics.

The difference is precision on one specific question: Buffett separates
total capital expenditure into maintenance capex and growth capex, and
argues that only maintenance capex belongs in the owner earnings
deduction. Bezos's FCF metric — operating cash flow less total purchases
of fixed assets — makes no such distinction. In a high-reinvestment period
at Amazon, total capex includes enormous amounts of growth investment in
fulfilment capacity, AWS infrastructure, and logistics that are not
maintenance spending at all; they are discretionary bets on future scale. A
strict owner earnings calculation for Amazon during its high-growth years
would show _higher_ owner earnings than Bezos's FCF number, because the
maintenance capex required to hold the current competitive position was a
fraction of total capex.

Neither metric is wrong. They answer different questions. Owner earnings
asks: what does this business generate if it maintains its current position
without growing? FCF per share asks: what cash does the business produce
after all capital decisions, growth and maintenance together? Both questions
are useful; the investor should know which one is being answered.

The Anderson on Bezos vs Bezos on Bezos page ([[Anderson on Bezos vs Bezos on Bezos]]) notes that the free-cash-flow chapter is one place where
Anderson's secondary account is relatively faithful to the primary.

## What this is not

It is not an argument that free cash flow is the only relevant metric or that
GAAP earnings are always misleading. For a mature business with limited
reinvestment opportunities and stable accounting, earnings and free cash
flow converge. Bezos's argument applies most sharply to businesses in
heavy reinvestment mode, where the gap between current GAAP earnings
and future cash-generating capacity is widest and most consequential.

It is also not permission to ignore profitability. The 1997 letter states
that Amazon will _"balance our focus on growth with emphasis on long-term
profitability and capital management."_ The metric choice is about
choosing the right time horizon and the right accounting lens — not about
excusing indefinite losses. Amazon's cost-consciousness culture, referenced
across multiple letters as a hard constraint, is the complement: FCF
discipline requires both the investment and the operational frugality that
makes the investment pay off.
