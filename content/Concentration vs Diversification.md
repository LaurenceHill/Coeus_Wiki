# Concentration vs Diversification

**The tension:** Academic finance argues that diversification eliminates
idiosyncratic risk at no cost to expected returns, making it the
rational default for almost all investors. Buffett and Sleep both run
highly concentrated portfolios and argue that for the investor with
genuine analytical capacity, concentration produces superior long-run
results. The disagreement is not about preferences; it is about the
premise underlying the academic case.

## The academic position

Modern Portfolio Theory (Markowitz, 1952 onward) establishes that
a portfolio's expected return is determined by its asset weights and
expected individual returns, but its variance is determined by the
covariance structure across holdings. Because idiosyncratic risk is
diversifiable — it washes out when enough uncorrelated positions are
held — MPT argues that rational investors should hold the market
portfolio, supplemented or reduced by the risk-free asset to set the
desired volatility level. Holding a concentrated portfolio means
accepting idiosyncratic variance for which the market provides no
additional expected return.

The efficient market hypothesis strengthens the case: if prices
fully reflect available information, no investor can reliably identify
mispriced securities. Concentration is then not merely inefficient
on risk grounds; it is gambling, because the investor is taking
idiosyncratic risk on a premise (mispricing) that the EMH rejects.

## Buffett's position

Buffett's clearest statement of the concentration case is in the 1978
Berkshire letter and is developed further through 1984. The 1984
letter's "harem of forty women" image, credited to Billy Rose, is the
compressed argument: spreading analytical effort across forty
positions produces shallow knowledge of all forty, while concentrating
on five or six produces deep knowledge of those few. Depth of
understanding is the investor's only reliable edge; diversification
dilutes the edge along with the risk.

The 1991 letter adds John Maynard Keynes as a witness for the same
position. Keynes wrote to a business associate, F. C. Scott, on
August 15, 1934: *"As time goes on, I get more and more convinced
that the right method in investment is to put fairly large sums into
enterprises which one thinks one knows something about and in the
management of which one thoroughly believes. It is a mistake to think
that one limits one's risk by spreading too much between enterprises
about which one knows little and has no reason for special
confidence... One's knowledge and experience are definitely limited
and there are seldom more than two or three enterprises at any given
time in which I personally feel myself entitled to put full
confidence."* The argument is structurally identical to Buffett's:
the constraint on concentration is analytical capacity, and spreading
beyond what that capacity covers does not produce safety, it produces
an illusion of safety.

Buffett's framing of diversification as a "confession of ignorance"
is deliberately provocative but precise: for the investor who genuinely
cannot identify which of several securities is most attractive,
diversification is the correct answer. For the investor who has done
the analytical work and reached a reliable conclusion, diluting that
conclusion with positions of lower conviction is a mistake. The policy
is contingent on the quality of the underlying analysis.

The financial strength prerequisite (1984 letter, reinforced in the
wiki's [[Concentration]] page) adds a structural condition: concentration
is only appropriate for the investor whose financial position can
withstand being wrong. Buffett's 47% ownership of Berkshire insulates
him from the career risk that forces most institutional managers toward
diversification regardless of conviction. The academic case for
diversification is partly correct for professionals who cannot afford
to be wrong for twelve consecutive months; it is less compelling for
the owner-operator who faces no redemption risk.

## Sleep's position

Sleep arrives at the same operational conclusion — a portfolio of
seven to ten positions in the Nomad Partnership's later years, and
effectively five to seven by the time of closure — by a somewhat
different route. His argument runs primarily through [[Reinvestment Risk]] and the [[Scale Economics Shared]] model.

The Scale Economics Shared filter is very narrow. Sleep argues that
there are only a small number of businesses in any given market that
genuinely share their scale advantages with customers in a self-
reinforcing way. If you have found several of them, diversifying
away from them into lower-quality alternatives is clearly wrong.
The concentrated portfolio is not a choice against diversification;
it is the correct response to a narrow opportunity set.

Sleep also argues in the 2013 interim letter that the investee
businesses were "youthful" — on average twelve to fifteen years old
at initial purchase — with very long runways ahead of them. For a
business with a long runway and a durable structural advantage, the
main investment risk is not price volatility (diversifiable) but the
risk of exiting too early and facing the [[Reinvestment Risk]] of
finding something equivalent. Concentration and long duration together
are the response to this risk profile.

## Fisher's position

Fisher is the earliest and most explicit source in this wiki on the
concentration question. His formulation in the [[Five Don'ts and Five More Don'ts]] is direct: for individuals, any holding of over twenty
different stocks is a sign of financial incompetence; ten to twelve is
usually the right number.

The reasoning runs through two channels. First, genuine understanding
of a company — the kind produced by the [[Scuttlebutt]] method and
sustained monitoring against the [[Fifteen Points]] — requires sustained
analytical attention. An investor cannot maintain genuine understanding of
forty or fifty positions. What she calls diversification is actually
ignorance distributed across many positions, and the benefit of her
analytical skill disappears into the noise. Second, truly outstanding
companies are scarce. Finding five to ten that genuinely qualify is
difficult work; adding fifteen mediocre ones to achieve a round-number of
diversification is simply diluting the good with the mediocre.

Fisher's A/B/C taxonomy gives this argument its operational form. Type A
companies are outstanding in both management quality and business
characteristics; these are the companies worth holding in size and
understanding thoroughly. Type B companies are adequate; they can be held
but warrant smaller positions and more frequent re-evaluation. Type C
companies are speculative — acceptable as small positions when the upside
is asymmetric, but not as a portfolio's foundation. Fisher's portfolio is
intentionally concentrated in Type A companies. Diversifying into Type B
and C positions does not reduce risk in any meaningful sense; it introduces
the risk of inadequate analytical coverage and the certain cost of inferior
expected returns.

Fisher also addresses the relationship between diversification and research
quality directly: if the [[Scuttlebutt]] method requires substantive
conversations with customers, competitors, and former employees of each
company held, the investor who holds thirty positions simply cannot do
this work. The constraint on concentration is not a philosophical
preference but a practical limit on the quality of analysis that is possible
per position. Concentration and analytical quality reinforce each other.

## Where the conflict is real

The genuine disagreement is empirical: can investors reliably identify
mispriced securities, or can they reliably identify a small number of
businesses with structural advantages that will compound over long
periods? The EMH says no to both. Buffett and Sleep say yes, with
different mechanisms (mispricing for Buffett's early career, structural
advantage for the Berkshire period; structural advantage throughout for
Sleep).

The evidence is equivocal. Concentrated active managers, as a group,
do not outperform diversified index funds — but the distribution is
wide. The argument from Buffett and Sleep is not that all concentrated
investors outperform but that the few who have a genuine edge should
concentrate, because diversification dilutes whatever edge they have.
This is a claim about a specific subset of investors, not about active
management in general.

## Where the conflict dissolves

Both the academic position and the Buffett-Sleep position agree that
investors without a genuine analytical edge should not concentrate.
The disagreement is about whether such an edge can exist and be
sustained. Neither side disputes the mathematical logic of MPT given
its premises; the dispute is about whether the premises apply to
specific investors.

Both sides also agree that concentration without genuine conviction
is dangerous — what Buffett calls "concentration without the filter."
Picking five securities at random and holding them in equal weight is
not what Buffett or Sleep advocate. The policy is concentration
conditioned on deep analysis and high conviction, not concentration
as a style.

## The failure mode on each side

Excessive diversification produces index-like returns at active-
management fees, with the additional cost of analytical effort
spread so thin it produces no useful signal. This is the dominant
outcome for the active management industry.

Excessive concentration, applied without genuine analytical capacity
or without the financial cushion to absorb being wrong, produces
catastrophic losses from which recovery is slow or impossible. The
financial-strength prerequisite Buffett states in the 1984 letter
is not decoration; it is load-bearing.
