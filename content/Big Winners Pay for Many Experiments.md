# Big Winners Pay for Many Experiments

**Source:** [[Invent and Wander]] — 2015 shareholder letter,
"Big Winners Pay for Many Experiments." This is the primary-source
version of what Anderson rebrands as [[Successful Failure]].

## Summary

Bezos's explicit asymmetric-payoff argument: in business, returns
are long-tailed, which means a small number of large wins finance
a large number of failed experiments. The math, in Bezos's own
formulation: *given a 10% chance of a 100× payoff, take that bet
every time, even though you'll be wrong nine times out of ten.*

The 2015 letter contains the canonical baseball metaphor. In
baseball, the maximum payoff per swing is four runs (a grand
slam). In business, every once in a while a single decision can
produce 1,000 runs. The truncated outcome distribution in
baseball makes the swing-for-the-fences strategy moderately
useful; the long-tailed distribution in business makes it
essential.

## What this fixes about the Anderson framing

Anderson's [[Successful Failure]] page in this wiki notes that
his framing stretches the underlying idea. Bezos's actual frame
is sharper in three ways:

- **The math is explicit.** Bezos states the asymmetric-payoff
  case in concrete probabilistic terms (10% × 100×). Anderson
  reframes it as a cultural disposition toward failure
  tolerance, which loses the math.
- **The claim is about portfolio behaviour, not individual
  failure.** Bezos isn't celebrating individual failures. He's
  describing the *expected-value math of running a portfolio
  of asymmetric bets*. The emotional reframe of "successful
  failure" is Anderson's; the underlying claim is statistical.
- **The big winners are the point.** Bezos names them — AWS,
  Marketplace, Prime — as the three pillars whose returns
  finance everything else. Anderson's framing emphasises the
  failures (Fire Phone) more than the wins, which inverts the
  emphasis.

The corrected reading: this isn't a principle about being
comfortable with failure. It's a principle about being willing
to take bets that are *individually likely to fail* because the
*portfolio's expected value* is dominated by the rare large
win.

## Why most companies don't do this

Bezos's diagnosis: "Most large organizations embrace the idea of
invention but are not willing to suffer the string of failed
experiments necessary to get there."

Three structural reasons:

- **Failure is visible; success is delayed.** A failed
  experiment shows up immediately as a write-off. The win that
  would have justified the failures shows up years later, if at
  all. Quarterly reporting cycles amplify the asymmetry.
- **Career incentives.** The executive who runs the failed Fire
  Phone is identifiable; the executive who runs the
  not-yet-conceived AWS-equivalent doesn't exist yet. Career
  preservation pushes toward incremental certain wins over
  swing-for-the-fences attempts.
- **Cost-of-capital pressure.** Companies with weaker cash
  generation can't afford the failed experiments without
  threatening the core business. The math only works if the
  core business is strong enough to absorb the losing bets.

## What this requires to operate

Three preconditions Bezos doesn't always make explicit but the
reader should:

1. **Strong existing cash flow.** The 10%-of-100× math fails if
   the cost of the failures bankrupts the firm before the
   winning bet arrives.
2. **Bounded individual failures.** The bets need to be
   structured as [[Two-Way Doors]] — reversible enough that a
   single failed bet doesn't create catastrophic damage.
3. **Patient capital.** The firm needs investors who tolerate
   the multi-year period during which only the failures are
   visible. Without this, the strategy is forced into
   reversal mid-cycle.

Sleep's [[Patience as Competitive Advantage]] is the
investor-side complement: an investor with patient capital can
hold companies running this strategy through the period when
the failures are visible and the wins haven't arrived yet.
Without that patience, the company gets pressured into stopping
the strategy precisely when the long-tail wins would have
arrived.

## Where this connects to Sleep

Sleep doesn't use this exact framing for his own portfolio
decisions, but he applies essentially the same logic when
holding companies that run this strategy. Sleep's willingness to
hold Amazon through years of negative-margin reinvestment was
predicated on understanding that the *portfolio of bets*
internal to Amazon had the asymmetric-payoff structure Bezos was
describing. Sleep was effectively investing *in Bezos's
willingness to do this*, which is a different thesis from
investing in Amazon's current operating metrics.

## Where this contradicts Fisher

This is one of the points where Fisher and Bezos genuinely
diverge — not in the sense of disagreement, but in the sense of
operating at different points in the value chain.

Fisher's first Don't is *don't buy into promotional companies*
— companies running asymmetric bets without a profitable core
to absorb the failures. Bezos is running asymmetric bets *from
within* a profitable core. The two are compatible: Fisher would
not have told an investor to buy Amazon in 1999 (when the core
wasn't yet profitable enough to absorb the experiments), but
might have approved by 2010 (when AWS had become a third
visible profit pillar).

The contradiction page [[Bet on Big Ideas vs Don't Buy Promotional Companies]] discusses this in more detail.

## What this isn't

It isn't permission to make speculative bets without a thesis.
The "10% chance" in Bezos's formulation requires honest
estimation. A bet that the analyst *says* has a 10% chance of
working but actually has a 1% chance is not the same kind of
bet. The math depends on the probabilities being roughly
right.

It also isn't permission to bet the firm. Bezos is explicit
about the three pillars (AWS, Marketplace, Prime) being the
*reason* the strategy worked at scale. Without a profitable
core, the failed experiments accumulate into ruin.
