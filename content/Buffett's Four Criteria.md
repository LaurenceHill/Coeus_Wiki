# Buffett's Four Criteria

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1977 letter, "Insurance
Investments" section.

## Summary

The first canonical statement of Buffett's investment-selection framework.
The exact passage:

> We select our marketable equity securities in much the same way we
> would evaluate a business for acquisition in its entirety. We want the
> business to be (1) one that we can understand, (2) with favorable
> long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and
> (4) available at a very attractive price.

The framework appears, with minor variations, across most subsequent
letters and is the spine of Buffett's investment philosophy. The 1977
formulation is the foundational version.

## Why it matters

The framework's power is in what it excludes more than what it demands.
Each criterion eliminates a class of investments that other frameworks —
quantitative screens, momentum, sector rotation — would have admitted.
The four together compress decades of investing experience into a list
short enough to remember and strict enough to act as a real filter.

The framing also collapses the supposed distinction between buying stock
and buying a business. The marketable-equity decision is treated as a
fractional acquisition; the same criteria apply, just at smaller scale.
The implication runs deep — a stock you would not want to own the
entirety of is, by this test, a stock you should not own a fraction of.
The framework is therefore as much a *posture* toward equity ownership as
a checklist for selecting it.

## What each criterion does

**Understandable.** A constraint on the investor, not the business. The
test is not whether the business is simple in absolute terms; it is
whether the investor can predict its economics over the relevant holding
period. The criterion eliminates businesses where the investor would have
to rely on someone else's judgement about the core economics. The
principle becomes more explicit in later Buffett writing as the "circle
of competence" idea — a phrase not yet present in 1977 — but the core
claim is here: depth of understanding is asymmetric, and the investor
should operate where their depth is genuine.

**Favourable long-term prospects.** Industry choice and durability. The
1977 letter pairs this criterion with the textile-vs-insurance contrast:
*"the importance of being in businesses where tailwinds prevail rather
than headwinds."* See [[Tailwinds vs Headwinds]] for the standalone
treatment. The criterion is forward-looking — past results are evidence
about prospects but not the criterion itself. A business with strong
past results and deteriorating prospects fails this criterion even when
it passes the others.

**Honest and competent management.** The people-quality criterion.
Notable that Buffett pairs the two — honesty *and* competence — as a
joint requirement. Buffett's pairing implies neither alone is sufficient:
honest but incompetent management cannot deliver; competent but dishonest
management can deliver, but the value is captured by the operator rather
than the owner. The 1977 letter's praise of Phil Liesche at National
Indemnity, Gene Abegg at Illinois National Bank, and Chuck Huggins at
See's illustrates how Buffett evaluates this criterion in concrete cases —
naming specific operating decisions and outcomes rather than CEO
biographies. This criterion is the Buffett analogue to Fisher's
[[Fifteen Points]] point on management integrity, and to
Bezos's [[Missionaries vs Mercenaries]] heuristic.

**Available at a very attractive price.** The valuation criterion. The
word *very* matters — Buffett does not say "fair price" or "reasonable
price." The framework treats price as a hurdle, not a fine adjustment. A
business satisfying the first three criteria but only modestly priced
does not meet the test. The criterion also implies a willingness to wait;
businesses satisfying criteria 1–3 are not usually available at criterion-4
prices. The 1977 letter's passage on welcoming lower market prices for
stocks already owned — *"if their business experience continues to satisfy
us, we welcome lower market prices of stocks we own as an opportunity to
acquire even more of a good thing at a better price"* — is the same idea
seen from the other direction.

The 1981 letter sharpens the same point in the language of acquisition
discipline. Buffett describes coming "quite close to a major purchase
involving both a business and a manager we liked very much" — i.e., a
candidate that passed criteria 1–3 — but walking away because the price
demanded "would have left our owners worse off than before the purchase.
The empire would have been larger, but the citizenry would have been
poorer." This is criterion 4 acting as veto over the other three. See
[[Acquisition Discipline]] for the broader framing in the 1981 letter.

## How criterion 4 functions in practice (1978 reinforcement)

The 1978 letter adds an operational observation that the 1977 framing
left implicit. Buffett, discussing a year in which Berkshire bought
heavily into common stocks, notes that the four-criteria filter does
not change but that criterion 4 is the binding constraint most of the
time. Years pass in which businesses meeting criteria 1–3 are abundant
but priced out of reach; in such years the discipline is to do nothing.
The 1978 letter calls this out because 1978 was the opposite — a year
in which the price criterion was unusually satisfied, so the volume
of equity buying rose. The behavioural lesson is not that the criteria
change in different markets but that *which* criterion is binding
changes; the investor's job is to recognise which one and act
accordingly. Doing nothing because criterion 4 fails is itself an
application of the framework, not a deviation from it.

## The criteria as outward-facing filter (1982 onward)

The 1982 letter takes the four-criteria framework and turns it
outward. Where the 1977 framing was inward — a private filter
governing Berkshire's selection — the 1982 letter publishes a
matching set of conditions as a printed advertisement to potential
acquisition counterparties. The list (refined 1985, 1986) is six
items rather than four, but the structural correspondence is exact.
The 1982 ad's *demonstrated consistent earning power* is criterion
2 (favourable long-term prospects) restated as a measurable test;
*future projections are of little interest to us, nor are
'turn-around' situations* is the same criterion's rejection of the
managerial-kiss myth. *Management in place* is criterion 3 in
acquirer-side form: Berkshire admits it cannot supply management
and therefore requires honest and competent management to be
already there. *Simple businesses — if there's lots of technology,
we won't understand it* is criterion 1 written in the negative.
*An offering price* is criterion 4 made operationally enforceable —
no exploratory conversations without a price, because the
discipline of criterion 4 cannot be exercised against an unstated
counterfactual.

What the 1982 ad shows is that the four criteria are not just a
selection filter for marketable equities but a generalised posture
toward business ownership. The same questions that govern stock
selection govern wholly-owned acquisition. The 1985 letter's
Scott & Fetzer acquisition is the worked example of the framework
running outward: the company's name appeared in a newspaper after
its planned ESOP fell through, the criteria were satisfied,
Berkshire moved from first letter to signed contract within
fourteen days. The unified framework permits speed because the
filter has been pre-committed; deals that pass it deserve action,
deals that fail it do not deserve discussion. See [[Acquisition Discipline]] for the broader treatment.

## A property the criteria share

All four criteria can be assessed without reference to current market
prices except the fourth. This is the working-backward move applied to
investing: start with the business, work backward to whether the market
price makes the investment attractive. A working-forwards investor — one
who starts with the market price and looks for businesses that justify
it — inverts the order and routinely buys worse businesses for what
looks like a good price.

The structural similarity to Bezos's [[Working Backward]] is real. Both
authors are arguing for ordering decisions so the inputs control the
conclusion, rather than letting whatever information is most readily
available drive the analysis. In Buffett's case the readily-available
information is the market price; the discipline is to refuse to start
there. In Bezos's case the readily-available information is what the
team can already build; the discipline is to refuse to start there
either.

## What this framework isn't

It isn't a screening tool. None of the four criteria are quantifiable in
a way that lets a screen produce a ranked list. Each requires judgement;
the four together require sustained judgement over years. The criteria
filter out far more than they admit — most investments fail at least one —
but they do not substitute for the work of evaluating each remaining
candidate.

It also isn't a complete framework. The 1977 letter is silent on several
questions a full investment process would address: how big a position to
take, when to sell, how to think about portfolio concentration, how to
handle businesses that satisfied the criteria when bought but no longer
satisfy them later. Later letters address each of these. The four
criteria are the entry filter; they are not the operating manual for
the full investment process.

## Where this connects to Fisher

Fisher's [[Fifteen Points]] is the closest analogue — a
checklist that filters before evaluation. Fisher's list is longer and
more operationally specific (point 1 asks about products with revenue
runway; point 9 about labour relations); Buffett's is shorter and more
abstract. The two are compatible: a Fisher fifteen-point analysis would
satisfy Buffett's first three criteria almost by construction, leaving
only the price test. A Buffett-passing investment would typically score
well on most of Fisher's points, though Fisher's emphasis on management
depth and labour relations would catch edge cases the compressed Buffett
list might miss.

## Where this connects to Sleep

Sleep does not restate a four-criteria framework, but his investment
process across the [[Nomad Partnership Letters]] maps cleanly
onto these four. Sleep's [[Destination Analysis]] is criterion
2 (favourable long-term prospects) operationalised through a specific
question: *where will the business be in twenty years?* Sleep's emphasis
on missionary management is criterion 3.
[[Patience as Competitive Advantage]] is what makes criterion
4 reachable — the willingness to wait until the price is *very*
attractive, not merely fair.
