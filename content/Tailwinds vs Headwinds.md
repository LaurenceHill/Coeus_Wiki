# Tailwinds vs Headwinds

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1977 letter, "Insurance
Underwriting" section; 1991 letter, "A Change in Media Economics and
Some Valuation Math" section; 2006 letter, newspaper industry analysis.

## Summary

Buffett's compressed lesson on the primacy of industry choice over
operator skill. The exact line:

> One of the lessons your management has learned — and, unfortunately,
> sometimes re-learned — is the importance of being in businesses where
> tailwinds prevail rather than headwinds.

The lesson is illustrated by the textile-vs-insurance contrast in the
same letter. The textile business consumed substantial managerial effort
and produced modest, intermittent results because the industry economics
ran against the operator. The insurance business, despite some operating
mistakes, produced a satisfactory overall outcome because the economics
ran with the operator.

## Why it matters

The argument is almost a counterweight to the rest of the
investment-selection framework. Criterion 3 of [[Buffett's Four Criteria]]
emphasises management quality, but the headwinds-vs-tailwinds lesson is
that even excellent management cannot make a bad industry profitable in
the long run. Buffett's exact framing: _"In a sense, this is the opposite
case from our textile business where even very good management probably
can average only modest results."_

This is uncomfortable for the standard "we hire great people and they
figure it out" management theory. Buffett's empirical claim is that
great people in industries with the wrong economics produce modest
results, while ordinary people in industries with the right economics
often produce satisfactory ones. The implication is that operator
selection matters less than industry selection, inverted from common
belief.

## What counts as a tailwind

The 1977 letter does not define the term abstractly, but the
insurance-side details suggest at least three components.

The first is _pricing power_ — the ability to raise prices to match cost
increases without losing volume. The 1977 letter notes that insurance
costs were rising at roughly 1% per month and underwriting profits
required matching rate increases. Industries where rate increases are
accepted have tailwinds; industries where they are resisted have
headwinds.

The second is _capital efficiency_. Insurance generates float — premiums
collected before claims paid — which can be invested. Textiles consume
capital to maintain capacity. The first compounds the operator's
results; the second drags on them.

The third is _cyclical asymmetry_. The 1977 letter notes that insurance
underwriting was profitable in 1977 because of rate increases following
the 1974–75 crisis, and that the cycle would turn. A tailwind business
does not have to be permanently favourable; it has to be one where the
bad cycles end and the good ones recur. A pure headwind business is one
where the bad conditions are structural rather than cyclical.

## What counts as a headwind

The 1977 textile passage gives the structural diagnosis. Three features
of the textile business made it a headwind industry, and they
generalise.

Commodity output that competitors can produce identically. The product
can be substituted at the margin by anyone, so pricing power compresses
to whoever has the lowest cost. The textile mills' competitors could
match their output cost-for-cost.

High labour intensity with limited automation runway. The 1977 letter
notes that the New Bedford and Manchester mills' labour forces had
"high average age possessing relatively non-transferable skills" — a
humane reason for staying in the business, but also a structural reason
the operation could not be restructured into something else.

Capital expenditure required to maintain rather than expand the
operation. Money put in does not compound; it preserves a position that
the industry economics are eroding. Textiles required ongoing
investment; insurance generated investable surplus.

## How to use this as an investor

The diagnostic question to ask before evaluating management is whether
the industry's economics are running with or against the operator. If
with, modest management can produce acceptable results and good
management can produce excellent ones. If against, even excellent
management is fighting the current.

The implication for [[Buffett's Four Criteria]] is that criterion 2
(favourable long-term prospects) does most of the heavy lifting.
Criterion 3 (honest and competent management) is necessary but not
sufficient — it does not rescue a headwind industry. An investor who
emphasises management quality without first checking industry economics
is solving the easier problem and skipping the harder one.

## How later letters reinforce the same lesson

The 1978–1981 letters return to this idea repeatedly, each time with
a sharper formulation.

The 1978 letter gives the textbook formulation of the headwind
mechanism: _"as long as excess productive capacity exists, prices tend
to reflect direct operating costs rather than capital employed."_ This
is the structural reason a commodity-output, capital-intensive industry
cannot earn its cost of capital across a full cycle. Owners are paying
for capacity that the market refuses to price.

The 1979 letter inverts the lesson onto the buy side. Discussing the
purchase of Waumbec Mills — bought at less than working capital, and
still a mistake — Buffett concludes: _"turnarounds seldom turn, and the
same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business
purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a
bargain price."_ The "good business at a fair price" formulation
becomes one of Buffett's recurring slogans. The same letter notes that
some businesses — a network TV station is the example — make it
_"virtually impossible to avoid earning extraordinary returns on
tangible capital employed,"_ and concludes: _"despite a fancy price
tag, the 'easy' business may be the better route to go."_ The
asymmetry is structural: the easy business prices in the structural
advantage but still produces better long-term results than the hard
business priced at a discount.

The 1980 letter compresses the lesson to its most-cited form: _"when a
management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a
reputation for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the
business that remains intact."_ The same letter introduces the
"localized excisable cancer" distinction: GEICO and American Express,
both bought during periods of crisis, were not turnarounds but
extraordinary franchises with a discrete fixable problem. A true
turnaround is an attempt to change the underlying economics of the
business; that almost never succeeds. A franchise rescue is an
attempt to remove an obstacle to economics that already work; that
sometimes does. The diagnostic is whether the headwind being faced
is the industry's underlying economics or a contingent operational
crisis sitting on top of healthy economics.

The 1981 letter formalises the diagnostic into Buffett's two
categories of acquirers worth admiring (see [[Acquisition Discipline]]).
Category 1 is acquirers who have specialised in tailwind businesses —
ones with pricing power and capital efficiency. Category 2 is the
rare manager who can actually convert toads into princes. Buffett
explicitly says he does not qualify for Category 2. The implication
for the 1977 lesson is that the asymmetry has not narrowed; it has
widened, because the manager-as-rescuer story has been tested against
more data and continues to fail.

The 1985 letter is the longest and most explicit reinforcement, and
the one that makes the lesson canonical. After eleven years of
attempting to make Berkshire's textile operation work, Buffett
shuts it down and writes a multi-page post-mortem. Three passages
from that section have entered the wider Buffett canon.

The most-quoted formulation generalises the 1980 line into a
metaphor that has stuck:

> A good managerial record (measured by economic returns) is far
> more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of
> how effectively you row. … Should you find yourself in a
> chronically-leaking boat, energy devoted to changing vessels is
> likely to be more productive than energy devoted to patching
> leaks.

The same passage gives the inverted Samuel Johnson aphorism — _"a
horse that can count to ten is a remarkable horse, not a
remarkable mathematician"_ — and applies it: _"a textile company
that allocates capital brilliantly within its industry is a
remarkable textile company, not a remarkable business."_ The
distinction matters because it cuts off a defence often heard from
operators in declining industries — that _given the industry,_ the
operator is doing well. Buffett's response is that doing well
within a leaking boat does not change the rate at which the boat
is taking on water.

The 1985 letter also includes the most-detailed worked example in
the corpus: _Burlington Industries._ In 1964 Burlington had sales
of $$1.2 billion against Berkshire's $$50 million; it was the
industry leader; its stock sold at $$60 to Berkshire's $$13.
Burlington committed to staying in textiles and over 1964–1985
deployed roughly $$3 billion of capital expenditure (more than
$$200 per share on the original $$60 stock). On a 2-for-1 split-
adjusted basis its stock by 1985 stood at $$34, almost unchanged
in nominal terms — and the CPI had more than tripled, leaving
each share commanding about one-third of the purchasing power it
had carried in 1964. The capital was rationally deployed within
the industry; the industry was the wrong industry; the
shareholders were ruined in real terms while the company looked,
year by year, like a competently run firm.

The 1985 letter also identifies the _collective-action_ version
of the lesson, distinct from the simple operator-skill version.
Each individual textile company's capital expenditure decisions
appeared rational in isolation: a new loom that reduced unit
costs would, on a return-on-investment test, look like a winner.
But the same calculation was being run by every other company.
Once enough companies invested, the cost reductions became
industry-wide and were competed away in lower prices. Buffett's
image: _"each company's capital investment decision appeared
cost-effective and rational; viewed collectively, the decisions
neutralized each other and were irrational — just as happens
when each person watching a parade decides he can see a little
better if he stands on tiptoes."_ Headwind industries are
characterised by exactly this collective-action trap. Standing
on tiptoes does not let anyone see the parade better; it just
makes everyone's calves sore.

The 1985 textile-machinery auction is the bookend. Equipment
that originally cost about $$13 million and had a current book
value of $$866,000 — and that could have been replaced new for
$$30–50 million — sold at auction for gross proceeds of $$163,122,
a sum less than the cost of removal. Looms bought for $$5,000 in
1981 found no takers at $$50 and were finally sold for scrap at
$$26 each. _"The economic goodwill attributable to two paper
routes in Buffalo — or a single See's candy store — considerably
exceeds the proceeds we received from this massive collection of
tangible assets."_ The line ties the headwinds-vs-tailwinds
lesson directly to [[Economic Goodwill]]: a tailwind business
generates intangible value that compounds; a headwind business
destroys tangible value while looking solvent on the books.

## The 1991 case study: the media franchise becoming a business

The 1991 letter provides the most detailed real-time case study in
the corpus of a tailwind becoming a headwind. Newspapers, television
stations, and magazines had, from roughly the postwar period through
the mid-1980s, satisfied three conditions that made them structural
tailwinds: their product was desired; consumers perceived no close
substitute; and pricing was unregulated. The result was the ability
to raise advertising and subscription prices aggressively, earning
high returns on capital with minimal additional investment.

By 1991 two of these conditions were eroding. Consumer choices for
information and entertainment had multiplied — cable television,
home video, direct mail — while the pool of consumer attention had
not grown. _"500 million American eyeballs and a 24-hour day are all
that's available. The result is that competition has intensified,
markets have fragmented, and the media industry has lost some —
though far from all — of its franchise strength."_

The valuation consequence of the transition is the 1991 letter's most
precise contribution to the tailwind/headwind framework. Buffett
walks through the arithmetic explicitly:

A newspaper or TV station expected to grow earnings perpetually at 6%
annually, without requiring additional capital to do so, discounted
at 10% was worth approximately 25 times current after-tax earnings.
This is the franchise-as-perpetual-annuity valuation. The same
business, once the growth assumption is removed and earnings are
expected to "bob around" a central figure with no structural growth,
is worth approximately 10 times the same earnings. _"Thus a seemingly
modest shift in assumptions reduces the property's valuation to \[40%
of what it was]."_

This arithmetic makes the structural point in its sharpest form:
a headwind business and a tailwind business with identical current
earnings should not carry identical multiples. The multiple is an
encoding of the expectation about character — franchise or commodity —
and when that expectation changes, the multiple collapses even before
earnings do. Investors who hold the prior multiple into the transition
are not being conservative; they are being inaccurate.

The 1991 letter also makes an important secondary distinction: the
difference between cyclical decline and structural transition. _"The
intrinsic value losses that we have suffered have been moderated
because the Buffalo News, under Stan Lipsey's leadership, has done
far better than most newspapers."_ Cyclical factors hurt current
look-through earnings, Buffett notes, _"but these factors do not
reduce intrinsic value."_ Structural factors do. The investor's task
is to distinguish one from the other — a question that connects
directly to [[Financial Community Appraisal]] and to the
mismanagement-tolerance distinction in [[Franchise vs Business Economics]].

## The 2006 case study: newspaper economics and the internet headwind

The 2006 letter provides the most explicit update to the 1991 media analysis.
By 2006 the structural transition the 1991 letter anticipated had proceeded
far enough that Buffett is willing to state it without qualification: _"the
days of lush profits from our newspaper are over."_

The historical economics of newspapers were the most reliable example of a
franchise tailwind Buffett ever observed. Two dynamics operated simultaneously.
The first was winner-take-all consolidation at the city level: in a one-paper
city, the paper that led in circulation attracted the most advertisers, which
made it more attractive to readers, which attracted more advertisers. The 2006
letter names this circularity _"Survival of the Fattest."_ Once a city paper
emerged as the sole survivor, its pricing power in both advertising and
circulation was nearly unlimited: rates could be raised annually, profits
rolled in, and management quality was largely irrelevant. The 2006 letter
quotes a _"not-too-bright publisher"_ on the underlying secret: _"I owe my
fortune to two great American institutions: monopoly and nepotism."_

The franchise rested on a specific structural foundation: classified advertising.
Newspapers were the only practical venue for finding a job, selling a car, or
advertising a home. The revenue base was captive. Advertisers had no
substitute.

The internet destroyed both pillars simultaneously. Classified advertising
migrated online to platforms that were faster, cheaper, and more searchable.
General display advertising fragmented as consumer attention dispersed across
cable television, satellite broadcasting, and an infinite number of websites.
The 2006 letter's sharpest formulation: _"if cable and satellite broadcasting,
as well as the internet, had come along first, newspapers as we know them
probably would never have existed."_ The implication is that the newspaper
franchise was not an enduring structural feature of information economics —
it was an artefact of a particular technological moment when no alternative
aggregation point for local information existed.

The fixed-cost structure that had been an advantage when volume was high became
a catastrophic disadvantage when volume fell. _"Fixed costs are high in the
newspaper business, and that's bad news when unit volume heads south."_ A
newspaper cannot quickly eliminate its printing plant, distribution network,
or editorial staff as revenue declines; the cost structure is quasi-fixed
against a revenue stream that is falling structurally, not cyclically. The
2006 letter distinguishes between cyclical and structural decline with unusual
clarity: _"we will work hard in Buffalo to develop a sustainable business
model. I think we will be successful. But the days of lush profits from our
newspaper are over."_ The second sentence is a statement about structure, not
a forecast about a bad quarter.

The diagnostic difference between the 1991 analysis and the 2006 one is
worth noting. In 1991, Buffett saw a franchise weakening — the tailwind was
diminishing — and described the valuation consequence with precision (franchise
at 25x transitioning toward business at 10x). By 2006 the analysis has
progressed further: the underlying economics have crossed a threshold from
weakening franchise to structurally challenged business. The 1991 warning
was about multiple compression ahead; the 2006 conclusion is that the
structural deterioration is irreversible.

## What this isn't

It is not an argument that headwind businesses should always be exited.
The 1977 letter explicitly defends staying in textiles on three grounds:
employer obligations to a workforce with non-transferable skills, the
historical role of textile cash flows in funding the original insurance
acquisition, and the possibility of modest residual profits with hard
work. Buffett's later position on Berkshire's textile operations evolved
in subsequent letters, but the 1977 framing acknowledges that "shutter
the headwind business" is too simple a rule. Capital allocation is
constrained by obligations the framework does not capture.

It is also not an argument that all tailwinds last. The 1977 letter is
explicit that the insurance underwriting cycle would turn and that
_"unusual managerial discipline will be required, as it runs counter to
normal institutional behavior to let the other fellow take away
business — even at foolish prices."_ A tailwind without operator
discipline gets squandered when the cycle turns. The lesson cuts both
ways.

## Where this connects to Sleep

Sleep does not use this exact vocabulary, but his eventual portfolio of
compounders — Costco, Amazon, Berkshire — is a portfolio of tailwind
businesses, selected partly on industry economics rather than purely on
operator skill. The [[Cigar Butt to Compounder]] evolution
the wiki's index references is, in part, the move from buying
mediocre-industry businesses cheaply to buying tailwind-industry
businesses at fair prices.

## Where this connects to Fisher

Fisher's [[Fifteen Points]] point 1 — whether the company
has products or services with sufficient market potential to make a
sizable increase in sales possible — is the Fisher analogue to the
tailwind side of this distinction. Fisher does not use the headwinds
framing as starkly, but his 1958 emphasis on growth industries over
mature ones points at the same asymmetry. Fisher's [[Stocks and Inflation]]
argument extends this: precisely because inflation is structurally persistent
in democratic economies, only growth-industry businesses (tailwind businesses)
provide genuine inflation protection. The tailwind criterion and the
inflation-hedge criterion resolve to the same underlying question about
the business.
