# Successful Failure

**Source:** [[The Bezos Letters]] — Anderson, Chapter 1 (Principle 1 of
his fourteen Growth Principles). This is a secondary-source reframing.
The primary-source treatment is [[Big Winners Pay for Many Experiments]],
drawn directly from Bezos's 2015 shareholder letter.

## What Anderson means by "Successful Failure"

Anderson's concept is drawn from Jim Lovell's description of the Apollo 13
mission as a "successful failure": the original goal (landing on the moon)
was not achieved, but the mission succeeded in the only way that then
mattered (returning the crew safely), and the lessons from the failure
strengthened NASA's subsequent programme. Anderson extends this to
Amazon: a failed experiment that produces learning is a better outcome than
not experimenting at all.

Anderson's version of the doctrine: *"If he tries something and it works,
that's great. But if he tries something and it doesn't work, he looks for ways
not only to make it work but to make it worth it."* The canonical examples
are Amazon Auctions (failed, led to Marketplace), zShops (failed, led to
Marketplace), and the Fire Phone (failed, but the engineering team and
learnings migrated to Echo and Alexa).

The core claim: an organisation that is too risk-averse to tolerate failed
experiments becomes incapable of the large bets that generate outsized
returns. Bezos: *"companies that don't continue to experiment, companies
that don't embrace failure, they eventually get in a desperate position where
the only thing they can do is a Hail Mary bet at the very end of their
corporate existence."*

## Where Anderson's framing holds

The cultural point is real and well-documented. Amazon does build failure
tolerance into its budgets and hiring practices. The "Bar Raiser" hiring
process, the Pay to Quit programme at Zappos, and the Career Choice
tuition investment are all consistent with an organisation that treats
experimentation and learning as structural rather than optional.

The practical guidance Anderson derives is also sound: distinguish between
competence-failure (unacceptable — Amazon has an "intolerance for
incompetence") and experiment-failure (expected — the result of genuinely
trying something whose outcome was uncertain). The former is a character
or skill problem; the latter is the cost of doing business in a regime of
asymmetric expected returns.

## Where Anderson's framing loses precision

The primary-source treatment in [[Big Winners Pay for Many Experiments]]
is sharper in two ways that Anderson's cultural framing elides.

First, Bezos's original frame is explicitly mathematical. *Given a 10% chance
of a 100× payoff, take that bet every time.* The emphasis is not on being
comfortable with failure — it is on understanding that the *portfolio's*
expected value is dominated by rare large wins, and that refusing the bets
because they individually fail most of the time is misapplied arithmetic.
Anderson's reframe of this as a cultural mindset loses the mathematical
rigour and makes the doctrine sound more like an attitude than a calculation.

Second, Bezos's emphasis in the primary source is on the *winners*, not the
failures. AWS, Marketplace, and Prime are the subject of the 2015 letter's
argument; the Fire Phone is the footnote. Anderson's structure — opening the
chapter with the Fire Phone write-off — inverts this emphasis and makes the
doctrine sound like a philosophy of failure-tolerance rather than a theory of
long-tailed return distributions.

The practical consequence of Anderson's framing is that it can be used to
justify experiments without a return thesis. Bezos's framing cannot: the 10%
chance of 100× requires honest estimation of the odds. A bet with a
genuinely small chance of a large win is not the same as a bet taken because
failure is culturally acceptable.

## What this means for how the page is used

matter — and this one does.
