# Reinvestment Risk

**Source:** [[Nomad Partnership Letters]] — 2008–2009 letters;
implicit across the full collection. Reinforced in later letters
and in the closing postamble.

## Summary

Reinvestment Risk is the cost incurred when a sold position must be
replaced with a less attractive one. Sleep's framing: the largest
single investment mistake is selling a position that subsequently
rises ten-fold, because the mistake is not merely the forgone gain
on the original position — it is the compounded disadvantage of then
having to hold whatever the proceeds were redeployed into instead.

Conventional risk analysis focuses on the cost of holding a bad
position. Reinvestment Risk is the cost of not holding a good one.
It is systematically underestimated, Sleep argues, because the cost
is invisible: an investor who sells and reinvests sees the returns
of the replacement position in their account; they do not see the
gap between those returns and what the original position would have
produced. This invisibility allows the mistake to be repeated without
an obvious warning signal.

## The mechanism

When an investor sells a position for any reason — valuation concerns,
capital redeployment, portfolio rebalancing — the proceeds must go
somewhere. In practice, the alternatives available at the moment of
sale are whatever the market is offering at that moment, and there
is no guarantee that those alternatives are as attractive as what
was sold. A great business compounding at 15–20% per annum, held
for another ten years, would produce a specific and large return.
The replacement position — whatever was available in the market at
the time — will produce whatever it produces, and the realistic
expectation is that it will be somewhat inferior.

The asymmetry compounds. A position that produces 20% per annum
for ten years turns \$1 into \$6.19. A replacement that produces 10%
per annum for the same period turns \$1 into \$2.59. The difference
— \$3.60 per dollar originally invested — is the reinvestment cost
of a sale that seemed modest at the time. Over a full investment
career, repeated instances of this decision produce dramatically
different outcomes.

## The Wal-Mart evidence

The 2009 letter reproduces a chart showing Wal-Mart's price-to-
earnings ratio alongside its earnings growth from the early 1970s
through the late 2000s. The chart demonstrates that for approximately
thirty years, an investor who had analysed Wal-Mart and identified
it as a great business using the [[Scale Economics Shared]] model
would have found it, at almost every point, appearing to be fully
or even slightly richly valued on near-term earnings metrics.

Sleep's observation: an investor who sold Wal-Mart in, say, 1978
because it looked fully priced would have faced the challenge of
finding an equally good business to reinvest in. Almost certainly,
the alternative would have been inferior. The same choice recurred
year after year for three decades. The investor who held through all
of it — even through periods of apparent overvaluation — would have
substantially outperformed the investor who sold at any of those
"rational" exit points.

The chart's lesson is about [[Destination Analysis]] as much as about
reinvestment risk: a business on its way to a great destination may
appear expensive on near-term metrics for an extended period, and
the investor who anchors to those metrics will systematically exit
too early and face the reinvestment problem repeatedly.

## Why the risk is underestimated

Sleep identifies several reasons investors consistently underestimate
reinvestment risk:

**The comparison is invisible.** The investor who sells sees the
returns of the replacement; they do not see the counterfactual return
of the original position. There is no account statement line showing
"opportunity cost of sale." The mistake is real but hard to observe.

**Short-term logic is compelling.** Selling a position that has
risen substantially "locks in the gain." Selling a position that has
fallen "stops the loss." Both are arguments that refer to the
position already held, not to the comparison between holding it and
the realistic alternatives. The reinvestment problem becomes visible
only after the fact.

**Activity is valorised.** Investment management culture rewards
the appearance of diligence, and trading is visible diligence.
Holding a position through volatility looks like passivity. The
incentive structure of the industry encourages transactions that
generate reinvestment risk rather than minimising it.

**Taxes and friction.** A sale triggers realisation of any embedded
capital gain, which reduces the proceeds available for reinvestment.
This friction compounds the reinvestment problem: the investor who
holds avoids the tax drag on turnover; the investor who trades
repeatedly pays it repeatedly.

## The conclusion: near-permanent holdings

The 2008–2009 letters mark the pivot in Sleep's portfolio management
from a more transactional approach (including cigar butts, turnarounds,
and distressed situations) to the explicitly near-permanent holding
approach. Sleep writes that the best businesses he has identified
are ones where the right answer, under almost all circumstances, is
to do nothing — because the alternatives are worse, the thesis remains
intact, and the reinvestment risk of selling is large.

In the postamble (written in 2021 after the Partnership's closure),
Sleep reflects: "we had what we needed, just a few superb businesses
and we were unlikely to sell any of those to fund the purchase of
another cigar butt." The sentence captures the logic of reinvestment
risk in its most compressed form: once a portfolio contains genuinely
great businesses, the only real question is whether any of them has
deteriorated. Transacting for the sake of finding something new is
likely to reduce, not improve, the portfolio's quality.

## Contradictions

Reinvestment Risk is in direct tension with Fisher's third valid
reason to sell — the discovery of a clearly better opportunity.
Fisher's framework treats this as a legitimate trigger for a sale
even from a position that has not deteriorated. Sleep treats the same
situation with scepticism: the "clearly better opportunity" must
clear the bar of being not merely better in an expected-return sense
but substantially better, because the realistic cost of being wrong
includes the compounded reinvestment disadvantage. See
[[Selling vs Holding Forever]].
