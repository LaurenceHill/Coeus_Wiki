# Growth vs Value False Dichotomy

**The tension:** Conventional investment practice divides investors
into "growth" investors (who pay high multiples for businesses with
fast-growing earnings) and "value" investors (who buy businesses
trading below their asset value or at low earnings multiples). Sleep,
Buffett, and Fisher all argue this division is a category error — one
that causes both groups to make systematic mistakes.

## The conventional framework

"Value" investing, in the Graham tradition, means buying securities
at a meaningful discount to their net asset value or normalised
earnings power. The investor is compensated by the gap between price
and a conservative estimate of current worth. Growth is a nice
additional feature but not what drives the thesis.

"Growth" investing, in its popular form, means buying businesses
whose earnings are growing faster than average and accepting a premium
multiple as the price of access to that growth. The investor is
compensated by the earnings growth compounding into a large future
earnings base that justifies today's price in retrospect.

The dichotomy is institutionally entrenched: equity funds are
routinely classified as "value" or "growth" for marketing and
benchmarking purposes, and the classification persists even though
the underlying investment rationale is the same in both cases.

## Sleep's argument

Sleep names the dichotomy explicitly in the 2004 Nomad letter and
identifies it as the most common mislabelling in the investment
industry. His argument: all investing is the attempt to pay less
than intrinsic value. Intrinsic value incorporates both current
earnings power and future earnings growth, because the discounted
value of a business's future cash flows depends on both. Separating
"growth" from "value" is therefore as conceptually confused as
separating the "size" of a number from its "magnitude."

The practical error the dichotomy produces: a "value" investor
who screens on current price-to-earnings or price-to-book will
systematically exclude the best [[Scale Economics Shared]] businesses.
Costco, Amazon in its investment-heavy years, and AirAsia all screen
as "expensive" on near-term earnings multiples while offering very
large intrinsic value at the destination state. Applying a value
screen to near-term multiples and dismissing the result as "too
expensive" is not value investing; it is the confusion of a metric
for the concept.

Conversely, a "growth" investor who pays any price for fast earnings
growth without regard for competitive durability is making a different
error: growth that will be competed away or that relies on a temporary
market condition has very little value, regardless of its near-term
rate. The [[Robustness Ratio]] is Sleep's answer to this version
of the mistake — it anchors the assessment of growth's value to the
structural durability of the competitive position that enables it.

## Buffett's argument

Buffett names the dichotomy explicitly in the 1992 letter's "Common
Stock Investments" section — the clearest statement in the entire
corpus:

> "many analysts feel they must choose between two approaches
> customarily thought to be in opposition: 'value' and 'growth.'
> Indeed, many investment professionals see any mixing of the two
> terms as a form of intellectual cross-dressing. We view that as
> fuzzy thinking...In our opinion, the two approaches are joined at
> the hip: Growth is always a component in the calculation of value,
> constituting a variable whose importance can range from negligible
> to enormous and whose impact can be negative as well as positive."

The 1992 letter also argues that the term "value investing" is
redundant: _"What is 'investing' if it is not the act of seeking value
at least sufficient to justify the amount paid? Consciously paying
more for a stock than its calculated value — in the hope that it can
soon be sold for a still-higher price — should be labeled
speculation."_ The implication is that "growth investing" is only a
coherent category if the growth has been paid for with additional
value; otherwise it is speculation dressed as investment analysis.

The unifying framework Buffett offers is John Burr Williams's
discounted-cash-flow formula from _The Theory of Investment Value_
(written over fifty years before the 1992 letter): _"The value of any
stock, bond or business today is determined by the cash inflows and
outflows — discounted at an appropriate interest rate — that can be
expected to occur during the remaining life of the asset."_ Note, as
Buffett emphasises, that the formula is identical for stocks and bonds.
The only distinction is that for equities the analyst must estimate the
future "coupons" herself. Growth is simply one of the inputs to the
coupon estimate; it has no independent status.

The 1992 letter delivers the counterexample that makes the point
concrete: airline businesses. Investors have poured capital into the
domestic airline industry to finance profitless growth for decades.
_"For these investors, it would have been far better if Orville had
failed to get off the ground at Kitty Hawk: The more the industry has
grown, the worse the disaster for owners."_ Growth destroys value when
the incremental capital invested earns below its cost. _"Growth benefits
investors only when the business in point can invest at incremental
returns that are enticing — in other words, only when each dollar used
to finance the growth creates over a dollar of long-term market value.
In the case of a low-return business requiring incremental funds,
growth hurts the investor."_

The 1992 letter also introduces Buffett's clearest statement of the
purchase-discipline that flows from this framework: _"we believe this
margin-of-safety principle, so strongly emphasized by Ben Graham, to
be the cornerstone of investment success."_ The [[Margin of Safety]]
page develops this further.

The 1992 material fills out what was already implicit throughout the
earlier letters. The 1977 letter establishes that Buffett wants
businesses that earn good returns on capital; the 1983 [[Economic Goodwill]] discussion captures the point without naming the dichotomy —
a business with genuine Economic Goodwill is simultaneously a "value"
investment (the goodwill is worth paying for) and a "growth" investment
(the excess returns can be reinvested at the same high rate). Trying
to classify it as one or the other distorts what is being bought.

Buffett's oft-quoted formulation — _"it's far better to buy a
wonderful business at a fair price than a fair business at a wonderful
price"_ — is a direct statement of the dichotomy's failure. A "value"
investor focused narrowly on current metrics will prefer the fair
business at a wonderful price; the correct analysis prefers the
wonderful business at a fair price, because the long-run compounding
of the wonderful business dominates.

## Fisher's argument

Fisher does not use the growth-vs-value language — _Common Stocks
and Uncommon Profits_ predates the dichotomy's institutionalisation —
but his [[Fifteen Points]] are exclusively about business quality,
management capability, and the ability to sustain growth. Fisher
would have found the "value" label meaningless: if you buy a business
that cannot sustain its earnings, you have not purchased value
regardless of how cheap it looked. If you buy a business that can
sustain and grow its earnings for decades, you have purchased enormous
value regardless of how expensive it looked on today's metrics.

Fisher's patience framework [[When to Sell]] makes the same
point from the sell side: the three valid reasons to sell are all
about whether the business has changed, not about whether its
price has risen to a level that "growth" or "value" screeners would
label expensive. This is implicitly a rejection of the dichotomy:
if the business is as good as it was when purchased, neither a high
current earnings multiple nor a move away from "value" territory
is a reason to sell.

## Where the three framings agree

Sleep, Buffett, and Fisher all reach the same practical conclusion:
the relevant quantity is intrinsic value, calculated over a long
horizon, incorporating both the current earnings base and the rate
at which it can sustainably grow. A price below intrinsic value is
attractive; a price above it is not; and no label — "growth" or
"value" — changes this basic arithmetic.

All three also agree that the most dangerous version of the dichotomy
is the growth investor who pays any price for growth without assessing
durability. Fast growth in a business without a structural advantage
dissipates quickly as competitors enter; paying a premium multiple
for it produces poor returns. This is the failure mode the
[[Robustness Ratio]] is designed to screen against: nominal growth
matters much less than the structural conditions that determine
whether it persists.

## What this contradiction is actually with

This page is different from the others in the wiki's contradiction
section. The tension is not between two of the five primary sources;
it is between all three sources (Sleep, Buffett, Fisher) on one side
and the institutionalised convention of the investment industry on the
other. It is preserved here because the convention is so widespread
that investors studying these sources will repeatedly encounter the
growth-vs-value framing and need a clear statement of why all three
sources reject it.
