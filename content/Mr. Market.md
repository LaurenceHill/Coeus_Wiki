# Mr. Market

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1987 letter, "Marketable
Securities — Permanent Holdings" section (the parable); 1990 letter,
Wells Fargo discussion and portfolio-buying-posture section (the food-price
analogy and contrarian-thinking correction); 1997 letter, "How We Think
About Market Fluctuations" section (the hamburger analogy and net-buyer
logic); 2008 and 2009 letters (crisis capital allocation and approval
as enemy of investing). Graham's original formulation appears in *The
Intelligent Investor* (1949); Buffett cites Graham explicitly and
reproduces the parable in full.

## Summary

Ben Graham, Buffett's teacher, proposed imagining that you own a business
in partnership with a fellow named Mr. Market. Mr. Market shows up every
day and names a price at which he will either buy your share or sell you
his. The critical feature: he has "incurable emotional problems." On good
days he is euphoric and names a very high price, afraid you will take his
interest and rob him of coming gains. On bad days he is despondent and
names a very low price, terrified that you will unload your stake onto him.
He doesn't mind being ignored — tomorrow he will return with a new price.
The more manic-depressive he is, the more useful he is to you.

The practical instruction that follows from the parable is precise: "Mr.
Market is there to serve you, not to guide you." His pocketbook is what
matters, not his wisdom. If he shows up in a foolish mood, you can ignore
him or exploit him — but it is disastrous to fall under his influence. If you
cannot value your business independently and with more judgement than
Mr. Market brings, you do not belong in the game. As they say in poker:
if you've been at the table thirty minutes and don't know who the patsy is,
you're the patsy.

## Why it matters

The parable reframes the investor's relationship to market prices at the
structural level. It does not say prices are usually wrong. It says that prices
are episodically wrong in ways that are useful to the investor who can
maintain independent judgement — and harmful to the investor who cannot.

The distinction matters because price movements carry social weight. When
a stock falls sharply, the natural instinct is to treat the fall as evidence that
something is wrong with the business. When it rises sharply, the instinct is
to treat the rise as confirmation that the assessment was correct. Mr. Market
inverts this: price movements are evidence of Mr. Market's mood, not of
the business's condition. The business's condition is determined by its
operating results, its competitive position, its cash generation — none of
which Mr. Market understands better than a careful analyst does.

Buffett cites Graham's weighing machine observation directly: "In the short
run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run it is a weighing
machine." The voting machine is subject to all the pressures of social
proof, momentum, narrative, and fear. The weighing machine is indifferent
to all of these. The investor's advantage is that she is operating on the
weighing machine's timescale while Mr. Market quotes prices on the voting
machine's timescale. Delayed recognition, Buffett adds, is not merely
tolerable — it can be an advantage, giving the investor a chance to buy
more of a good thing at a bargain price before the weighing machine catches
up.

## The 1987 context

Buffett introduces the Mr. Market parable in the 1987 letter specifically to
explain Berkshire's posture after October's market crash. While money
managers running "portfolio insurance" strategies were automatically selling
as prices fell — mechanically accelerating the decline — Buffett's response
was to look for buying opportunities. A few prices fell far enough to be
interesting before they rebounded. The portfolio insurance episode is
treated as a vivid example of investors who had lost the ability to distinguish
between Mr. Market's mood and underlying business value: they had
outsourced their judgement to price momentum, becoming precisely the
kind of market-follower the parable warns against.

The 1987 letter also notes the irony that "professional" investors —
managing billions — had made markets more volatile, not less, by treating
stocks as "tokens in a game, like the thimble and flatiron in Monopoly."
This is a larger claim: that the domination of markets by institutional
traders with short time horizons makes the Mr. Market divergences larger
and more frequent, not smaller — creating better opportunities, not worse,
for the investor with genuine conviction.

## The 1990 extension: optimism as the real enemy

The 1990 letter extends the Mr. Market framework with two passages
that sharpen the practical instruction.

The first is the food-price analogy, which gives the concept its most
intuitive form: *"Investors who expect to be ongoing buyers of
investments throughout their lifetimes should adopt a similar attitude
toward market fluctuations; instead many illogically become euphoric
when stock prices rise and unhappy when they fall. They show no such
confusion in their reaction to food prices: Knowing they are forever
going to be buyers of food, they welcome falling prices and deplore
price increases."* Buffett's treatment of Wells Fargo stock during the
1990 California real-estate panic is the applied case: the stock fell
almost 50% within a few months, and Berkshire welcomed the decline
because it allowed further purchases at panic prices.

The second passage corrects the most common misreading of the
contrarian position: *"The most common cause of low prices is pessimism
— sometimes pervasive, sometimes specific to a company or industry. We
want to do business in such an environment, not because we like
pessimism but because we like the prices it produces. It's optimism
that is the enemy of the rational buyer."* And immediately after: *"None
of this means, however, that a business or stock is an intelligent
purchase simply because it is unpopular; a contrarian approach is just
as foolish as a follow-the-crowd strategy. What's required is thinking
rather than polling."* The Mr. Market parable does not license buying
whatever is unloved; it licenses buying what is independently known to
be undervalued and is unloved only because Mr. Market is depressed. The
distinction requires an independent prior assessment of value — without
which the parable becomes an excuse rather than a discipline. This is
[[Denial, Anchoring, Drift]] in reverse: just as investors can hold
a deteriorating thesis by anchoring to a prior high, they can buy a
deteriorating business by anchoring to the observation that it is cheap.
The parable requires a prior, not just a price.

## The 1997 extension: the net-buyer logic

The 1997 letter offers a stripped-down version of the framework that
removes the psychology entirely and replaces it with arithmetic. The
argument is a quiz: if you plan to eat hamburgers throughout your life and
are not a cattle producer, should you wish for higher or lower beef prices?
The answer is obvious. The market question is the same: if you expect to be
a net buyer of stocks for the next five years, should you hope for a higher
or lower market?

*"Many investors get this one wrong. Even though they are going to be net
buyers of stocks for many years to come, they are elated when stock prices
rise and depressed when they fall. In effect, they rejoice because prices
have risen for the 'hamburgers' they will soon be buying."*

The editorial instruction Buffett offers for reading market news: when a
headline says "Investors lose as market falls," edit it mentally to
*"Disinvestors lose as market falls — but investors gain."* Every fall has a
buyer and a seller; what hurts one necessarily helps the other. Berkshire's
own saving programme — retaining all earnings and reinvesting them — means
that lower market prices are a standing advantage for its shareholders:
the same retained dollar buys more of good businesses when prices are
depressed than when they are elevated.

The argument also runs through the investees: companies such as Coca-Cola,
Washington Post, and Wells Fargo repurchased their own shares throughout
the period. *"The repurchases that Coca-Cola, The Washington Post and Wells
Fargo made in past years at very low prices benefited Berkshire far more
than do today's repurchases, made at loftier prices."* This completes the
mechanism: Mr. Market's depressed moods are not just buying opportunities
for Berkshire directly but amplifiers for the compounding of every
long-term holding through share repurchases.

## Crisis capital allocation (2008–2009)

The financial crisis of 2008–2009 produced Buffett's sharpest distillation of
the Mr. Market framework into operating principle. Two maxims from the 2008
letter are worth preserving verbatim.

The first is about the posture to take toward capital allocation in panics:
*"When investing, pessimism is your friend, euphoria the enemy."* This
inverts the emotional response that markets typically produce. Falling prices
feel bad; rising prices feel good. The Mr. Market framework insists these
feelings are backwards for the net buyer of assets.

The second goes further, addressing the social dimension of investment
decisions — the desire for approval from peers, commentators, and the
market itself: *"Approval is not the goal of investing. In fact, approval
is often counter-productive because it sedates the brain and makes it less
receptive to new facts or a re-examination of conclusions formed earlier.
Beware the investment activity that produces applause; the great moves are
usually greeted by yawns."* The observation cuts at the institutional
investor's structural problem: managing client relationships and
managing capital are two different jobs, and optimising for the former
tends to corrupt the latter.

The 2009 letter extends this into the specific context of the 2008 crisis,
where Berkshire had arranged its finances to be a capital supplier rather
than a supplicant: *"A climate of fear is their best friend"* for investors
who have maintained the ability to act. The essential precondition — surplus
liquidity, no forced selling — is not separate from the Mr. Market posture
but its practical enablement. The investor who cannot survive Mr. Market's
panic cannot profit from it. See [[Leverage and Liquidity]] for the
financial architecture that makes crisis deployment possible.

The 2009 letter also offers the most compressed version of the rain-gold
maxim: *"When it's raining gold, reach for a bucket, not a thimble."*
Berkshire deployed $15.5 billion in 25 days during the post-Lehman panic
— rates available during those days "would have been unavailable in normal
markets."

## The EMT parallel

Buffett explicitly links the Mr. Market parable to his critique of Efficient
Market Theory in the surrounding period. The EMT crowd, he observes,
moved from the correct observation that the market is frequently efficient
to the incorrect conclusion that it is always efficient. Mr. Market demolishes
the "always" — if the market were always efficient, Mr. Market would have
no incurable emotional problems; every daily price would be a fair estimate
of underlying value. The empirical record of investors who exploited Mr.
Market's episodes — Graham himself, the investors in the
"Graham-and-Doddsville" tradition documented in Buffett's 1984 lecture —
is the refutation. See [[Financial Community Appraisal]] for Fisher's
independently developed parallel argument.

## The failure mode

The parable has two ways to misapply it. The first is to assume that
because Mr. Market is often wrong, his prices should always be disregarded.
They should not. When prices rise far enough above estimated intrinsic
value, Mr. Market is offering to buy you out at prices that exceed what the
business warrants. Buffett notes in the 1987 letter that Berkshire will sell
holdings that have become overpriced in the market, even from the
permanent holdings, if prices become genuinely irrational on the high side.
Mr. Market serves you in both directions: as a buyer when he is depressed
and as a seller when he is euphoric.

The second failure mode is to confuse Mr. Market's bad mood with a
genuine deterioration in the business. This is the hardest practical
challenge the parable creates. A falling price is usually Mr. Market being
depressed, but sometimes it is the weighing machine correctly assessing a
business that is genuinely in decline. The investor who invokes Mr. Market
as a reason to hold a deteriorating business is using the parable as
emotional cover for analytical failure. The parable provides a posture
toward market prices, not a substitute for re-evaluating the business itself.

## Contradictions

There is a tension between the Mr. Market framework and the [[Selling vs Holding Forever]] contradiction page. Mr. Market says sell when he is
euphoric; Sleep's near-permanent holding framework says almost never
sell the quality compounders. The resolution Buffett's own practice
suggests: the permanent holdings he describes in the 1987 letter are
businesses he would not sell even at prices that appear far above intrinsic
value, because the reinvestment problem — finding an equivalent business —
is too difficult. For those, "near permanent" effectively wins. For other
positions, Mr. Market's euphoric moods are genuine selling triggers.
