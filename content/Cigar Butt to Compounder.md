# Cigar Butt to Compounder

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1989 letter, "Mistakes of
the First Twenty-five Years," for the canonical naming and critique; 1991
letter, "Twenty Years in a Candy Store" section, for the definitive
applied case study; 2023 letter, Munger tribute as the definitive account
of the 1965 pivot moment;
[[Nomad Partnership Letters]] — early letters (2001–2003) for the cigar
butt phase; 2009 letter for the articulated transition; postamble (2021)
for the retrospective.

## Summary

Sleep's investment approach evolved over the thirteen years of the
Nomad Partnership from a Graham-style orientation — buying statistically
cheap, distressed, or temporarily unloved businesses for a one-time
recovery — to the deliberate pursuit of "near permanent" holdings in
high-quality compounding businesses that should not be sold.

The transition is not merely a change of investment style. It reflects
a genuine analytical conclusion: that the [[Reinvestment Risk]] of
exiting quality compounders is larger than most investors account for,
and that the [[Scale Economics Shared]] model, where it operates, creates
competitive positions that deepen over time rather than eroding.

The phrase "cigar butt" refers to Benjamin Graham's metaphor: a
cheap business is like a discarded cigar with a few puffs still left
in it. You pick it up, take the last puff, and discard it. The return
is one puff. The investment requires constant replacement — you must
find a new cigar after each one is spent. Buffett made the same
transition earlier and described it as moving from "fair companies at
wonderful prices" to "wonderful companies at fair prices."

## Buffett's canonical statement (1989)

Buffett names the cigar butt approach explicitly in the 1989 letter's
"Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years" section, calling the purchase of
Berkshire Hathaway's textile operations his first and most instructive error:

> "A cigar butt found on the street that has only one puff left in it may
> not offer much of a smoke, but the 'bargain purchase' will make that puff
> all profit."

The 1989 letter then offers the clearest statement of why the approach fails
and what replaces it: "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair
price than a fair company at a wonderful price." The cigar butt, Buffett
explains, creates two structural problems beyond the one-puff limitation.
First, the original "bargain" price is rarely as cheap as it looks — in a
difficult business, no sooner is one problem solved than another surfaces.
Second, any initial advantage is quickly eroded by the low return the
business earns on capital. The temporal dimension is decisive: "Time is the
friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre." A business
bought at a 20% discount to liquidation value and sold at fair value ten
years later, having earned 4% on capital throughout, produces a terrible
result compared to a wonderful business compounding at 15% over the same
period. The arithmetic of time rewards quality and punishes mediocrity,
regardless of purchase price.

Buffett acknowledges learning this lesson "several times over" before it
stuck — the Hochschild Kohn department store acquisition, bought at a
discount to book with attractive extras, was sold three years later for
approximately what was paid. Charlie Munger understood the principle
early; Buffett describes himself as a slow learner. By the time of the 1989
retrospective, however, the doctrine is complete: first-class businesses
accompanied by first-class managements, at a fair price.

## The See's Candy retrospective (1991)

The 1991 letter's "Twenty Years in a Candy Store" section provides
the most compelling case study for the compounder thesis in the entire
corpus — a twenty-year track record available for inspection.

Blue Chip Stamps (then a Berkshire affiliate) purchased See's Candy
in January 1972. The asking price was $40 million for full ownership;
with $10 million of excess cash, the true price was $30 million.
See's had tangible net worth of only $7 million. Buffett and Munger
argued the price down to $25 million — *"not yet fully appreciative
of the value of an economic franchise."* The sellers accepted.

Over the next twenty years: sales grew from $29 million to $196
million; pre-tax profits grew from $4.2 million to $42.4 million; and
the additional capital required to run the business was only $18
million (from $7 million of net worth at purchase to $25 million in
1991). The remaining $410 million of pre-tax profits were distributed
to Berkshire for redeployment elsewhere.

The arithmetic is the clearest available illustration of why the
compounder thesis wins over long periods. A cigar-butt purchase at a
30% discount and a rapid recovery might produce 43% in a year. But
See's, purchased at a premium to tangible book and held without
selling, produced a return on the original $25 million purchase price
that compounded at a rate that dwarfs any one-puff cigar. The business
grew its earnings roughly 10-fold while requiring almost no additional
capital to do so — which is the definition of a franchise-calibre
compounder.

Buffett identifies the analytical foundation of the purchase: *"We saw
that the business had untapped pricing power."* Annual price increases
at See's were accepted by customers year after year without volume
loss, because See's customers perceived no adequate substitute for
See's chocolate in the relevant geography. The pricing power was
structural, not cyclical, and it held for twenty years. Identifying
that structure before the market had capitalised it — and before
Buffett himself fully appreciated the vocabulary to describe it —
was the core of the analytical work.

The letter also gives the operational mechanism: *"For an increase in
profits to be evaluated properly, it must be compared with the
incremental capital investment required to produce it. On this score,
See's has been astounding."* This is the compounder test in its
operational form — the same question Fisher asks about [[Fifteen Points]]
and Sleep asks through the [[Robustness Ratio]]: not whether earnings
are growing, but whether they are growing in excess of the capital
required to sustain them.

## The 1965 pivot: Munger's definitive account (2023)

Buffett has told the story of moving from Graham to quality-at-fair-prices across many letters, but the 2023 Munger tribute provides the most precise account of when the transition happened and who caused it. In 1965, Munger advised Buffett directly: forget about buying another distressed business like Berkshire Hathaway's textile operations; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices and abandon everything you learned from Ben Graham. This was not a refinement of the existing method — it was a replacement.

Buffett credits this advice as the origin of the entire compounder architecture. The 1989 letter named the intellectual framework; the 2023 letter names the moment and the person. Together they give the clearest possible account of why the transition happened: Munger was right and Buffett, to his credit, recognised it and acted on it even though it required discarding the method that had already made him successful.

The 2023 letter adds one detail that the 1989 letter did not: Munger had been certain of his position but gave Buffett full operational control once Buffett was persuaded. This matters for the compounder thesis because the transition required not just intellectual adoption of a new framework but the willingness to act on it decisively — to begin paying fair prices for wonderful businesses rather than wonderful prices for fair ones, to hold through apparent overvaluation rather than selling at recovery, and to concentrate rather than diversify. Munger supplied the conviction; Buffett supplied the execution.

## The early phase

The earliest Nomad letters (2001–2003) show a portfolio with genuine
diversity of approach: cigar-butt situations, distressed debt in
Argentina, regional businesses trading at deep discounts to asset value,
turnaround situations. The analytical work is focused on near-term
catalysts and reversion to asset value.

Sleep is candid in later letters about the limitations of this approach
not because it was unprofitable — it was — but because it generates
constant transactional demand. Each position, once it recovers to a
fair value, must be sold and replaced. The investor running this
approach is always looking for the next cheap stock, always doing the
next piece of research, always facing the reinvestment problem.

The most expensive moment in a cigar-butt strategy is the moment of
exit: you have sold a position that was cheap, presumably at a fair
price, and must now find something else that is cheap. The pool of
alternatives may be smaller, more picked-over, or simply different in
quality from what was sold. The transaction that looks like a
disciplined exit is also the moment of maximum reinvestment risk.

## The Costco observation

The transition begins, in the letters' chronology, with the Costco
observation in the early 2000s. Sleep notices that Costco is structurally
different from businesses he has been buying. It is not cheap in the
traditional sense — it does not trade at a deep discount to book value
or at a distressed earnings multiple. But the more Sleep analyses
the business, the less he wants to sell it at any plausible near-term
price, because the business is getting better as it gets bigger, in
ways that create a widening gap between Costco and any realistic
competitor.

This is the first encounter with what Sleep will later formalise as
[[Scale Economics Shared]]. The Costco position does not fit the
cigar-butt framework. It does not have a recovery price at which
the thesis is complete. It has a destination — a large, scale-efficient,
price-leader retailer — whose value is not captured in any near-term
earnings multiple. See [[Destination Analysis]].

## The 2009 crystallisation

The 2008–2009 period marks the explicit statement of the transition.
The Nomad portfolio has been heavily concentrated in a small number of
[[Scale Economics Shared]] businesses — Costco, Amazon, AirAsia,
Berkshire Hathaway — and Sleep decides that the portfolio should be
managed as a near-permanent holding. The language shifts from asking
"when should we sell this position" to asking "what would have to be
true for us to sell this position."

The 2009 letter includes the Wal-Mart chart: an illustration of how a
great compounding business can remain apparently cheap for thirty years,
and how an investor who sold at any point along the way would have faced
the reinvestment problem of finding something equivalent. Sleep uses
the chart to argue that the analysis supporting a sale — "it looks
expensive on current earnings" — is almost always wrong for this class
of business, because the destination value is not captured in near-term
earnings.

## Why the transition is analytically justified

Sleep's argument is not that cigar butts are bad investments. The
early Nomad letters show they can be very good ones. The argument is
that the comparison between cigar butts and quality compounders, done
carefully, favours compounders — and that the favour grows with the
holding period.

A cigar butt at a 30% discount to fair value produces approximately
43% if it takes one year to recover and approximately 0% if it never
recovers. A quality compounder compounding at 15% per annum produces
100% over five years, 200% over eight years, 305% over ten years. The
longer the holding period, the more dramatically the compounder wins.
But the cigar-butt investor rarely extends the holding period beyond
recovery, because the whole thesis is a reversion trade. The compound
path is available only to the investor who has the analytical framework
to justify holding beyond apparent full valuation — which is exactly
what [[Destination Analysis]] provides.

## The closing postamble

In the 2021 postamble written after the Partnership's wind-down, Sleep
reflects: "we had what we needed, just a few superb businesses and
we were unlikely to sell any of those to fund the purchase of another
cigar butt." The sentence crystallises the conclusion of the transition:
once a portfolio contains genuinely excellent compounders, the right
question is not what to replace them with but whether any of them has
deteriorated. The answer in Nomad's final years was no, which is why
the portfolio was effectively static.

Sleep also notes that Nomad mirrored Buffett's own evolution: the
early letters acknowledge the Graham influence explicitly, and the
later letters show the same migration toward quality and permanence
that Buffett documented less systematically in the Berkshire letters
from the 1970s onward.
