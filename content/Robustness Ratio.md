# Robustness Ratio

**Source:** [[Nomad Partnership Letters]] — 2005 letter, Appendix
"Measuring the Moat."

## Summary

The Robustness Ratio is Sleep's quantitative measure of the strength
of a [[Scale Economics Shared]] business's competitive position. It is
defined as:

> **Customer savings ÷ Shareholder earnings**

The numerator is the annual savings the business delivers to its
customers relative to what they would pay at a comparable competitor.
The denominator is the annual earnings the business generates for its
shareholders. The ratio answers a single question: for every dollar of
profit the business earns, how much value is it giving away to customers
that a competitor would have to match?

A higher ratio means the business is sharing more with customers
relative to what it keeps. A lower ratio means the business is
extracting more margin relative to what it shares.

## Why it matters

The ratio makes the competitive durability of a business legible in a
way that conventional moat language — "brand," "network effects,"
"switching costs" — does not. Those terms are qualitative and
difficult to compare across businesses. The Robustness Ratio is
expressed in the same units (dollars) for all businesses and can be
estimated from publicly available financial data.

The mechanism is straightforward: a competitor trying to take market
share from a scale-economics-shared business must match its customer
value proposition. If the business saves customers $$6 for every $$1
it earns for shareholders, the competitor must provide $$6 of value
for every $$1 it hopes to earn — an implausible ongoing subsidy for
most well-capitalised challengers. A business saving customers $$1 for
every $$1 it earns is far easier to undercut, because a competitor
only has to match the $$1 of customer savings.

The ratio also reveals something that conventional financial metrics
obscure. A business with low reported margins may be extremely robust
to competition if its low margins are the result of deliberately sharing
scale savings with customers. A business with high reported margins may
be fragile if those margins represent a gap between cost and price that
a scale-equivalent competitor could close.

## The worked examples from the 2005 letter

**Costco** is Sleep's canonical high-ratio business. At the time of
the letter, Costco was estimated to save its members approximately
$$9–10 billion annually relative to what they would pay at comparable
retailers. Costco's annual earnings attributable to shareholders were
approximately $$1.5 billion. The Robustness Ratio was therefore
approximately 6:1. Sleep's interpretation: to displace Costco, a
competitor would need to offer its customers $$6 of savings for every
$$1 of profit it hoped to earn. Given that building equivalent scale
takes years and sustained losses, the practical barrier is very high.

**GEICO** is Sleep's lower-ratio example. GEICO operates as the
low-cost auto insurer in the United States, saving policyholders
roughly the same amount as it earns for its shareholders —
approximately 1:1. A 1:1 ratio is not worthless: GEICO still has
a cost advantage, and customers are still better off with GEICO than
with higher-cost alternatives. But the ratio signals that a well-funded
competitor willing to operate at breakeven could, in principle, match
GEICO's price. This is a different and somewhat lower order of
competitive protection.

Sleep uses GEICO partly because it is a Berkshire Hathaway subsidiary,
making the comparison between a 1:1 and a 6:1 ratio illustrative
rather than adversarial. Buffett admires GEICO enormously; Sleep is
not suggesting it is a poor business. The point is that the _structure_
of competitive protection is different, and the ratio reveals that
difference in a way that qualitative moat language would not.

## How to estimate the ratio

The numerator — customer savings — requires identifying what the
customer would pay at an equivalent alternative and comparing it to
what they pay at the business being assessed. For retailers, this
often means comparing shelf prices or total basket costs against a
named competitor. For insurance companies, it means comparing premium
rates against the industry average. The calculation is imprecise but
directionally reliable: a business saving customers 5–6 times what
it earns is different from a business saving customers 0.5 times what
it earns, regardless of measurement uncertainty.

The denominator — shareholder earnings — is the business's net income
or free cash flow, depending on which better reflects true economic
earnings. For businesses in heavy investment phases (Amazon in
its early years, for example), reported earnings may understate true
economic earnings; for businesses in harvest mode, they may overstate.
The ratio should be assessed on a normalised basis.

## Caveats

The ratio is not static. As a business grows, the customer savings it
delivers typically grow in absolute terms (more customers, lower per-
unit costs, more passed on). But shareholder earnings can grow faster
or slower depending on capital allocation decisions. A business that
aggressively reinvests — as Amazon did for most of its history — will
show very low shareholder earnings and a therefore extreme Robustness
Ratio, not because the moat is exceptionally strong but because the
denominator is temporarily suppressed. Care is needed in interpreting
ratios for capital-intensive growth businesses.

The ratio also cannot distinguish between a business that deliberately
shares savings (structural) and one that has low margins because of
operational inefficiency (weakness). The qualitative analysis must
accompany the quantitative measure: the question is whether low margins
are a choice or a constraint.
