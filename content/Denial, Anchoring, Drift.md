# Denial, Anchoring, Drift

**Source:** [[Nomad Partnership Letters]] — 2008–2009 letters;
developed in response to the financial crisis and its aftermath.

## Summary

Sleep identifies three psychological failure modes as the most
insidious mistakes in long-duration investing. Unlike a bad purchase
decision — which is visible, dateable, and traceable to a specific
error in analysis — denial, anchoring, and drift all occur gradually,
disguise themselves as patience or stability, and produce their worst
damage precisely when the investor believes they are behaving
correctly.

The three are distinct in mechanism but related in consequence.
Each causes an investor to maintain a position that should be
re-evaluated or exited, while providing a plausible narrative for
why holding is the right answer.

## Denial

Denial is the refusal to accept that a thesis has broken down.
The investment was made on a set of premises. One or more of those
premises has been refuted — by new information, by management
behaviour, by competitive developments, or by changes in the
industry structure. The investor in denial updates the investment
narrative to accommodate the disconfirming evidence rather than
updating the position.

The tell-tale sign is the expanding narrative. An investment thesis
that initially required three sentences to justify now requires
three paragraphs. The new paragraphs are not additions to the thesis;
they are workarounds for evidence that the original thesis can no
longer accommodate. Each workaround makes the investor more
psychologically invested, because they have spent analytical effort
constructing it.

Sleep's observation: the investor in denial is not being irrational
in their own internal logic. The accommodation of disconfirming
evidence by modifying the thesis is, in a strict sense, Bayesian —
new evidence should update priors. The problem is that the updating
is systematically biased in the direction of preserving the original
conclusion. The investor updates the thesis, not the position.

The antidote Sleep implies is the deliberate use of the "kill"
test: would I buy this business today, at this price, knowing what
I now know, if I did not already own it? If the honest answer is no,
the psychological work required to maintain the position is likely
denial, not considered patience.

## Anchoring

Anchoring is the use of a reference price — typically the purchase
price or the historical peak price — as the primary benchmark for
assessing whether to hold, add, or exit a position. The purchase
price is not relevant to the future returns of the position. The
intrinsic value of the business and its current market price are
the relevant quantities. The purchase price determines what the
investor has already experienced; it has no bearing on what they
will experience next.

The most common anchoring failure is the refusal to add to a fallen
position because the current price is below the purchase price, making
the loss feel "real" on paper. The position is now cheaper — and
arguably more attractive, if the thesis is intact — but the investor
is reluctant to formalise the loss by adding at a lower level.
The anchor (purchase price) is distorting the decision that should
be made on current fundamentals.

The reverse failure also occurs: reluctance to sell a position that
has risen well above the purchase price, not because the business
is still attractive but because selling feels like "crystallising"
a success. The investor's reference point (the purchase price) makes
the decision feel different from what it is. If the business is
overvalued at the current price, the correct decision is to sell
regardless of where it was bought.

Anchoring is particularly dangerous in combination with denial:
an investor who is both in denial about thesis deterioration and
anchored to a purchase price has a double incentive to maintain a
position that should be exited. The anchor provides a financial
reason not to sell; the denial provides an analytical reason not
to sell.

## Drift

Drift is the gradual deterioration of a held business that occurs
without triggering a re-evaluation, because no single development
is large enough to force the investor to revisit the thesis. Over
months or years, management changes, competitive encroachment,
pricing pressure, or cultural decay may erode the qualities that
made the original investment attractive. But each individual
development is small enough to be absorbed into the existing
narrative without a formal re-assessment.

The result is that the investor, at some later date, owns a business
that does not resemble the one they analysed and bought. The
investment thesis — which was probably never formally written down,
and certainly has never been compared against current reality —
is still the original one, applied to a business that has since
changed substantially.

Drift is the hardest of the three failure modes to detect because
it operates on the same time horizon as genuine patience. The investor
holding a great business through near-term volatility and the investor
holding a drifted business through structural deterioration may look
identical from the outside. The difference is only visible through
rigorous, scheduled re-evaluation of the thesis against current
evidence — an exercise that the investor in drift tends to avoid.

## The structural difficulty

Sleep notes that these three failure modes are especially dangerous
because they are invisible at the moment they occur. A bad purchase
decision produces an immediate loss; the investor knows something
went wrong. Denial, anchoring, and drift produce a loss only after an
extended period during which the investor believes they are doing the
right thing. By the time the cost becomes visible, the investor has
often held a deteriorated position for years and has an elaborate
narrative explaining why they were right to do so.

The Nomad Letters' approach to the problem is procedural: routinely
and rigorously apply the "kill" test to every position, without
exception. "We try to kill our companies," Sleep writes in the
2012 annual letter, describing the regular practice of attempting
to identify circumstances in which the thesis fails. This is the
opposite of confirmation bias: the explicit search for reasons the
position should be exited. A position that survives the kill test
is held with renewed confidence, not complacency.
