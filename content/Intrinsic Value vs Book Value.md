# Intrinsic Value vs Book Value

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1983 letter, with
reinforcement and extension across 1984, 1985, 1989, 1993, 1994, and most
subsequent letters; the 2018 letter formally retires book value as
Berkshire's headline performance metric.

## Summary

The 1983 letter contains the canonical Buffett distinction between two
measures of corporate worth that beginners and even some professionals
treat as interchangeable. Buffett's compressed formulation:

> Book value tells you what has been put in; intrinsic business value
> estimates what can be taken out.

Book value is an accounting magnitude. It records the capital
historically contributed to the business by owners, plus retained
earnings, less distributions. It is precise, auditable, and almost
always a poor proxy for what the business is worth to its owners.

Intrinsic business value is the discounted value of the cash that can
be taken out of the business over its remaining life. It is imprecise,
unauditable, and in nearly every case the figure that actually
matters. The 1983 letter develops the distinction at length because
many of the analytical errors discussed elsewhere in the Berkshire
corpus — overpaid acquisitions, ill-timed buybacks, misjudged share
issuances — flow from substituting one measure for the other.

The 1989 letter provides the most explicit DCF formulation Buffett ever
gave in the Berkshire corpus: intrinsic value, "with perfect foresight," is
calculated by "taking all future cash flows of a business — in and out —
and discounting them at prevailing interest rates. So valued, all businesses,
from manufacturers of buggy whips to operators of cellular phones, become
economic equals." The phrasing "with perfect foresight" is not ironic —
it is a concession about precision. Buffett is not saying the calculation is
easy or that anyone has perfect foresight. He is saying that the correct
_concept_ is the discounted value of all future cash flows; that the estimation
is necessarily imprecise; and that the imprecision is no reason to use a
worse measure. The owner earnings formula (see [[Owner Earnings]]) is
his attempt to make the cash flow input to this calculation more reliable
than GAAP earnings.

## Why it matters

Most public-company decisions involving the price of a share — buying
it, selling it, issuing more, repurchasing existing — depend on a
view of intrinsic value. Where the decider quietly substitutes book
value because intrinsic value is harder to estimate, the decision is
systematically biased.

Buffett's argument is not that book value is useless. It is that
book value is _one input_ into intrinsic value, useful as a sanity
check and as a long-term track of capital preserved or destroyed,
but useless as a substitute for the larger judgement. The 1985
letter's discussion of the textile-machinery auction — equipment
with a book value of $$866,000 sold for less than the cost of removal
— is the extreme illustration: book value can be substantial when
intrinsic value is zero. The reverse is equally common: businesses
like See's where intrinsic value has compounded far beyond the book
on which the original capital sat.

The reason book value persists as a working proxy despite all this
is that it is _measurable_. Intrinsic value can only be estimated.
Buffett's point — restated repeatedly across the corpus — is that
_"better roughly right than precisely wrong."_ Imprecise estimates
of the right magnitude beat precise measurements of the wrong one.

## Where book value misleads

Three categories of error recur.

The first is _book-value-as-floor_. Some investors treat book value
as a kind of liquidation backstop — the worst-case scenario if the
business fails. The textile case shows this is not generally true.
For commodity businesses with industry-wide overcapacity, the
liquidation value of plant and equipment can be a fraction of book.
Burlington Industries, which Buffett uses as the 1985 case study,
spent $$3 billion of capital expenditure between 1964 and 1985 trying
to remain competitive in textiles, ending up with sales-per-share
purchasing power one-third of what it had been twenty years earlier
in real terms. The capital was on the books; it was not, in any
useful sense, value.

The second is _book-value-as-ceiling_. Some acquirers refuse to pay
above book value on principle, treating the premium as automatic
overpayment. The 1983 Goodwill appendix is the rebuttal: where a
business consistently earns far more than its tangible capital
requires, Goodwill — the gap between purchase price and book — is
not a premium for sentiment, it is the price for a stream of
earnings that does not require additional capital to maintain.
Refusing to pay it is refusing to buy the business that produces
the earnings. See [[Economic Goodwill]] for the standalone treatment.

The third is _book-value-as-management-yardstick_. Many corporate
compensation plans reward earnings growth with no examination of
the capital base required to produce it. The 1985 letter's savings-
account analogy makes the failure mode explicit: if you quadruple
the capital in a savings account at 8%, your earnings quadruple
without any managerial skill being demonstrated, and an option-
holding manager paid on earnings would profit handsomely from the
arithmetic. The intrinsic-value test asks the right question: did
the additional capital earn at least its cost? The book-value test
does not even pose the question.

## Buffett's working approximation

The 1983 letter gives the working test Buffett uses to bridge the two
measures: at Berkshire, _book value is a useful but understated
proxy for intrinsic business value_. The proxy is "useful" because
movements in book value usually move in the same direction as
intrinsic value, and "understated" because Berkshire's important
businesses (See's, the _News_, GEICO, NFM by 1983) had already
generated large quantities of [[Economic Goodwill]] that did not
appear on the balance sheet at full economic value.

The 1984 letter sharpens this further. Buffett notes that for that
year, intrinsic business value and book value increased at about the
same rate, an unusually clean coincidence. He uses the unusual
clarity to introduce the _dollar-retention test_: retained earnings
should produce, over time, at least one dollar of market value per
dollar retained. The test is an intrinsic-value test by another
name — it asks whether the increment of retained capital earned a
return high enough to justify its retention rather than its
distribution. The 1984 letter argues that this test is the only
defensible criterion for dividend policy, and that companies whose
retained earnings have failed it should distribute or buy back
rather than retain.

The 1985 letter contains the most-quoted application of the
distinction: the discussion of the _Washington Post Company_. WPC
was bought in mid-1973 at not more than one-quarter of its
then-per-share intrinsic business value. The market valuation
($$100 million) and the intrinsic-value estimate ($$400–500 million)
were both visible to the same set of investors. The advantage,
Buffett writes, _"was attitude: we had learned from Ben Graham that
the key to successful investing was the purchase of shares in good
businesses when market prices were at a large discount from
underlying business values."_ The case is also a worked example of
the _triple dip_: business value compounded, per-share value
compounded faster because of buybacks at sub-intrinsic prices, and
the discount narrowed so the price gain ran ahead of the per-share
business gain.

## The estimation problem

Buffett does not pretend intrinsic value is easy to compute. The
1983 letter is explicit that _two different estimators almost always
get materially different numbers_ — including, the 1983 letter
admits, Buffett and Munger themselves on occasion. The estimation
problem is real and durable.

The way around it is not to pretend the problem doesn't exist. It is
to demand a _margin of safety_ — buy only when the estimated
intrinsic value is enough above the price that being wrong by a
substantial fraction still leaves the investment sound. The Graham
inheritance is direct: intrinsic value is too imprecise to use as a
fine adjustment, but more than precise enough to say that buying at
20–25% of estimated intrinsic value (the 1973 WPC case) is a
different kind of investment than buying at 95% of estimated
intrinsic value (most equity investing). The first kind survives
estimation error; the second does not.

This is the same posture as criterion 4 of [[Buffett's Four Criteria]] — _very_ attractive prices, not merely fair ones. The
distinction is not asceticism. It is a response to the structural
imprecision of the underlying measure.

## The 1994 formal definition and the Scott Fetzer case

The 1994 letter gives the cleanest formal definition Buffett ever offered:
_"We define intrinsic value as the discounted value of the cash that can
be taken out of a business during its remaining life. Anyone calculating
intrinsic value necessarily comes up with a highly subjective figure that
will change both as estimates of future cash flows are revised and as
interest rates move. Despite its fuzziness, however, intrinsic value is
all-important and is the only logical way to evaluate the relative
attractiveness of investments and businesses."_

The education analogy in the same letter captures the book/intrinsic
divergence with unusual clarity: the "book value" of a college education
is the cost paid, including foregone wages. The intrinsic value is the
discounted present value of the additional earnings the degree will
produce. Some graduates earn far more than their education cost; for some
the investment was poor. _"In all cases, what is clear is that book value
is meaningless as an indicator of intrinsic value."_

The Scott Fetzer case is the most concrete numerical illustration.
Berkshire purchased Scott Fetzer in early 1986 for $$315.2 million when
its book value was $$172.6 million — paying a $$142.6 million premium in
the belief that intrinsic value was close to double book. Over the
following nine years, book value fell from $$172.6 million to $$94 million
as goodwill charges progressively reduced the carrying value on
Berkshire's books. Over the same period, earnings nearly doubled: from
$$40.3 million in 1986 to $$79.3 million in 1994. The carrying value
halved while the economic value grew substantially.

_"Clearly, the intrinsic value of the business has consistently grown,
even though we have just as consistently marked down its carrying value
through purchase-premium charges that reduced Berkshire's earnings and
net worth."_ By 1994, the gap between Scott Fetzer's intrinsic value and
its carrying value on Berkshire's books was "huge." This is the book/
intrinsic divergence in its most legible form: accounting rules and
economic reality moving in opposite directions simultaneously. The
business that produces more cash each year is worth more; the accounting
entry that records what was paid for it shrinks by formula.

## Why book value persists

Despite all the above, book value remains a starting point in
practice — including for Buffett. The 1984 and 1985 letters both
report book-value gains as the headline performance number. The
explanation runs: book value is auditable, comparable across firms,
and over long enough horizons tracks the right magnitude. The
shareholder reading the annual report is being given a number that
is _roughly_ informative and _exactly_ verifiable. The trick is to
remember that the precision is in the wrong place — the figure is
a lower bound on what is actually being asked about, not the
answer to it.

## The formal retirement of book value (2018)

For nearly three decades, the opening paragraph of each Berkshire annual
report featured the percentage change in per-share book value. The 2018
letter ends that practice. Buffett states plainly: _"The annual change in
Berkshire's book value — which makes its farewell appearance on page 2 —
is a metric that has lost the relevance it once had."_

Three circumstances are given. First, Berkshire has _"gradually morphed
from a company whose assets are concentrated in marketable stocks into one
whose major value resides in operating businesses."_ Book value tracks
marketable securities at market prices reasonably well. It does not track
operating businesses well — accounting rules require them to be carried at
amounts far below current economic value, a mismark that has grown
materially as Berkshire's wholly-owned businesses have compounded.

Second, the mismark is asymmetric in a structurally significant way. When
Berkshire owns a marketable security that has lost value, the loss is
reflected in book value. When it owns one that has gained — including
businesses generating large economic goodwill — the gain is systematically
understated. Losers are written down; winners are never written up to full
economic value. Over years this produces a book-value figure that is
increasingly disconnected from what a knowledgeable buyer would pay for
Berkshire as a whole.

Third, the repurchase programme itself corrupts the measure. Berkshire
repurchases shares at prices above book value but below intrinsic value.
Each such repurchase causes per-share intrinsic value to rise — existing
shareholders own a larger fraction of unchanged underlying business value —
while per-share book value falls, because cash (a book-value asset) is
exchanged for retired shares at prices above book. _"That combination causes
the book-value scorecard to become increasingly out of touch with economic
reality."_

Going forward, Buffett states, Berkshire's market price will be the best
long-run measure of business performance. He acknowledges the irony: market
prices can be _"extremely capricious"_ in the short term — the 54-year
performance table in the same letter shows Berkshire shares falling by more
than 37%, 48%, and 50% at various points. Over time, however, price and
intrinsic value converge. The daily price is not the answer; the long-run
trajectory of market price is a better proxy for intrinsic value than the
trajectory of book value once the business has shifted from marketable
securities to owner-operated subsidiaries.

## The five groves framework (2018)

As a practical substitute for book value, the 2018 letter proposes
summing the values of five distinct components of Berkshire — the "five
groves" — and subtracting the deferred tax liability on unrealised gains
in the equity portfolio.

The first and most valuable grove is the collection of wholly-owned
non-insurance businesses. These earned $$16.8 billion after tax in 2018,
and Buffett describes the relevant measure as earnings remaining _after_
all income taxes, interest, management compensation (cash and stock-based),
restructuring costs, depreciation, amortisation, and headquarters
overhead — a figure he explicitly contrasts with the adjusted EBITDA
presentation common elsewhere.

The second grove is the equity portfolio of marketable securities, worth
approximately $$173 billion at year-end 2018, with a deferred tax liability
of roughly $$14.7 billion embedded within it.

The third grove is a set of partly-owned businesses accounted for under the
equity method — principally Kraft Heinz (26.7%), Berkadia and Electric
Transmission Texas (50% each), and Pilot Flying J (38.6%).

The fourth grove is Berkshire's cash and fixed-income holdings: $$112 billion
in Treasury bills and equivalents, plus $$20 billion in other fixed-income,
at year-end 2018. Buffett designates a portion of this as permanently
untouchable — at least $$20 billion is pledged as a permanent liquidity
reserve. See [[Leverage and Liquidity]].

The fifth grove is the insurance float. Float appears as a liability on the
balance sheet, yet has historically acted as free or better-than-free
capital, funding the other four groves. It is structurally different from
the others because its value is conditional — it is worth its face amount
only if underwriting discipline is maintained. See [[Insurance Float]].

The five-groves framework is not a formula for calculating intrinsic value
with precision; it is a decomposition that allows rough estimation of the
parts in aggregate. Buffett's summary: _"At Berkshire, the whole is greater
— considerably greater — than the sum of the parts."_ The combination
enables capital to be allocated across all five groves without tax friction,
eliminates counterparty risk between subsidiaries, and produces funding
advantages unavailable to separately-held businesses.

## Contradictions

The Buffett position is in tension with both the strict-Graham
position (which assigns more weight to book and replacement value
in the framework Buffett inherited) and with the strict-academic
position (which assigns weight to neither, treating market price as
the intrinsic value by definition). The 1985 letter is openly
amused by the academic position — _"what could be more advantageous
in an intellectual contest than to have opponents who have been
taught that thinking is a waste of energy?"_ — but the disagreement
is real and is the foundation of the active-vs-passive investing
contradiction. A future contradiction page on
[[Active vs Passive Investing]] is the natural home for
the academic side of this argument.

The Sleep position in the [[Nomad Partnership Letters]]
is broadly consistent with Buffett's: Sleep also operates on
intrinsic-value estimates and uses [[Destination Analysis]] as his framing of the same exercise. The framings differ
in vocabulary, not substance.
