# Regret Minimization Framework

**Source:** [[Invent and Wander]] — Walter Isaacson's introduction
recounts Bezos's account of the 1994 decision to leave the hedge
fund D. E. Shaw and start Amazon. The framework is one of the most
quoted Bezos passages and is curiously absent from
[[The Bezos Letters]].

## Summary

A mental tool for high-stakes decisions: project yourself forward to
old age — Bezos uses age 80 — and ask, _which choice will leave me
with fewer regrets?_ The reframe replaces a near-term cost-benefit
analysis (which is biased toward the safe option, because the costs
are vivid and the benefits are speculative) with a long-horizon
identity question (which is biased toward the meaningful option,
because regret over inaction tends to outlast regret over failed
attempts).

Bezos's original framing, as quoted in Isaacson:

> I want to have minimized the number of regrets I have. I knew that
> when I was eighty, I was not going to regret having tried this. I
> was not going to regret trying to participate in this thing called
> the internet that I thought was going to be a really big deal. I
> knew that if I failed, I wouldn't regret that, but I knew the one
> thing I might regret is not ever having tried. I knew that that
> would haunt me every day.

## Why it works as a decision tool

Three behavioural mechanisms underneath the framework:

- **Asymmetry of regret.** Regret over inaction tends to be
  durable and growing — it's the path not taken, the hypothetical
  that keeps refining itself. Regret over failed action tends to
  be sharper but more bounded — the failure happened, the lessons
  arrived, the chapter closed.
- **Status-quo bias suppression.** Near-term decision-making
  systematically over-weights known costs (current salary,
  comfortable position, social standing) against speculative
  benefits (a thing that might work). Projecting to age 80
  collapses this asymmetry — at 80, the current salary is
  irrelevant.
- **Identity framing.** The question "what kind of person do I
  want to have been?" produces different answers than "what is
  the EV of this option?" Bezos chose the founding of Amazon at
  a 30% probability of success because the identity question and
  the EV question pointed the same direction; the framework let
  him notice this rather than getting stuck on the EV.

## When it applies and when it doesn't

**Applies:**

- Genuinely irreversible or hard-to-reverse decisions (Type 1
  decisions in the [[Two-Way Doors]] vocabulary).
- Decisions where the personal-meaning dimension is real, not
  rationalised. Founding a company, switching careers, marrying,
  major moves.
- Situations where the _cost of trying and failing_ is bounded
  but the _cost of not trying_ compounds.

**Doesn't apply, or applies less well:**

- Reversible decisions where the answer is to try, observe, and
  adjust. The framework is over-engineered for these.
- Decisions where the asymmetry runs the other way — where the
  cost of trying and failing is genuinely catastrophic
  (financial ruin, reputational destruction, legal consequence).
  Bezos's situation was unusually clean: a 30% chance of
  success, a known fallback (return to finance), a supportive
  partner, parental investment of life savings he believed they
  could absorb. Most decisions don't have this profile.
- Decisions made under emotional pressure where "regret at 80"
  becomes a rationalisation for something the person already
  wanted to do. The framework requires honest reasoning about
  what one would actually regret.

## Connection to Munger's inversion

Munger's standard move is to ask _how would I fail?_ and avoid
that. Bezos's framework is a related inversion: ask _what would I
regret?_ and avoid that. They're not identical — Munger's is
about avoiding folly in the present; Bezos's is about avoiding
the specific folly of _not trying_ when trying was the right
move. Both work by approaching the decision from the failure
side rather than the success side.

The two are complementary. Munger's inversion catches the
"don't do something stupid" failures. Bezos's framework catches
the "don't fail to do something brave" failures.

## Connection to Sleep's frameworks

[[Destination Analysis]] and the regret-minimization framework
are operating on the same temporal asymmetry from different
sides. Sleep is asking _where will the business be in twenty
years?_ and using the answer to evaluate a present-day
investment decision. Bezos was asking _where will I be at age
80?_ and using the answer to evaluate a present-day life
decision. In both cases the long-horizon view collapses
short-term distortions that would otherwise dominate.

## What this isn't

It isn't a license for impulsive decisions framed as
"following one's passion." Bezos's framework, properly applied,
forces honest reasoning about what one would actually regret —
and the honest answer is sometimes "the safer path." The
framework rewards self-knowledge, not pre-commitment to a
particular outcome.

It also isn't an investing framework. Bezos is using it for a
career decision, and the projection to age 80 is about _life
trajectory_, not portfolio strategy. The investing analogue
([[Destination Analysis]], [[Long-Term Thinking]]) operates
on similar temporal logic but with different inputs.
