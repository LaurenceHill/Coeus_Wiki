# Issuance and Buyback Discipline

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1982 letter (issuance
discipline, in the context of acquisition consideration); 1984 letter
(buyback discipline, in the context of GEICO and General Foods
proportionate redemptions and the Washington Post case); reinforced
1985 (Capital Cities/ABC structure, the WPC _triple dip_); 1997 letter,
"A Confession" section (stock-only merger aggregate record); 2007 letter
(Dexter shoes as issuance cautionary tale); 2009 letter (BNSF
stock-for-stock analysis and counterfeit-money argument); 2011 letter
(IBM buyback counter-intuition; long-term holders should prefer low
prices); 2012 letter (repurchase limit raised to 120% of book value;
capital allocation hierarchy); 2016 letter (three-partner arithmetic
example); 2018 letter (repurchase policy extended; price-sensitivity
principle restated); 2021 letter (three paths to per-share value
creation; portfolio repurchases by investees); 2022 letter (demagogue
rebuttal; repurchase at value-accretive prices as benefit to all).

## Summary

A pair of decisions that look symmetric and that Buffett argues
_should_ be governed by the same principle.

When a company issues new shares, it is selling a piece of itself.
The selling discipline mirrors any other selling discipline: do not
sell at a price below the value of what you are selling. The 1982
letter applies this to acquisitions paid for in stock — Berkshire
had often been approached with such proposals and Buffett uses the
1982 letter to make explicit that an acquisition is only attractive
when _the intrinsic business value received is at least equal to the
intrinsic business value given_. Issuing under-priced shares to buy
fairly-priced ones makes the acquirer's shareholders poorer even
when the headline accretion looks attractive.

When a company buys back its own shares, it is the same transaction
inverted: buying a piece of itself. The same discipline applies in
mirror image. The 1984 letter's compressed formulation:

> When companies with outstanding businesses and comfortable
> financial positions find their shares selling far below
> intrinsic value in the marketplace, no alternative action can
> benefit shareholders as surely as repurchases.

Together the two statements form a single rule: _transact in your
own shares only when intrinsic value and price are favourably
aligned, in whichever direction._ Issue when the market is
overvaluing the shares; buy back when the market is undervaluing
them; do nothing in the wide middle band where price and value are
roughly aligned.

## Why it matters

Issuance and buyback decisions are the most consequential capital
allocation choices a manager makes — more consequential than most
acquisitions, because they touch every share rather than a portfolio
weighting. They are also the two decisions where managerial
incentives diverge most sharply from shareholder interests.

The 1985 letter's stock-options argument identifies the structural
source of the conflict. A manager paid in fixed-price options
benefits from share issuance even at sub-intrinsic prices (because
issuance dilutes intrinsic value but the option holder's downside
is limited), and benefits from _not_ repurchasing because
repurchases reduce share count and therefore reduce the option's
notional payoff base under some plan structures. The same manager,
if held to the issue-and-buyback discipline, would forgo personal
gains to achieve the right outcome for shareholders. The
discipline is therefore not natural; it must be imposed by either
ownership stake (Buffett's 47% personal stake at Berkshire as of
1984\) or compensation design (the 1985 letter's recommended
options structure with carrying-cost adjustments and exercise
prices set at intrinsic value rather than market value).

## Buyback discipline in detail

The 1984 letter develops the buyback case in two parts.

The _arithmetic part_ is straightforward: when a company buys back
shares at, say, fifty cents on the dollar of intrinsic value, every
remaining share's claim on that intrinsic value increases. The
remaining shareholders get $2 of value for $1 of cash deployed —
a return better than almost any acquisition will produce. The 1984
letter notes that _"corporate acquisition programs almost never do
as well and, in a discouragingly large number of cases, fail to
get anything close to $1 of value for each $1 expended."_ The
acquisition compared with the buyback is therefore frequently a
worse deal in cash-on-cash terms before the strategic narrative is
even introduced.

The _signalling part_ is more subtle. A management that buys back
when the market is below intrinsic value demonstrates two things at
once: that it understands the difference between price and value,
and that it is willing to act for shareholders rather than for
managerial empire-building. The 1984 letter's compressed point:

> Investors should pay more for a business that is lodged in the
> hands of a manager with demonstrated pro-shareholder leanings
> than for one in the hands of a self-interested manager marching
> to a different drummer.

The signal works only because it is costly. Buying back at a
sub-intrinsic price is straightforwardly the right call but it
shrinks the empire the manager runs. Most managers prefer the
empire. The willingness to defy that preference is what the
_demonstrated_ in _demonstrated pro-shareholder leanings_ refers
to. The 1984 letter explicitly excludes greenmail repurchases
from the endorsement — those serve no one but the extorting
shareholder and the management buying peace at the other
shareholders' expense.

The Washington Post case in the 1985 letter is the worked example.
Kay Graham bought back substantial WPC shares in the mid-1970s at
prices well below intrinsic value. Berkshire owned shares
throughout. The result was the _triple dip_ Buffett describes:
business value compounded; per-share business value compounded
faster than the underlying business value because of the buybacks
(the same total business value spread over fewer shares); and
the discount-to-intrinsic-value narrowed as the market eventually
re-rated the company. Berkshire's $10.6 million 1973 investment
became a yearend-1985 holding worth $221 million including the
proportionate-redemption proceeds. The intrinsic-value
compounding was the largest contributor; the buybacks
significantly amplified it; the re-rating ran ahead in the late
phase. Each layer multiplied the others.

## Issuance discipline in detail

The mirror discipline gets less attention in the corpus because it
shows up mostly in the negative — in deals Berkshire chose _not_ to
do because the consideration would have been issued shares below
intrinsic value. The 1982 letter's framing is direct: _"the issuer
of stock receives, in essence, the same consideration as the buyer
of stock receives."_ The acquirer issuing shares to buy a target is
simultaneously _selling_ shares of the acquirer at the implicit
price. If those shares are worth more than the consideration
received, the acquirer's shareholders are funding the deal at their
own expense even when the deal is reported as accretive.

The 1985 letter's "business wanted" ad addresses this point in
practical terms: _"We prefer to buy for cash, but will consider
issuance of stock when we receive as much in intrinsic business
value as we give. Indeed, following recent advances in the price
of Berkshire stock, transactions involving stock issuance may be
quite feasible."_ The condition shifts with the market price of
Berkshire's shares: feasible when those shares are at or above
intrinsic value, infeasible when they are below it. The discipline
is an iron rule, not an abstraction.

The discipline applies equally to share-issuance for capital-
raising rather than acquisition. The 1985 letter's discussion of
the insurance industry's $3 billion of capital raises in 1985 is
sympathetic — those raises were made at advantageous conditions
because tight insurance pricing supported book values that were
attractive relative to the share-issuance prices. Share-issuance
under different conditions, into a market that is undervaluing
the franchise, is the symmetric error and is what the 1982
discipline is intended to prevent.

## How the two halves fit together

The unified rule reads cleanly: _the issue-or-buy decision should
turn on the relationship between price and intrinsic value, and
nothing else._ Strategic narrative, peer-group practice,
accounting consequences, all rank below the intrinsic-value test.
The 1983 [[Berkshire Business Principles]] manual codifies the
issuance half (principle 10) and the buyback half is implicit in
the 1984 letter's strong statement before being formalised in
later manual revisions. Across the corpus, share transactions in
either direction are described as governed by this single
discipline, and deviations are described — when they occur in
other companies — as departures from it.

The discipline also depends on a _real_ estimate of intrinsic
value being available. The framework collapses if the manager
either cannot estimate intrinsic value, or chooses to estimate it
in a self-serving way. Both failure modes are common enough that
the 1985 letter's language is unusually direct: _"officers and
directors sometimes consult the Tax Code to determine the lowest
prices at which they can, in effect, sell part of the business
to insiders."_ The discipline is a discipline, not a procedure
that runs itself.

## The 1997 confession: a roster of .380 hitters

The 1997 letter contains Buffett's most candid retrospective on the issuance
record: _"If you aggregate all of our stock-only mergers (excluding those we
did with two affiliated companies, Diversified Retailing and Blue Chip
Stamps), you will find that our shareholders are slightly worse off than they
would have been had I not done the transactions. Though it hurts me to say
it, when I've issued stock, I've cost you money."_

The explanation is not that sellers were dishonest or that the acquired
businesses underperformed. The businesses and managers delivered. The
problem is structural: Berkshire's portfolio is a collection of exceptionally
good businesses, and trading fractional ownership in any of them — including
Coke, Gillette, American Express, and the operating subsidiaries — for
something merely good is a step backwards even when the acquisition is
sensibly priced. A baseball analogy: acquiring a player who bats .350 is
a wonderful event _except_ when the team must trade a .380 hitter to make
the deal. _"Because our roster is filled with .380 hitters, we have tried
to pay cash for acquisitions, and here our record has been far better."_

The inference Buffett draws is unusually strong: _"We believe that it is
almost impossible for us to 'trade up' from our present businesses and
managements."_ This is not a statement about price — it is a statement
about the rarity of businesses that would constitute a genuine upgrade.
Where such businesses don't exist (most of the time), the discipline is to
pay cash or not transact at all.

The corollary for any acquirer — not just Berkshire — is precise: paying a
takeover premium makes sense only if (a) the acquirer's stock is overvalued
relative to the target's, or (b) the two businesses will earn more combined
than separately. Most acquirers who issue shares are tacitly conceding point
(a) while invoking point (b). When both are shaky, the voracious issuer is
"running Wall Street's version of a chain-letter scheme."

## True owners versus option holders (2001)

The 2001 letter provides the clearest positive example of what genuine
ownership alignment looks like in practice. When Berkshire acquired MiTek,
55 members of the MiTek management team were invited to buy 10% of the
company, each putting up a minimum of $100,000 in personal cash — many
borrowed to participate.

Buffett notes the contrast with a standard option arrangement in a single
sentence: _"As they would not be if they had options, all of these managers
are true owners. They face the downside of decisions as well as the upside.
They incur a cost of capital. And they can't 'reprice' their stakes: What
they paid is what they live with."_

The option structure fails the symmetry test on all three dimensions. Option
holders face limited downside (the option expires worthless but no further
loss accrues); they incur no cost of capital on their holding (the strike
price is not paid until exercise, if at all); and the "repricing" mechanism
— resetting the strike price when the stock falls — allows them to
participate in a recovery without having borne the full pain of the decline.
True ownership, with real cash at risk and no repricing, produces the
alignment that the issuance discipline is designed to achieve. The MiTek
arrangement is the structural answer to manifestation (3) of the
[[Institutional Imperative]]: managers whose interests are symmetrically
aligned with shareholders do not need to manufacture projections that support
the CEO's craving, because their interests and the CEO's are no longer
structurally divergent.

## The Dexter shoes cautionary tale (2007)

The 2007 letter offers the most expensive personal illustration in the corpus
of what issuing undervalued stock costs. In 1993 Berkshire acquired Dexter
Shoe, paying $433 million in Berkshire stock. The shoe business subsequently
became worthless — killed by foreign competition. But the disaster does not
end there.

The Berkshire shares issued to fund the purchase continued to appreciate after
the deal. By 2007, those shares were worth approximately $3.5 billion — more
than eight times the nominal acquisition price. Buffett's 2007 verdict: _"To
date, Dexter is the worst deal that I've made."_ The loss is not merely the
$433 million spent on a business that went to zero. It is the $3.5 billion
of compounding Berkshire value that was handed to the Dexter sellers and that
left the Berkshire register permanently.

The arithmetic demonstrates why issuance decisions are irreversible in a way
that cash decisions are not. A cash acquisition that fails costs the cash and
the business value. A stock acquisition that fails costs the cash equivalent
_at current prices_ plus all subsequent appreciation forgone on the issued
shares. At a compounder like Berkshire, with decades of future appreciation
ahead, the true cost of any stock issuance is unknowable at the time of the
deal and tends to be far larger than it appears.

## BNSF and the counterfeit-money argument (2009)

The 2009 letter's discussion of the BNSF acquisition contains Buffett's most
systematic treatment of when issuing stock is legitimate and when it is not.

The argument runs in two directions. When the acquirer's stock trades below
intrinsic value, using it as acquisition currency is a mistake: _"you simply
can't exchange an undervalued stock for a fully-valued one without hurting
your shareholders."_ The Company A / Company B illustration makes this
concrete: if both companies' shares trade at $80 against intrinsic value of
$100, and Company A offers 1.25 shares for each Company B share, it is
giving $125 of intrinsic value to receive $100 of intrinsic value. The target's
shareholders end up owning 55.6% of the combined entity despite equal starting
values — a pure transfer from A's shareholders to B's.

When the acquirer's stock trades _above_ intrinsic value, the logic inverts:
_"Using it as a currency works to the acquirer's advantage. That's why bubbles
in various areas of the stock market have invariably led to serial issuances
of stock by sly promoters. Going by the market value of their stock, they can
afford to overpay because they are, in effect, using counterfeit money."_ The
1960s conglomerates built by serial acquisitions at elevated P/E multiples are
the historical example.

For BNSF specifically, Berkshire issued approximately 95,000 shares — 6.1% of
those previously outstanding — for 40% of the $100 per share deal price,
with the remainder in cash. Buffett acknowledges the partial stock issuance
was a compromise: if Berkshire shares traded below intrinsic value (as he
believed), issuing them at all was a cost. The justification was the
opportunity — deploying $22 billion of cash into a business Berkshire
understood well, at a price that was fair without being cheap — was large
enough to accept the partial stock payment as a necessary cost. But: _"If we
had needed to use more stock to make the acquisition, it would in fact have
made no sense. We would have then been giving up more than we were getting."_

The 2009 letter also delivers the most biting summary of investment-banker
incentive misalignment in the context of stock-for-stock deals. In more than
fifty years of board memberships, Buffett had never heard investment bankers
discuss the true cost of what the acquirer was giving in stock-funded deals —
only the value of what was being received. The recommendation: _"Directors
should hire a second advisor to make the case against the proposed acquisition,
with its fee contingent on the deal not going through. Absent this drastic
remedy, our recommendation in respect to the use of advisors remains: 'Don't
ask the barber whether you need a haircut.'"_

## The IBM buyback counter-intuition (2011)

The 2011 letter contains the clearest statement of why long-term shareholders should _want_ a company they own to buy back shares at low prices rather than high ones. Buffett uses IBM as the worked example. If IBM spends $50 billion on buybacks over five years at an average price of $200, it retires 250 million shares and Berkshire's ownership stake in a smaller share count rises. If the price averages $300, IBM retires only 167 million shares and Berkshire's stake grows less. In the fifth year, assuming the same earnings, Berkshire's share would be $100 million higher under the "disappointing" low-price scenario — and eventually worth roughly $1.5 billion more.

The logic generalises: _"If you are going to be a net buyer of stocks in the future, either directly with your own money or indirectly (through your ownership of a company that is repurchasing shares), you are hurt when stocks rise. You benefit when stocks swoon."_ Buffett traces his own conversion to this view to Chapter 8 of Ben Graham's _The Intelligent Investor_: _"Immediately the scales fell from my eyes, and low prices became my friend."_ The emotional resistance — most shareholders take comfort in rising prices — is the specific behavioural error the IBM example is designed to correct. Talking up the stock of a company one owns while that company is conducting buybacks is actively harmful to long-term holders, not helpful.

## Repurchase limit and the 2012 refinement

The 2011 letter announced Berkshire would repurchase its own shares at up to 110% of book value. The brief window of purchases — $67 million before the price rose above the limit — demonstrated the discipline was genuine. The 2012 letter raised the limit to 120% after concluding that 110% was too conservative: intrinsic value exceeded book value by a sufficient margin that purchases up to 120% still represented genuine value creation for continuing shareholders. The 2012 letter also clarified the first law of capital allocation: _"what is smart at one price is dumb at another."_ The limit is not a formula but a judgment anchored to intrinsic value; if intrinsic value were to deteriorate, the limit would need revisiting. And the minimum liquidity threshold — $20 billion in cash equivalents — acts as an absolute constraint: Berkshire will not repurchase shares if doing so would breach this floor.

## The three-partner arithmetic (2016)

The 2016 letter provides the simplest illustration Buffett ever gave of
why repurchase price relative to intrinsic value is the only test that
matters. Three equal partners each own a third of a business worth $3,000
in total — each partner's share is thus worth $1,000.

If one partner wishes to sell and the remaining two agree to buy out the
departing partner's interest at $900 — a 10% discount to intrinsic value —
each staying partner ends up owning half of a $3,000 business after paying
$450. Their position is worth $1,500, up from $1,000. The transaction is a
gift to those who stay.

If the same buyout is done at $1,100 — a 10% premium to intrinsic value —
each staying partner ends up owning half of a $3,000 business after paying
$550. Their position is worth $1,500 but cost them $1,100 combined for the
retained half, a clear loss.

_"The math is not complicated, and the lesson applies to all buybacks: What
is smart at one price is stupid at another."_

The simplicity of the example is its strength. It requires no DCF model, no
earnings multiple, no peer comparison. It reduces the repurchase decision to
a single question: is the price below the value of what we are buying? If
yes, buy back. If not, do nothing. All the complexity in real-world
repurchase analysis flows from the difficulty of estimating intrinsic value
— the arithmetic itself is settled.

## Price-sensitivity and the informed-seller obligation (2018)

The 2018 letter extends the repurchase discipline in two directions. The
first is a restatement of the price-sensitivity principle in its most direct
form: _"Blindly buying an overpriced stock is value-destructive, a fact lost
on many promotional or ever-optimistic CEOs."_ The modifier "blindly" is
important. CEOs who repurchase without regard to price — treating buybacks
as a default use of excess cash regardless of where shares trade relative to
intrinsic value — are destroying value just as surely as acquirers who
overpay for targets.

The second is an obligation to inform. Buffett states plainly that Berkshire
will provide shareholders with the information they need to make their own
intelligent estimate of intrinsic value before the company repurchases from
them. _"We do not want a partner to sell shares back to the company because
he or she has been misled or inadequately informed."_ This is the disclosure
counterpart to the price discipline: repurchasing at a discount to intrinsic
value benefits continuing shareholders, but only if departing shareholders
have had a genuine opportunity to make an informed decision about whether to
sell. Repurchasing from uninformed sellers is a transfer of wealth achieved
through opacity rather than through economic value creation.

The 2018 letter also clarifies that Berkshire prepares no monthly earnings
reports and has no company-wide budget, which means no quarterly "number to
hit." This structural absence of short-term reporting pressure removes a
common incentive for earnings-driven buyback timing — the practice of
announcing repurchase programmes specifically to support stock price in
quarters where reported earnings disappointed — and keeps the discipline
anchored to intrinsic value rather than to the investor-relations calendar.

## Three paths to per-share value creation (2021)

The 2021 letter offers the most explicit taxonomy in the corpus of the
distinct mechanisms by which per-share intrinsic value increases. There
are three paths, not one:

First, organic growth in the business's earning power — higher revenues,
improved margins, expanding competitive position. This is the one most
investors focus on.

Second, Berkshire's own share repurchases at below intrinsic value, which
increase each continuing shareholder's fractional interest in the same
underlying businesses.

Third — and most easily overlooked — repurchases by Berkshire's investees.
The Apple example is the clearest: Berkshire's ownership stake grew from
5.2% to 5.55% during the period without Berkshire spending a dollar on
new shares. Apple's own buyback programme retired Apple shares, and
Berkshire's fractional claim on Apple's future earnings rose automatically.
Berkshire then amplified this on top with its own share repurchases, so that
the net effect on Berkshire shareholders' indirect ownership of Apple's
earnings was compounded twice — once at the Apple level and once at the
Berkshire level.

The 2021 letter's formulation: Berkshire spent $51.7 billion on its own
repurchases over two years, retiring approximately 9% of the shares
previously outstanding. This was not idle capital; it was a direct
investment in Berkshire's own businesses at a price Buffett judged to be
below intrinsic value, producing the same economic effect as buying back
into a collection of businesses — BNSF, BHE, Apple, GEICO, and the rest —
at a modest discount. _"Berkshire will forever be a repurchaser."_

## The demagogue rebuttal (2022)

By 2022, share repurchases had become politically contentious. The 2022
letter contains Buffett's most direct response to the claim that
repurchases harm shareholders or the country.

The argument is simple. When a company buys back shares at a value-
accretive price — below intrinsic value — every continuing shareholder
benefits: their fractional interest in the company's future earnings
increases at no cost to them. The selling shareholder also benefits: they
received the price they were willing to accept. _"Has this transaction
harmed anyone? Is the manager somehow favored over the continuing passive
owners? Has the public been hurt?"_ The answer to all three is no,
provided the price is value-accretive. The only party who loses is the
continuing shareholder when the company repurchases at an _overpriced_
level — which is the case Buffett has always warned against, and which the
price-discipline sections above address.

_"When you are told that all repurchases are harmful to shareholders or
to the country, or particularly beneficial to CEOs, you are listening to
either an economic illiterate or a silver-tongued demagogue (characters
that are not mutually exclusive)."_

The bluntness is unusual for the corpus. It reflects Buffett's view that
the political critique conflates the practice of repurchasing with the
separate question of whether companies repurchase wisely — a legitimate
concern, addressed by insisting on price discipline — and uses the
conflation to condemn the mechanism entirely. His three-partner arithmetic
(see above) demonstrates that the mechanism itself is sound when applied
correctly.

## What this isn't

It isn't a recommendation to buy back at any price below market.
The test is intrinsic value, not market price. A buyback at a
discount to market that is still a premium to intrinsic value
fails the test, even if it pleases short-term traders.

It isn't a recommendation against ever issuing shares. Berkshire
itself issued shares to acquire businesses on multiple occasions
in the corpus — including the Blue Chip merger that produced the
1983 Owner's Manual audience. The test was satisfied each time;
the discipline does not forbid the transaction, only the
unfavourably-priced version.

It also isn't a substitute for owning a good business. The 1985
_triple-dip_ example only worked because the underlying business
(WPC) was a good business in the first place. Buybacks at
sub-intrinsic prices in a business whose intrinsic value is
declining merely accelerate the decline relative to the share
count. The discipline assumes the rest of the [[Berkshire Business Principles]] are operating; it does not substitute for
them.

## Annual meeting additions: stop-loss absurdity, Coke dilution math, put options

[[sources/Buffett and Munger Unscripted]] adds three formulations on
issuance and buyback discipline not in the Chairman's Letters.

**The stop-loss absurdity.** A recurring annual meeting argument against
stop-loss rules as applied to stock positions. If you own a business at
$100 and it falls to $80, the stop-loss rule says sell. But if the business
has not changed — if the fall is Mr. Market being depressed rather than
the business deteriorating — then the rational response is to buy more, not
to sell. The stop-loss rule inverts the correct logic. Buffett's formulation:
imagine if a grocery store raised its prices every time demand increased and
cut them every time demand decreased — it would be considered irrational.
Yet this is exactly what the stop-loss investor does with stocks. The issuance
and buyback discipline is the institutional version of this insight: buy
when cheap, sell when expensive, not the reverse.

**Coca-Cola dilution math: worked through.** At several meetings, Buffett
has walked through the arithmetic of what Coca-Cola's share issuance for
management compensation cost existing shareholders. The exercise: Coke was
issuing shares for executive compensation each year; over a decade, this
amounted to a meaningful percentage of the share count; each additional share
diluted the earnings-per-share and intrinsic-value-per-share of existing
holders. Buffett's objection at Coke board meetings was to the magnitude of
the dilution, not to the concept of equity compensation. The math he presents
makes the cost visible in a way that the accounting statement does not —
because the options were not expensed (see [[concepts/Earnings Integrity]])
and the dilution appeared only in the share count rather than in the income
statement. The worked arithmetic is the practical demonstration of why
the options-expensing battle mattered: the cost was real, but the accounting
made it invisible.

**Put options on Berkshire shares.** Buffett has noted at meetings that
Berkshire has occasionally sold put options on its own stock — an application
of the buyback discipline in derivatives form. If Berkshire believes its
shares are undervalued and would be happy to buy them at a given price, it
can sell a put at that price and collect the premium. If the stock falls to
the strike price, Berkshire buys shares it was already willing to buy. If
the stock stays above the strike, Berkshire keeps the premium. This is a
transaction that only makes sense if management genuinely believes the shares
are worth more than the strike price; and it is only rational for management
to believe this if they are focused on intrinsic value rather than share
price management. The put option strategy is therefore a diagnostic for
the authenticity of the buyback discipline: managers who do this are putting
the corporate balance sheet behind their valuation conviction.

## Contradictions

None internal to the corpus.

The discipline is in tension with the academic-efficient-markets
position, which holds that intrinsic value and market price
_are_ the same thing by definition and that the
issue-or-buyback decision therefore reduces to a coin flip net
of transaction costs. The 1984 letter is explicitly amused by
this position. The disagreement is the same one that shows up in
[[Intrinsic Value vs Book Value]] and will eventually anchor the
[[Active vs Passive Investing]] contradiction page.
