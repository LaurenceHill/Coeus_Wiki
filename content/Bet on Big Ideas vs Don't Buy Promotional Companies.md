# Bet on Big Ideas vs Don't Buy Promotional Companies

**The tension:** Bezos's asymmetric-bet portfolio strategy — take every bet
with a 10% chance of a 100× payoff — appears to endorse exactly the kind
of company Fisher warns investors never to buy: one that is visibly losing
money on speculative ventures in pursuit of a large-but-unproven payoff.
Both authors are addressing business strategy and investor selection. The
conflict is real at the surface. It resolves when you look at the structural
position from which each man is speaking.

## The two positions

**Bezos's position** — stated in the 2015 letter and summarised in
[[Big Winners Pay for Many Experiments]] — is that the return distribution
in business is long-tailed, meaning a small number of very large wins
finance a large number of failed experiments. The strategic implication:
a management team that refuses to tolerate failed experiments is refusing
to take the bets that produce the large wins. AWS, Marketplace, and Prime
are named explicitly as the three pillars whose payoffs financed everything
else. The asymmetric-bet posture is not optional if you want to build an
enduring business at Amazon's scale; it is structural.

**Fisher's position** — stated in *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits*
and the *Five Don'ts* as summarised in [[Five Don'ts and Five More Don'ts]]
— is that the investor should never buy into a promotional company.
Fisher's definition is a company whose current stock price reflects hope for
future breakthroughs rather than demonstrated operating results, and whose
management is more focused on the stock price than on building the
underlying business. Fisher is particularly sceptical of companies that
promote themselves aggressively while producing losses, because the
promotion substitutes for results that haven't materialised.

At the surface, Amazon circa 1999 fails Fisher's test. It was losing money
at scale, its valuation was driven almost entirely by future expectations,
and the large bets (AWS didn't exist yet; Marketplace was embryonic;
Prime was years away) had no demonstrated payoff.

## Where the tension is real

The tension is genuine on the investor-selection question. An investor
applying Fisher's Don'ts in 1999 would likely have declined to buy Amazon.
Fisher's Don't is designed precisely to exclude companies that depend on
future experiments that have not yet proven themselves. Amazon in 1999
was a company where the core book business was profitable at the unit
level but was being diluted by aggressive reinvestment, and the new ventures
were not yet identifiable as the three pillars they would become. Fisher
would have needed to evaluate the *portfolio* of experiments in progress —
not just the current results — and Fisher's framework was not built to price
that kind of optionality.

This is not a trivial objection to Fisher. The difficulty of pricing early-stage
Amazon using Fisher's criteria is evidence that the criteria, however useful,
were built for a somewhat different kind of company: the exceptional
industrial-era business with a profitable core, strong research and
development, and a clear relationship between today's product and
tomorrow's earnings. Fisher's methodology rewards demonstrated excellence
in a going concern; it is less well-adapted to a company still in the process
of building the core that will make the experiments viable.

## Where the tension dissolves

Bezos is not advocating that a company run large speculative bets without
a profitable core to absorb the failures. He is explicit in the 2015 letter
that the strategy works *because* of the three pillars — the large winners
create the cash flow that makes the losing experiments affordable. Without
that cash flow, the losing bets accumulate into ruin. This is Fisher's Don't,
restated from the inside: the promotional company Fisher warns investors
against is precisely the company that runs the asymmetric-bet strategy
without the profitable core that makes it sustainable.

The resolution the [[Big Winners Pay for Many Experiments]] page reaches
is structurally correct: Fisher and Bezos are compatible, but they are
operating at different points in the company's development. Fisher would
not have approved Amazon in 1999. He might have approved Amazon in
2010, when AWS was visible as a third profit pillar, when Marketplace had
demonstrated its flywheel effect, and when Prime had matured enough for
the subscriber economics to be legible. By 2010, Amazon's asymmetric
bets were being financed by demonstrated cash-generating businesses — the
structure Fisher requires.

The investor who applied Fisher's criteria mechanically in 1999 missed a
200× return over the next twenty years. The investor who ignored Fisher's
criteria and bought every promotional company in 1999 mostly lost
everything. The difference is analytical, not principled: Amazon in 1999
had the unit-level economics, the management quality, and the emerging
flywheel that made the speculative bets structurally different from the
companies Fisher's Don't was designed to exclude. Fisher's framework
requires the investor to make that distinction correctly, which is genuinely
hard to do in real time.

## What Bezos's framework requires that Fisher doesn't emphasise

[[Big Winners Pay for Many Experiments]] identifies three structural
preconditions that make the asymmetric-bet strategy work: strong existing
cash flow, bounded individual failures (the bets must be structured as
[[Two-Way Doors]]), and patient capital. Fisher's framework identifies the
first precondition (the profitable core) but not the latter two.

The bounded-failures condition is particularly important. Fisher's Don't
treats the speculative venture as a binary — either the company has it
or it doesn't — without accounting for whether individual bets are
structured to be reversible. Amazon's failed experiments (the Fire Phone
being the most-cited) were bounded: they cost money and embarrassed
management, but they didn't threaten the core business, and the teams and
learnings were redeployable. A company that takes speculative bets that
could destroy the core if they fail is a different animal from one that runs
bounded experiments from a position of financial strength. Fisher's Don't
captures the former; Bezos's strategy describes the latter.

## The diagnostic question

The practical question a Fisher-trained investor must ask when evaluating a
Bezos-style company is not "is it losing money?" but "does the losing-money
reflect bounded experiments being financed by a genuinely excellent core,
or does it reflect speculative promotion without demonstrated underlying
quality?" The first is consistent with Fisher's deeper framework — which
demands management quality, research capability, and long-term orientation
— even if it violates Fisher's Don't interpreted mechanically. The second is
exactly what Fisher is warning against.

The two frameworks converge on this: both authors distrust companies that
prioritise the appearance of a story over the substance of the business.
Fisher distrusts promotional management for the same underlying reason
Bezos distrusts GAAP earnings optimisation — both are instances of
substituting the proxy for the underlying reality.
