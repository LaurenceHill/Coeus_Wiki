# Two-Way Doors

**Source:** [[Invent and Wander]] — 2015 shareholder letter, "Type 1 and
Type 2 Decisions." [[The Bezos Letters]] (Anderson, Chapter 7) provides
additional commentary and the "Disagree and Commit" complement.

## Summary

Bezos divides all decisions into two categories:

**Type 1 decisions** (one-way doors): consequential and irreversible, or
nearly so. If you walk through and don't like what you see, you cannot return
to where you were before. These require _methodical, careful, slow deliberation
with great consultation_.

**Type 2 decisions** (two-way doors): reversible and changeable. If the
decision turns out to be wrong, you can reopen the door and try again. These
should be _made quickly by high-judgment individuals or small groups_.

The diagnostic problem Bezos identifies in the 2015 letter is not the
difficulty of the individual decision categories — it is the systematic
misclassification that afflicts large organisations. As companies grow, they
tend to treat most decisions as Type 1 even when they are Type 2. The result:
_"slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion, failure to experiment sufficiently,
and consequently diminished invention."_

## The mechanism of miscategorisation

The miscategorisation happens through a combination of incentive structure
and loss aversion. A reversible decision that turns out badly still involves
public embarrassment and some cost; the manager who treated it as
irreversible (and therefore deliberated for three months) can at least claim
due diligence. The manager who treated it as reversible (and therefore
decided in a week) cannot claim that defence.

The organisational asymmetry: visible caution is rewarded even when it is
wrong. The absence of caution is penalised even when the decision was right.
Over many iterations, this produces an organisation where managers apply
heavy processes to lightweight decisions because the career risk of doing
otherwise is clear and the efficiency gain is diffuse.

Bezos's response is to make the categorisation explicit. If everyone in the
organisation understands the Type 1/Type 2 distinction, the person applying
a heavyweight process to a clearly reversible decision can be asked directly:
why is this a Type 1? The question alone is often corrective.

## The 70% information rule

Related to the Type 2 case: Bezos's instruction in the 2016 letter is that
most decisions should be made with approximately 70% of the desired
information. Waiting for 90% is, in most cases, being slow — and slowness
has a cost that is just as real as the cost of a bad decision. The asymmetry:
a good course-correction mechanism (which a Type 2 decision permits) makes
the downside of a 70%-information decision bounded; the slowness tax applies
to every decision and compounds.

The 70% rule is not permission for imprecision on Type 1 decisions. Type 1
decisions warrant the full deliberation budget. The rule applies to the much
larger class of decisions that are reversible, where the cost of delay exceeds
the benefit of additional certainty.

## Why this connects to other frameworks

Sleep's [[Denial, Anchoring, Drift]] describes what happens when reversible
decisions become treated as irreversible after the fact: the investor holds a
deteriorated position because selling feels like crystallising a loss. The
position is in fact a Type 2 decision — it can be reversed — but the
anchoring effect makes it feel like a Type 1. Bezos's framework applied to
investing would say: the kill-test ([[Denial, Anchoring, Drift]]) is the
mechanism for re-confirming that a held position is still a two-way door
and that you haven't psychologically converted it into a one-way door.

[[Big Winners Pay for Many Experiments]] depends on Two-Way Doors
structurally: the asymmetric-bet portfolio only works if individual failed
experiments don't cause catastrophic damage. The reason they don't is that
good experiments are designed as two-way doors — limited commitments that
can be abandoned if the initial results don't support continuation. The Fire
Phone was ultimately a two-way door: it failed, but the team and learnings
migrated to Echo and Alexa.

## Relationship to [[Disagree and Commit]]

Two-Way Doors describes the _nature_ of a decision. [[Disagree and Commit]]
describes the _process_ by which decisions are made and executed in the
presence of disagreement. They operate in sequence: first, establish what
kind of decision this is (Type 1 or Type 2), then apply the appropriate
deliberation depth, then — when the decision must be made — commit to it
regardless of residual disagreement. Disagree and Commit prevents the
post-decision second-guessing that would make even a two-way door feel
irreversible.
