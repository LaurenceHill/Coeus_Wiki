# Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits

**Type:** Primary source — investment methodology
**Author:** Philip A. Fisher
**Published:** 1958 (Part One); collected edition with *Conservative Investors
Sleep Well* (1975) and *Developing an Investment Philosophy* (1979)
published by Wiley as *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits and Other
Writings*

## What it is

*Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* is the book in which Fisher set out
the research and evaluation framework he had developed over his first two
decades as an investment counsellor in San Francisco. It arrived in 1958,
sold steadily on word of mouth, and eventually attracted the attention of
Warren Buffett, who called Fisher one of the two thinkers who most
influenced his own approach (the other being Benjamin Graham). The book
introduced two ideas that have since become standard vocabulary in
growth-company analysis: the Fifteen Points checklist and the Scuttlebutt
method.

The Wiley collected edition that constitutes the primary source for this wiki
contains three separate works. Part One is the original *Common Stocks and
Uncommon Profits* (1958). Part Two is *Conservative Investors Sleep Well*
(1975), which refactors the same framework into a four-dimension structure
better suited to the institutional audience Fisher was increasingly writing
for. Part Three is *Developing an Investment Philosophy* (1979), an
autobiographical essay in which Fisher recounts the formative experiences
behind the framework — the bank job in 1928, the Food Machinery
Corporation investment during the Depression, the Texas Instruments and
Motorola purchases in 1955, and the broader lessons about market timing,
in-and-out trading, and the fallacy of the Efficient Market Hypothesis that
he arrived at over fifty years of practice.

Note: this volume does **not** contain *Paths to Wealth Through Common
Stocks* (1960), which is a separate Fisher book. Wiki stubs for
[[Alert Corporate Management]], [[Stocks and Inflation]], and
[[Institutional Buying]] derive from that separate work and will remain
stubs until that source is provided.

## Why it matters

Fisher's framework is structurally different from the Graham value tradition
that dominated investment thinking when the book appeared. Graham's
approach starts from the balance sheet and looks for securities trading below
liquidation value. Fisher's approach starts from qualitative assessment of
management and treats the balance sheet as secondary evidence. Fisher is
not looking for cheap stocks; he is looking for companies that will be vastly
larger in ten or twenty years and holding them through the intervening
volatility.

The framework also places an unusually high weight on research that cannot
be obtained from published sources — conversations with customers,
competitors, suppliers, former employees, and industry specialists. Fisher
calls this the Scuttlebutt method (see [[Scuttlebutt]]) and regards it as the
primary source of genuine informational advantage. Printed records, in his
view, tell you what has happened. The grapevine tells you what is
happening and what is likely to happen next.

The combination of these two emphases — qualitative management
assessment and primary research — anticipates much of what later
practitioners, including Sleep and Bezos, describe as genuine long-term
thinking. Fisher explicitly rejected in-and-out trading, market-timing
strategies, and short-term earnings focus decades before these positions were
mainstream among institutional investors.

## What is in it

### Part One: Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (1958)

The core text. Eleven chapters. The first two chapters set up the problem:
why past financial records are insufficient for evaluating a stock and what
the Scuttlebutt method can do that published analysis cannot. Chapter 3
introduces the [[Fifteen Points]] in full — Fisher's complete checklist for
evaluating a growth company. Chapter 4 applies the framework to the
practical question of how many stocks to hold and how to classify
companies, introducing the A/B/C taxonomy ([[Concentration vs Diversification]]). Chapter 5 covers timing of purchase; Chapter 6 covers
[[When to Sell]] — Fisher's three reasons, stated as exhaustive. Chapter 7
covers [[Dividends]] — Fisher's contrarian position that dividend payments
are usually the wrong choice for investors who do not need current income.
Chapters 8 and 9 are the [[Five Don'ts and Five More Don'ts]], ten
behavioural rules for investors. Chapter 10, "How I Go About Finding a
Growth Stock," is a case-study illustration of the Scuttlebutt method in
practice. Chapter 11 is a brief summary.

### Part Two: Conservative Investors Sleep Well (1975)

Written seventeen years after Part One, in the aftermath of the 1973–74 bear
market. Fisher reformulates his framework as four dimensions of a
conservative investment rather than fifteen points, broadening the audience
from sophisticated individual investors to the institutional audience that had
grown dominant over that period.

The first three dimensions cover the same ground as the Fifteen Points:
(1) low-cost production, effective marketing, leading research, and sound
financial management; (2) people — management depth, integrity, employee
relations, and the discipline required to sacrifice near-term profits for
long-term growth; (3) inherent business characteristics that allow above-
average profitability to persist — scale advantages, proprietary technology,
marketing dominance.

The fourth dimension is the most original contribution of Part Two: the
[[Financial Community Appraisal]]. Fisher's argument is that every
significant price movement in a common stock is caused by a changed
appraisal by the financial community, not by the underlying facts of the
business. The appraisal is a composite of three sub-appraisals — of the
company, of its industry, and of common stocks as a whole. The appraisal
can diverge from reality for months or years in either direction; it
eventually corrects, but the timing of correction is unpredictable.

### Part Three: Developing an Investment Philosophy (1979)

An autobiographical essay commissioned by the Institute of Chartered
Financial Analysts. Fisher walks through the formative experiences that
produced his methodology: the 1928 radio-stock investigation (his first
application of Scuttlebutt), the 1929 crash and subsequent losses (which
taught him that correct forecasting without action produces nothing and that
low price-earnings ratios are not sufficient reason to buy), the Food
Machinery Corporation investment begun in 1931 (his template for an ideal
growth company), and the 1955 purchases of Texas Instruments and
Motorola (held through years of poor market performance before both
became large gains).

Part Three also contains Fisher's most explicit argument against in-and-out
trading, his formulation of the three-year rule (see [[When to Sell]]), his
argument against selling good stocks in anticipation of short-term market
downturns, his case-study refutation of the Efficient Market Hypothesis
using the Raychem Corporation (see [[Financial Community Appraisal]]),
and an eight-point summary of his complete investment philosophy.

## How to read it

Part One is the primary text. The Fifteen Points and the Scuttlebutt method
are set out there in full. Part Two is valuable for the Fourth Dimension
argument, which is underemphasised in the original book. Part Three is the
most readable section and contains the most concrete illustrations; it is also
where Fisher is most explicit about what he got wrong and why.

The Fifteen Points should not be read as a checklist to be scored
mechanically. Fisher is explicit that few companies will rate highly on all
fifteen simultaneously and that the investor must use judgement about which
points matter most for a particular business. The framework is a structured
reminder of what to investigate, not an algorithm.

Kenneth Fisher's preface to the Wiley collected edition summarises his
father's views accurately, with particular emphasis on scuttlebutt and the
qualitative character of the methodology. It is a useful introduction but
does not add material beyond what is in the text.
