# Psychology of Human Misjudgment

**Author:** Charles T. Munger
**Published:** 2005 (revised from memory, age 81). Original speech given
circa 1994–1995. This document is the definitive final version.

**Source type:** Primary — Munger's own writing and speech, revised and
expanded from earlier transcripts.

## What it is

"The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" is Munger's systematic catalogue of
the cognitive tendencies that cause intelligent, decent people to think and
act badly. The 2005 revision — written entirely from memory, without research
assistance, over approximately fifty hours — represents Munger's considered
final word on a subject he developed over several decades.

The speech grew from two starting points. The first was practical: Munger
found, early in his legal and business career, that he was surrounded by
extreme irrationality in patterns and subpatterns, and that the standard
frameworks available to him — economics, law, evolution — were inadequate
to explain what he was observing. The second was strategic: Munger's method
of collecting instances of bad judgement, then pondering ways to avoid such
outcomes, led him naturally toward a systematic catalogue of failure modes.

The influence of Robert Cialdini's _Influence_ was the proximate trigger for
reading psychology formally. Munger sent copies to all his children and gave
Cialdini a share of Berkshire Class A stock in thanks. He then worked through
three introductory psychology textbooks, found them inadequate in critical
ways, and constructed his own synthesis.

## Why it matters

The speech is the intellectual foundation beneath the Berkshire partnership's
way of thinking about human behaviour. Buffett's Institutional Imperative,
his distrust of incentive structures, his preference for managers whose
interests genuinely align with shareholders — all of these draw on this
framework even when they do not cite it. Munger's own investing approach —
concentrated, patient, quality-focused — is partly a design to avoid the
traps the speech catalogues: avoiding the Availability-Misweighing that
produces activity-for-activity's-sake, the Inconsistency-Avoidance that
prevents updating bad positions, the Social-Proof that causes herding.

The speech's deepest insight is its twenty-fifth tendency: the Lollapalooza
Effect. Single psychological tendencies are important but manageable. When
multiple tendencies align in the same direction simultaneously — as they do
in cult conversions, speculative manias, and institutional disasters — the
outcome is extreme and largely unpredictable from examining any one tendency
in isolation. This is the idea absent from every psychology textbook Munger
read, and it is the most important new idea in the speech.

## The 25 tendencies — summary index

1. **Reward and Punishment Superresponse** — incentives are superpowers;
   incentive-caused bias causes decent people to drift into harmful behaviour
   while rationalising it. "Get the incentives right" is the most important
   rule in management.

2. **Liking/Loving** — we ignore faults in what we love, favour things
   associated with it, and distort facts to sustain the feeling.

3. **Disliking/Hating** — the mirror image: we ignore virtues, distort facts,
   and systematically underestimate those we dislike.

4. **Doubt-Avoidance** — the brain rushes to remove uncertainty by reaching
   a decision; triggered especially by puzzlement combined with stress.

5. **Inconsistency-Avoidance** — the brain resists changing existing
   conclusions, habits, and identities. "The human mind works a lot like the
   human egg — when one sperm gets in, there's an automatic shut-off device."
   Darwin's practice of seeking disconfirming evidence is the canonical antidote.

6. **Curiosity** — largely constructive; the foundation of civilisation's
   advances, and an antidote to many other tendencies when cultivated.

7. **Kantian Fairness** — humans expect and apply fair treatment; violations
   trigger reactive hostility.

8. **Envy/Jealousy** — "It is not greed that drives the world, but envy"
   (Buffett). Conspicuously absent from psychology textbooks.

9. **Reciprocation** — automatic and extreme, operating largely at the
   subconscious level. Works for favours and disfavours equally. Partially
   explains the Watergate burglary (Cialdini's analysis).

10. **Influence-from-Mere-Association** — responses conditioned by
    association, not direct reward. Persian Messenger Syndrome (killing the
    bearer of bad news) is a corollary. The Berkshire antidote: "Always tell
    us the bad news promptly. It is only the good news that can wait."

11. **Simple Pain-Avoiding Psychological Denial** — distorting facts until
    they become bearable. Most extreme with love, death, and addiction.

12. **Excessive Self-Regard** — 90% of Swedish drivers think they're above
    average. The endowment effect. Bad hiring from over-weighting face-to-face
    impressions. Antidote: hire by past record, not interview impression.

13. **Overoptimism** — Demosthenes: "What a man wishes, that also will he
    believe." Antidote: trained use of Fermat/Pascal probability mathematics.

14. **Deprival-Superreaction** — losses hurt far more than equivalent gains
    help; even near-misses trigger this. Explains gambling addiction, labour
    disputes, and why investors refuse to add to fallen positions. Buffett's
    antidote for open-outcry auctions: "Don't go."

15. **Social-Proof** — thinking and acting as those around you do; most
    powerful under stress and puzzlement. Corporate boards display the "near
    ultimate form of inaction" through this tendency. Serpico Syndrome
    (social proof plus incentives producing near-total corruption).

16. **Contrast-Misreaction** — the brain registers contrast, not absolute
    values. Real estate broker trick. The boiling-frog dynamic. Ben Franklin:
    "A small leak will sink a great ship."

17. **Stress-Influence** — stress intensifies Social-Proof and other
    tendencies; heavy stress can cause breakdown (Pavlov's last work on dogs).
    Cults exploit isolation and stress to accelerate conversion.

18. **Availability-Misweighing** — the mind overweights what is easily
    available in memory. "An idea or a fact is not worth more merely because
    it is easily available to you." Antidote: checklists, and deliberate
    attention to factors that don't produce easy numbers.

19. **Use-It-or-Lose-It** — all skills atrophy with disuse. Antidote: the
    aircraft-simulator model of continuous practice. Fluency, once achieved,
    decays more slowly and returns faster.

20. **Drug-Misinfluence** — refers to Pain-Avoiding Denial; no extended
    treatment added.

21. **Senescence-Misinfluence** — cognitive decay with age; continuous
    thinking and learning can delay but not prevent it.

22. **Authority-Misinfluence** — man is born to follow leaders; misplaced
    deference causes disasters (Milgram experiment; copilot plane crashes).
    "Be careful whom you appoint to power because a dominant authority figure
    will often be hard to remove."

23. **Twaddle Tendency** — humans prattle and produce noise that damages
    serious work. The confused honeybee dance analogy.

24. **Reason-Respecting** — humans respond strongly to orders accompanied
    by reasons. Carl Braun's five-element rule: Who, What, Where, When, Why.
    The latticework metaphor: _"Learning is most easily assimilated and used
    when, lifelong, people consistently hang their experience on a latticework
    of theory answering the question: Why?"_ Dangerous byproduct: even
    meaningless reasons increase compliance.

25. **Lollapalooza Tendency** — see [[Lollapalooza Effect]]. The most
    original idea in the speech and the one absent from all textbooks Munger
    read.

## How to read it

The speech rewards slow reading with a checklist in hand. Munger's explicit
instruction is that the system works best as a checklist — the McDonnell
Douglas airliner-evacuation case study in the Q\&A section demonstrates this,
walking through the disaster by calling the roll of tendencies and showing
how each contributed.

The Q\&A section at the end is underread relative to the tendency-by-tendency
treatment. It contains: Munger's answer on whether the system is tautological
(yes, somewhat, and that's fine); a real-world multi-tendency case study;
the paradox question (the more widely the system is known, the less effective
the manipulations it describes become); and the economics-psychology
reconciliation question (Colin Camerer's bubble experiment at Caltech).

The document should be read alongside [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] —
particularly the [[Institutional Imperative]] and [[Earnings Integrity]]
pages — as many of Buffett's observations are applications of Munger's
framework without explicit citation.
