# Buffett Partnership Letters

**Author:** Warren E. Buffett  
**Period:** 1957–1969 (letters to partners of various predecessor entities,
consolidated as Buffett Partnership, Ltd. from 1962)  
**Format:** Semi-annual and annual letters to limited partners; occasional
special letters on specific positions or decisions  
**Pages:** approximately 152 (original PDF compilation)  
**Status in wiki:** ingestion complete; all letters 1957–1969 read

## What this source is

Before Berkshire Hathaway, Buffett ran a series of private investment
partnerships that he later consolidated into Buffett Partnership, Ltd.
(BPL). The letters he wrote to his partners across those twelve years are
the most pedagogically complete account of his early investment thinking —
more candid about method, more granular about specific positions, and more
systematic in their framework than the later Berkshire chairman's letters.

The letters serve three roles in the wiki. First, they document the
*cigar-butt era* — the period in which Buffett was operating in the
Graham-and-Dodd tradition of purely quantitative, statistically cheap
investments. Second, they document his *transition* from quantitative to
qualitative assessment, narrated in his own words from the inside, not
reconstructed retrospectively. Third, they contain several conceptual
frameworks — most importantly the Three Investment Categories and the
True Conservatism distinction — that do not appear anywhere in the
Berkshire letters corpus and are not covered by any other source in
this wiki.

## Performance record

The partnership's audited results against the Dow Jones Industrial Average:

| Period | BPL (cumulative) | Dow (cumulative) |
|---|---|---|
| 1957 | +10.4% | −8.4% |
| 1957–1961 | +251.0% | +74.9% |
| 1957–1968 | +2,610.6% | +185.7% |

**Compounded annual rate, 1957–1968:** 31.6% (BPL) vs. 9.1% (Dow)

Across twelve full years, the partnership never had a down year. The Dow
had five losing years over the same period; BPL outperformed in each of
them by substantial margins. The 1969 dissolution letter expects a
breakeven or slightly positive year for 1969, which Buffett describes as
his worst outcome against his own standards.

Buffett's canonical explanation for why the record was possible: the
partnership was sufficiently small in its early years to exploit
statistically cheap positions that institutional money could not touch;
the strategy shifted as capital grew; and the strategy would become
impossible again at even larger scale.

## The key letters

**1956–1961 (early letters, various partnerships):** Introduces the three
investment categories — Generals, Workouts, Controls — with worked examples.
Sanborn Map appears as the first detailed Controls case study.

**1962–1963:** Dempster Mill Manufacturing as the paradigmatic Controls
example. Harry Bottle's turnaround as an illustration of what capital
redeployment from a cigar-butt control can achieve. The "Joys of
Compounding" letter (January 1963) with the Columbus and Mona Lisa
mathematical illustrations.

**1964–1965:** Berkshire Hathaway first mentioned explicitly as a
Controls position (acquired November 1962 at \$7.60/share, average cost
\$14.86). Ground Rules formalised in writing. Ground Rule 7 (the 40%
concentration ceiling) introduced. True conservatism vs. conventionalism
distinction developed.

**1966–1967:** Four-way refinement of the investment categories (splitting
Generals into Private Owner Basis and Relatively Undervalued). The unnamed
dominant position — clearly American Express from the Salad Oil scandal
era — discussed without being identified, producing extraordinary returns.

**October 1967:** The most important single letter for understanding
Buffett's self-described evolution. Four explicit reasons given for reducing
the performance target: (1) fewer quantitative bargains in the current
market; (2) performance hysteria among institutions, making short-termism
rewarded; (3) capital size now too large to exploit many cheap situations;
(4) personal motivation changing. The qualitative-vs-quantitative spectrum
introduced explicitly.

**May 1969:** Formal announcement of dissolution. Three reasons: no good
ideas that meet his standards, capital too large for the quantitative
approach, personal desire to stop. The tone is unusually personal.

**October 1969:** Stock vs. bond analysis. For the first time in his
investing life, Buffett says that tax-free bonds (~6.5% after tax) are
approximately as attractive as professionally managed stocks. Recommends
Bill Ruane (later of Sequoia Fund) as successor advisor with candid
assessment of Ruane's strengths and the three likely negatives in his
future.

**December 1969 and February 1970:** Wind-down logistics. The December
letter contains the first clean public description of Berkshire Hathaway's
operating businesses — textile (acknowledged as unsatisfactory), National
Indemnity (excellent), Illinois National Bank and Trust (excellent) — and
the first statement of the philosophy that will define the Berkshire era:
*"I think about them as businesses, not 'stocks', and if the business does
all right over the long term, so will the stock."*

## What makes this source unusual

Two features of these letters that do not appear elsewhere in the corpus:

**Specificity about method.** The Berkshire letters rarely name individual
securities or discuss the analytical process at position level. The
partnership letters regularly describe individual holdings — Dempster Mill,
Sanborn Map, the unnamed large Generals position — with enough detail to
reconstruct the investment thesis. This makes them the best record of how
Buffett actually thought about businesses in the 1957–1969 period.

**Self-narrated evolution.** Buffett does not narrate his transition from
Graham to quality-at-fair-prices in the Berkshire letters; that story is
reconstructed retrospectively in the 1989 letter. The partnership letters
contain the transition happening in real time: the 1967 letter is the
first document in which Buffett explicitly describes moving along the
qualitative-quantitative spectrum and names the reasons for the shift.
The December 1969 letter is the first explicit statement of the
"businesses not stocks" philosophy that undergirds the entire Berkshire era.

## Relationship to other sources in the wiki

The partnership letters are the *earliest* layer of the Buffett corpus — the
foundation on which the Berkshire chairman's letters (1977 onward) were
built. The partnership era's three investment categories give way to
Berkshire's simpler (and later largely implicit) framework of buying
wonderful companies at fair prices. The cigar-butt methodology of the
early letters is the target of the 1989 letter's "Mistakes of the First
Twenty-five Years" critique.

Several concepts from later sources have their origin here:

- The Mr. Market discipline is present in practice (holding through price
  dislocations, refusing to treat price as evidence about business quality)
  before Graham's parable is cited by name in the 1987 Berkshire letter.
- The concentration argument appears explicitly as Ground Rule 7 before it
  is restated in the 1978 and 1984 Berkshire letters.
- The patience-vs-institutional-pressure tension is described in the 1967
  letter (performance hysteria) before it is fully formalised as the
  Institutional Imperative in the 1989 Berkshire letter.
