# Poor Charlie's Almanack

**Full title:** _Poor Charlie's Almanack: The Wit and Wisdom of Charles T. Munger_
**Editor:** Peter D. Kaufman
**First published:** 2005; revised and expanded editions through 2023
**Character:** Primary source — Munger's own words across eleven talks delivered
1986–2007, plus biographical essays, Mungerisms, and Q\&A sessions

## Summary

The fullest statement of the Munger intellectual framework outside the
[[sources/Psychology of Human Misjudgment]] speech that forms its eleventh
and final talk. Where the Psychology speech is a systematic catalogue of 25
tendencies, the Almanack is the working demonstration of how those tendencies
and a multidisciplinary worldview interact to produce what Munger calls
"elementary worldly wisdom."

The book's central claim — stated explicitly in Talk 2 and repeated with
variation through every subsequent talk — is that the most powerful thinking
tool available to any person is a [[concepts/Latticework of Mental Models]]: a
structured set of big ideas drawn from many disciplines, maintained in fluency,
and applied systematically as a checklist. This claim is not made abstractly. It
is demonstrated on live problems: the USC commencement case study works
through microeconomics and psychology to explain Wal-Mart; the Coca-Cola
thought experiment (Talk 4) uses Pavlovian conditioning, social proof, and
scale economics to derive a $2 trillion business plan from first principles.

## The eleven talks

**Talk 1 — Harvard School Commencement (1986)**
The inversion talk: delivered to a graduating law school class, Munger inverts
the question "how do I succeed?" into "how do I guarantee failure?" and
catalogues the reliable prescriptions for misery: ingratitude, resentment,
unreliability, learning nothing from others, giving up after first failure.
The argument is that avoiding folly is more tractable than chasing brilliance.

**Talk 2 — "A Lesson on Elementary Worldly Wisdom" (USC Marshall, 1994)**
The central talk and the first full statement of the latticework framework.
Covers: the 80–90 models argument; mathematics and compound interest;
accounting and its limitations; psychology and cognitive shortcuts; the
two-track analysis (rational + subconscious); microeconomics as ecosystem;
[[concepts/Advantages of Scale]] (geometry, advertising, informational, social
proof, purchasing); Wal-Mart vs. Sears; airlines vs. cereals; competitive
destruction and "surfing"; [[Circle of Competence]]; the pari-mutuel system
as a market analogy; the 20-slot punch card; the Federal Express incentives
example; the transition from Graham methodology to quality businesses.

**Talk 3 — "Worldly Wisdom Revisited" (Stanford Law, 1996)**
Extension of Talk 2 with additions on: the bridge analogy for mental models
(a limited number of techniques that must interact); ideology as cognitive
distorter (Chomsky example; Warren Buffett's father); psychology textbook
failures (denial, envy, incentive-caused bias not adequately treated); the
Milgram experiment as a lollapalooza case; Captain Cook's sauerkraut as
applied psychology; the tax deferral arithmetic (3.5 percentage points per
annum advantage from holding vs. trading).

**Talk 4 — "Practical Thought About Practical Thought?" (Informal, 1996)**
The five problem-solving notions: (1) decide big "no-brainer" questions first;
(2) numerical fluency; (3) [[concepts/Inversion]]; (4) multidisciplinary
elementary wisdom; (5) lollapalooza effects. Then the Coca-Cola thought
experiment: starting with $2 million in Atlanta in 1884, demonstrate how
combining Pavlovian conditioning, operant conditioning, social proof, and
scale economics would produce a $2 trillion business by 2034. The
demonstration simultaneously shows how psychology explains Coke's success
and how academia fails to explain it.

**Talk 5 — "The Need for More Multidisciplinary Skills" (Harvard Law, 1998)**
The educational critique: why professional education fails by being too
unidisciplinary. The pilot training model as the standard every serious
education should meet: wide formal coverage, practice-based fluency (not
test-passing), forward and reverse thinking, training time allocated by
importance, mandatory checklists, simulator maintenance of skills. "Bonkers"
is Munger's word for an educational system that doesn't meet this standard.

**Talk 6 — "Investment Practices of Leading Charitable Foundations"**
**(Foundation Financial Officers Group, 1998)**
The case for concentrated, low-turnover investment by foundations: the
Berkshire model vs. the Bernie Cornfeld "fund of funds" model. The
mathematics of croupier take. The Woodruff Foundation's 90% concentration
in Coca-Cola as the historical counterexample to forced diversification. The
most direct statement of Munger's investment views for institutions.

**Talk 7 — "Philanthropy Roundtable" (Pasadena, 2000)**
The febezzlement talk: Munger's extension of Galbraith's "bezzle" concept.
Investment management fees create a wealth effect without creating value —
"the functional equivalent of embezzlement." Three percent of assets in annual
frictional costs leaves foundations in a permanent shrinking mode if gross
equity returns regress to historical norms.

**Talk 8 — "The Great Financial Scandal of 2003" (written 2000)**
A morality play in fictional form: Quant Technical Corporation and the
"dollop by dollop system" of substituting stock option exercises for cash
bonuses to manufacture earnings growth. Written three years before the
actual wave of accounting scandals. The argument: permitting false accounting
for stock options is morally equivalent to leaving steel out of concrete in
high-rise construction — the harm is foreseeable, the practitioners know it,
and the accounting profession that authorised it belongs in the lowest circle
of Dante's hell alongside traitors.

**Talk 9 — "Academic Economics: Strengths and Faults"**
**(UC Santa Barbara, 2003)**
Nine defects in academic economics: (1) fatal unconnectedness / man-with-a-
hammer; (2) failure to follow the full-attribution ethos of hard science;
(3) physics envy causing false precision; (4) too much macro, not enough
micro; (5) too little synthesis; (6) extreme psychological ignorance; (7) too
little attention to second- and higher-order effects (Medicare cost forecast
off by 1,000%); (8) not enough attention to febezzlement; (9) not enough
attention to virtue and vice effects. The slot machine near-miss example as
demonstration of psychological ignorance in economics.

**Talk 10 — USC Gould School of Law Commencement (2007)**
Munger's personal life framework: (1) deserve what you want; (2) admiration-
based love; (3) acquisition of wisdom as moral duty; (4) lifetime learning as
the mechanism; (5) [[concepts/Inversion]] applied to life ("what will reliably
fail?"); (6) avoid heavy ideology; (7) the iron prescription — you are not
entitled to an opinion until you can state the opposition's case better than
they can; (8) eliminate self-serving bias; (9) avoid perverse incentives;
(10) work under people you admire.

**Talk 11 — "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" (revised 2005)**
Already ingested separately as [[sources/Psychology of Human Misjudgment]].
The 25 tendencies; the checklist use of tendencies; antidotes; lollapalooza
effects. The Almanack version is the definitive 2005 revision.

## The argument of the book, stated plainly

Munger's fundamental claim is that most educated people operate with far too
few mental models drawn from far too few disciplines. They have a hammer
and spend their lives seeing nails. The remedy is not more specialisation but
more breadth, deliberately maintained in a usable, checklist-accessible form.

The book demonstrates — rather than merely asserts — this claim. The Coca-
Cola thought experiment in Talk 4 is the clearest proof: a problem that "most
Ph.D. educators, even psychology professors and business school deans,
would not have answered correctly" is solved readily using nothing more than
freshman course material from multiple disciplines applied in combination.

The educational implication Munger draws is severe. He quotes Samuel
Johnson: maintenance of easily removable ignorance by a responsible person
is "treachery." This is not eccentric. It is the same standard that pilot
training applies to flying and that medical schools apply to surgery. The soft
sciences tolerate far lower standards, and the cost — in bad decisions,
financial disasters, and missed opportunities — is enormous.

## Relationship to other sources in this wiki

- [[sources/Psychology of Human Misjudgment]] — Talk 11; the psychological
  system that underpins the whole framework; the checklist of tendencies is
  the most important single tool in the latticework
- [[Nomad Partnership Letters]] — Sleep arrived at a similar emphasis on
  multidisciplinary thinking through entirely independent means; his
  [[Scale Economics Shared]] is the investor's version of Munger's
  [[concepts/Advantages of Scale]]
- [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — the applied demonstrations of Munger's
  framework in practice; every Buffett letter implicitly uses the latticework
- [[Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits]] — Fisher's [[Scuttlebutt]] method
  is a specific information-gathering application of the multidisciplinary
  approach
- [[sources/Buffett Partnership Letters]] — the cigar-butt era that Munger
  helped move Buffett away from; the Graham-to-quality transition narrated
  from Munger's perspective in Talk 2

## Key quotations

_"You need a latticework of mental models in your head. And you hang your
actual experience and your vicarious experience (that you get from reading
and so forth) on this latticework of powerful models. And, with that system,
things gradually get to fit together in a way that enhances cognition."_
— Talk 2 (USC, 1994)

_"It is not enough to think problems through forward. You must also think in
reverse, much like the rustic who wanted to know where he was going to die
so that he'd never go there."_
— Talk 4 (1996)

_"I could improve your ultimate financial welfare by giving you a ticket with
only twenty slots in it so that every investment decision you make will punch
a hole in the card."_
— Talk 2 (USC, 1994)

_"We don't leap seven-foot fences. Instead, we look for one-foot fences with
big rewards on the other side."_
— Talk 3 (Stanford Law, 1996)

_"You're much more likely to do well if you start out to do something feasible
instead of something that isn't feasible."_
— Talk 2 (USC, 1994)

_"I am not entitled to have an opinion unless I can state the arguments against
my position better than the people who are in opposition."_
— Talk 10 (USC Law, 2007)
