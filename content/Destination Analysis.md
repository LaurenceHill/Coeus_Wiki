# Destination Analysis

**Source:** [[Nomad Partnership Letters]] — 2005 letter; reinforced
across 2007, 2009, and later letters.

## Summary

Destination Analysis is Sleep's framing that the terminal state of
a business — what it looks like when it has reached a large and stable
form — matters more to valuation than the path by which it gets there.
The argument is a direct response to the limitations of conventional
discounted cash flow analysis, which requires precise near-term
earnings estimates and is sensitive to assumptions about discount rates
and growth rates over the first five years.

Sleep's observation: for a business with the structural properties of
[[Scale Economics Shared]] — where size reinforces the competitive
advantage, and the competitive advantage drives further size — the
specific quarterly sequence of earnings on the way to the terminal
state is largely irrelevant to intrinsic value. What matters is whether
the business reaches the destination, not exactly when or by which
route.

The phrase Sleep uses is: "the destination is more important than
the route."

## Why it matters

The insight reframes what kind of work an investor should do. Under
conventional DCF logic, an investor must estimate earnings for the
next five to ten years with meaningful precision, because the model is
sensitive to those near-term assumptions. Under Destination Analysis,
the investor's energy should go to two different questions: first,
is the destination genuinely attractive (what does the business look
like when it is large and mature)?; and second, is there a plausible
path from here to there (does the moat hold under competitive
pressure, management scrutiny, and capital market conditions)?

If the answer to both questions is yes with high confidence, the
route-dependent noise — quarterly earnings misses, short-term margin
compression from investment spending, lumpy capital expenditures —
becomes less material to the investment decision. An investor using
Destination Analysis can tolerate this noise without being forced to
re-evaluate the thesis on each quarterly result.

This is not an argument for ignoring near-term financial performance.
Significant deterioration in near-term economics can itself be evidence
that the path to the destination has been compromised. The point is
about where analytical effort should be concentrated: on the
structural durability of the destination, not on the precision of
the path.

## The Amazon illustration

Amazon is the most-cited example in the letters. Sleep and Zakaria
began building a position in Amazon in the early 2000s when the
company's near-term earnings were inconsistent and sometimes negative.
Under conventional analysis, Amazon was difficult to value: its
investment spending was heavy, its reported earnings were depressed,
and the near-term cash flow numbers were not reassuring.

Under Destination Analysis, the question was different. Amazon was
building infrastructure (fulfilment centres, data centres, third-party
merchant infrastructure) that would only generate returns at scale.
The destination — an internet retailer and cloud infrastructure provider
with structural cost advantages over any challenger and a customer base
whose habits had been built around Amazon's products — was
exceptionally attractive. The path to that destination was uncertain
in its timing but plausible in its structure, because the [[Scale Economics Shared]] model was working: lower prices were attracting more
customers, more customers were funding more infrastructure, more
infrastructure was enabling lower prices.

Sleep's point is that an investor who needed to model Amazon's next
three years of earnings precisely in order to buy it would likely
never have bought it — or would have sold it during periods of heavy
investment that depressed near-term earnings. The Destination Analysis
investor could hold through those periods because the evidence bearing
on the destination (customer growth, Prime retention, third-party
merchant growth) was positive even when near-term earnings were not.

## The Wal-Mart chart

The 2009 letter reproduces a chart showing Wal-Mart's share price
relative to its earnings per share from the early 1970s through 2009.
The chart shows that Wal-Mart traded at an apparent discount to its
terminal intrinsic value for most of the thirty years following its
public listing. Investors who analysed Wal-Mart in the mid-1970s
using conventional near-term metrics would have found a cheap regional
retailer. Those who used Destination Analysis would have found a
business on the way to becoming the world's largest retailer, using a
model — everyday low prices, volume purchasing, efficient logistics —
that was already proving its durability.

The chart's lesson is not simply that Wal-Mart was a good long-term
investment (which is obvious with hindsight). It is that the business
was persistently undervalued for an extended period because most
investors' analytical frameworks were not designed to capture the
terminal-state value. Near-term metrics said "cheap regional retailer";
Destination Analysis said "future dominant general merchandiser sharing
scale advantages with customers."

## What Destination Analysis does not do

Destination Analysis is not an excuse for ignoring valuation. A
business with an attractive destination can still be purchased at a
price that forecloses adequate returns. The framework shifts the
analytical work from near-term earnings precision to destination
quality and path plausibility; it does not remove the need to pay
a sensible price relative to the destination value.

The framework also does not eliminate the risk of being wrong about
the destination itself. A business can have a genuinely attractive
terminal state that it fails to reach — because competition destroys
the moat, because management makes poor capital allocation decisions,
because regulatory change eliminates the advantage, or because
the industry structure changes in ways that alter the destination.
Destination Analysis improves the quality of the analytical frame; it
does not remove uncertainty.

## Relationship to conventional valuation

Destination Analysis and DCF are not mutually exclusive. An investor
can construct a DCF built around a terminal value that is explicitly
tied to the destination state, rather than around precise near-term
earnings estimates. Sleep is not arguing against quantitative valuation;
he is arguing against frameworks in which the near-term path determines
most of the calculated value. For long-duration compounders, most of
the intrinsic value lies in the distant future; a framework that
weights the near term heavily will systematically undervalue them.

## Thiel's parallel: last mover advantage

Thiel arrives at almost precisely Sleep's destination logic from the
operator's side. In *Zero to One*, the argument is that first-mover advantage
is a tactic, not a goal: *"What really matters is generating cash flows far
into the future."* The goal is therefore to be the *last* mover — to make
the final great improvement in a market and enjoy durable profits from that
position. Thiel quotes Capablanca: *"To succeed, you must study the endgame
before everything else."* Sleep's formulation — "the destination is more
important than the route" — is the investor-side translation of the same
principle.

The practical consequence for valuation is also identical. A technology
company's value lies primarily in cash flows expected ten to fifteen years in
the future; a startup that optimises for near-term growth metrics while
ignoring durability is optimising for the wrong variable. This maps directly
onto Sleep's observation that conventional DCF analysis is systematically
wrong for long-duration compounders because it weights the near-term path too
heavily and the terminal state too lightly. Thiel's "durability" question —
the sixth of his seven questions for any business plan — is the founder's
version of the test Sleep applies from the outside.
