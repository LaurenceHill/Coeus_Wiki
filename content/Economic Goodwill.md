# Economic Goodwill

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1983 letter, the
*Goodwill and its Amortization* appendix; reinforced and applied in
the 1984 dividend-policy section, the 1985 three-businesses
discussion, and many subsequent letters.

## Summary

The 1983 appendix is one of the most carefully argued pieces of
writing in the Berkshire corpus. Buffett spends roughly a dozen
pages drawing a sharp distinction between two things that share a
name and almost nothing else.

*Accounting Goodwill* is the difference between the price paid for a
business and the fair value of its identifiable net assets. It is
required by accounting convention to be carried on the balance sheet
and amortised against earnings over a stated period (40 years, in
the 1983 rules; the rule has changed since but the conceptual point
has not).

*Economic Goodwill* is the present value of the stream of future
earnings that a business generates *in excess of* what would be
required by the capital it employs. It is invisible to the balance
sheet, often growing while accounting Goodwill is shrinking, and is
the substance of what makes a great business great.

The two diverge in opposite directions. Accounting Goodwill is
mechanical: it amortises down to zero on a fixed schedule. Economic
Goodwill is dynamic: it grows in inflation, in tailwinds, with brand
strengthening, and erodes in commodity competition or technological
change. The 1983 letter argues that when an investor or acquirer
treats the two as the same thing, both the accounting numbers and
the investment decisions are systematically wrong.

## Why it matters

Three consequences follow.

The first is *acquisition pricing*. A buyer who refuses to pay
above the tangible-asset book value of a business is implicitly
treating Economic Goodwill as zero. For See's Candy Shops — which
Buffett uses as the worked example — the tangible-asset value at
acquisition was around $8 million; the price paid was $25 million;
the $17 million Goodwill was, on the books, an accounting hair-shirt
to be amortised against future earnings. Twenty years later, See's
was producing roughly $13 million in pre-tax earnings on essentially
the same tangible-asset base. The Economic Goodwill was many
multiples of the Accounting Goodwill at acquisition, and was
*growing* while the Accounting Goodwill was being mechanically
amortised away. A buyer governed by the accounting framework would
have refused the deal.

The second is *reported-earnings interpretation*. A business with
substantial Economic Goodwill — See's, the *Buffalo News*, NFM by
the late 1970s — produces reported earnings that *understate* the
true earnings power, because amortisation of accounting Goodwill is
charged against earnings even though no economic asset is actually
being consumed. The 1983 appendix recommends pulling Goodwill
amortisation out of operating earnings entirely when judging the
underlying performance of such a business — the practice Buffett
adopts in subsequent annual reports, where Goodwill amortisation is
shown as a separate line item rather than charged against the
specific business that generated it.

The third is *capital allocation under inflation*. Buffett's most
important insight in the 1983 appendix — and the one that ties this
page to [[Inflation Tax]] — is that businesses with substantial
Economic Goodwill have an asymmetric advantage during inflationary
periods. Because their earning power is concentrated in intangibles
(brand, distribution, customer relationship, regulatory franchise)
rather than in tangible plant, the capital required to maintain the
business does not need to be replaced at higher and higher nominal
costs. The earnings can therefore rise broadly in line with prices
while the asset base required to produce those earnings grows
slowly or not at all. The 1983 letter's compressed line:

> Goodwill, in inflation, is the gift that keeps on giving.

The 1985 letter quantifies this for Berkshire's three then-largest
operating businesses (NFM, See's, *News*). Aggregate pre-tax
earnings rose from about $8 million fifteen years earlier to $72
million in 1985 — *a 9× increase* — while invested capital rose by
only about $40 million. The same earnings increase, achieved at
the average American business's $5-of-additional-capital-per-$1-
of-additional-earnings ratio, would have required over $300 million
of additional capital. The gap is Economic Goodwill compounding;
the asymmetry is precisely the one the 1983 appendix described.

## How to recognise Economic Goodwill

The 1983 appendix proposes a working diagnostic. A business has
substantial Economic Goodwill when its earnings persistently exceed
what the tangible capital employed in the business would justify at
ordinary rates. The typical American manufacturer earning, say, 12%
on tangible capital is doing fine but is not particularly Goodwill-
heavy: most of the earnings are explained by the capital base. A
See's earning, say, 25% on tangible capital is producing earnings
that the tangible-capital base alone cannot explain; the excess is
the visible signature of Economic Goodwill.

The diagnostic does not by itself identify *what* the Goodwill is.
That requires looking at the business: the See's case is brand
identity tied to a specific regional consumer experience; the *News*
case is dominant local-market position with the resulting
"bulletin-board" advertiser dependency; the GEICO case (developed
in later letters) is a structural cost advantage from direct-to-
consumer distribution. Different mechanisms, same diagnostic
signature.

The diagnostic also flags the failure mode in reverse. A business
where the tangible-capital base swells without earnings rising in
proportion is the opposite case — the textile case Buffett spends
the 1985 letter dissecting. Burlington Industries committed roughly
$3 billion of capital expenditure between 1964 and 1985 and ended
up, in real terms, with sales-per-share purchasing power one-third
of what it had been. The Goodwill was negative in any meaningful
sense, even though accounting conventions never recognised this.

## The 1983 appendix's larger argument

The appendix is also, less openly, an argument *against* the
prevailing accounting framework. Buffett's quietly devastating
observation is that mechanical amortisation of accounting Goodwill
penalises the very acquisitions that *should* be encouraged
(buying high-quality businesses with substantial Economic
Goodwill at fair prices) and reports them as worse-performing
than acquisitions that should be discouraged (buying capital-
heavy commodity businesses at book, where there is no Goodwill
to amortise but no Economic Goodwill either). The accounting
convention rewards the wrong behaviour.

The 1985 segment-information section reinforces the same
critique: Berkshire reports its segments without Goodwill
amortisation charged against the specific businesses, both
because the accounting practice misleads and because segment
information aggregated with Goodwill would obscure rather than
reveal underlying performance. The annual reports from 1983
forward implement the practice the appendix argues for.

## Where it connects across the wiki

To [[Tailwinds vs Headwinds]]: Economic Goodwill is one of the
operational signatures of being in a tailwind business. The
textile/insurance contrast that opens the 1977 letter is, in the
1983 vocabulary, a contrast between a business with negligible
Economic Goodwill (textiles, where the tangible plant is most of
the value) and a business with substantial Economic Goodwill
(insurance underwriting, where the franchise is the value).

To [[Inflation Tax]]: the inflation argument runs through both
pages but in mirrored direction. The Inflation Tax page describes
the corrosion of real returns for the typical investor in a
typical business; this page describes the asymmetric protection
afforded to investors in businesses where the capital base is
insulated from inflationary capital-replacement.

To [[Look-Through Earnings]]: Goodwill-heavy businesses produce
earnings well in excess of their reinvestment needs. The retained
earnings can therefore be deployed elsewhere by the parent without
starving the underlying business — the structural condition that
makes look-through earnings analysis especially powerful at
Berkshire.

To [[Buffett's Four Criteria]]: criterion 2 (favourable long-term
prospects) is in operational practice mostly a test for Economic
Goodwill. A business without Economic Goodwill is unlikely to have
favourable long-term prospects in the Buffett sense, because its
returns will tend toward the cost of capital under competitive
pressure. The 1985 Burlington case is the canonical illustration.

## What this isn't

It isn't a claim that all intangibles are valuable. Brand spending
that does not produce repeat purchase, marketing that does not
build loyalty, technology investments that competitors can match —
all are intangibles that produce no Economic Goodwill. The 1985
discussion of textile capital expenditure is the warning case:
each individual investment looked rational on a return-on-
investment basis, but the collective effect was that costs fell
industry-wide and prices fell with them, so no individual investor
captured the supposed return. Capital expenditure on a tangible
plant base does not become Economic Goodwill simply by being
called *strategic*.

It also isn't a guarantee of permanence. Economic Goodwill can
erode — the 1985 letter's discussion of See's same-store volume
declines is an early warning shot of exactly this risk in
Berkshire's most-cited Goodwill business. Goodwill needs
maintenance; the maintenance shows up in the operational
discipline of the people running the business, not in the
accounting line.

## Contradictions

None internal to the corpus. The position is in tension with the
strict-Graham book-value-as-floor framework Buffett inherited and
explicitly distanced himself from across the late 1970s and early
1980s; see [[Intrinsic Value vs Book Value]] for the broader
treatment.
