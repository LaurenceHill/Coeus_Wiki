# Long-Term Thinking

**Sources:** [[Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits]] — Fisher's
probabilistic argument against market timing and his explicit rejection
of in-and-out trading; [[Nomad Partnership Letters]] — Sleep's equity
yield curve and inactivity arguments; [[Invent and Wander]] — Bezos's
customer-and-mission framing; implied across [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]];
[[Zero to One]] — Thiel's last-mover advantage and technology-company
valuation horizon.

## Summary

Long-term thinking is one of the few investment dispositions on which
Sleep, Bezos, and Buffett agree without qualification. But they arrive
at the position from different starting points, and the differences
reveal distinct mechanisms — each worth understanding separately.

This is a multi-source concept page. The wiki does not try to collapse
the three framings into one; each makes a distinct claim.

## Fisher's framing: the probability argument

Fisher's case for long-term holding is the most explicitly quantitative of
the three. In Part Three of [[Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits]], he
frames the question of whether to sell a good stock in anticipation of a
market decline as a problem in comparative confidence.

An investor who has done the work — applied the [[Fifteen Points]] and
the [[Scuttlebutt]] method — can identify an excellent company with roughly
90% confidence. Short-term market forecasting, Fisher argues, cannot be
done reliably by anyone with more than about 60% accuracy, regardless of
skill or effort applied. Fisher's formulation: "I do not believe it possible
to play the in and out game and still make the enormous profits that have
accrued again and again to the truly long-term holder of the right stocks."

The mathematics are straightforward: it does not make sense to abandon a
position where one has high confidence (the excellent company) because of
an event about which one has low confidence (the short-term market
direction). Even if the short-term call is right and the market does decline,
the investor who sold now faces the harder problem of deciding when to
buy back — and typically does so too late, either waiting for a decline
larger than materialises or buying back only after the stock has risen above
the sale price.

Fisher also explicitly rejects in-and-out trading based on his own early
experience with California Packing Corporation, where he made three
successful short-term trades and then concluded they were a waste of time
relative to the returns available from holding excellent companies for many
years. His final position, reached by the end of World War II, was to confine
all efforts solely to making major gains over the long run and to never
again attempt short-term trading.

## Sleep's framing: the equity yield curve

Sleep's argument is structural. The investment market is populated
primarily by participants with short time horizons — benchmarked
quarterly against an index, subject to redemption risk, and measured
on trailing twelve-month returns. This creates a permanent
oversupply of competition at short time horizons and a persistent
undersupply of competition at long time horizons. The investor willing
and able to hold for ten years is competing in a far less crowded
market than the investor holding for one year.

This is the equity yield curve argument: just as longer-duration bonds
offer higher yields to compensate investors for holding the uncertainty
of extended time horizons, equities effectively offer higher expected
returns to investors who genuinely extend their holding horizon.
The premium is not from the securities themselves being different;
it is from the investor's willingness to absorb short-term volatility
that forces most participants to transact at inopportune moments.

The practical consequence: the patient investor's edge is not better
stock picking. It is the structural willingness to hold through
periods of apparent underperformance that cause others to sell.
See [[Patience as Competitive Advantage]] and [[Reinvestment Risk]]
for the detailed mechanisms.

## Bezos's framing: customer obsession requires long horizons

Bezos's argument is operational rather than financial. Long-term
thinking is not primarily about investment returns; it is a prerequisite
for making good decisions for customers, because the most valuable
things Amazon could do for customers — building Prime, investing in
fulfilment infrastructure, creating AWS — all required multi-year
investment horizons before generating financial returns.

The 1997 Amazon shareholder letter (the first in *Invent and Wander*)
establishes this as a first principle: "it's all about the long
term." Bezos's framing is that short-term earnings optimisation and
long-term customer value creation are in structural tension, and that
Amazon would consistently choose the long term. This is not a claim
about investor time horizons; it is a claim about management's
orientation.

Bezos also makes a mechanical observation: market leadership creates
more economic value than near-term profitability, because leading
positions in large markets compound at rates that eventually dominate
any near-term margin advantage. The implication is that a company
willing to sacrifice current earnings to build market leadership
is making the correct financial decision, but only visible as correct
over a long time horizon. See [[Destination Analysis]] and
[[Flywheel]].

## Buffett's framing: the cost of conventional short-termism

Buffett's treatment in the Berkshire letters is less systematic than
Sleep's or Bezos's but runs throughout. The consistent theme is that
the institutional pressure toward short-term results — quarterly
reporting, annual benchmarking, analyst coverage focused on the next
quarter — creates systematic misbehaviour in corporate management
and investment management alike.

At the corporate level: managers who optimise for near-term earnings
make capital allocation decisions that sacrifice long-term intrinsic
value. They avoid investment in intangible assets that will not show
up in earnings for years; they pursue acquisitions that inflate
near-term earnings through financial engineering; they buy back shares
at prices that flatter near-term earnings per share at the expense
of long-run book value. The 1984 letter's treatment of institutional
behaviour — the "failing conventionally is the route to go" observation
— makes the incentive structure explicit.

At the investment level: Buffett's own approach, holding positions
through volatility that would force most institutions to sell, is
described as a natural consequence of his alignment with Berkshire
shareholders (47% owners cannot redeem). The alignment removes the
short-term pressure, which allows the long-term position.

## Where the three framings agree

All three reach the same practical conclusion: the investor or manager
willing to operate on genuinely long time horizons has access to
decisions that short-term operators cannot make, and those decisions
tend to produce better long-run outcomes. The disagreement is about
why this is true, not about whether it is.

Sleep's argument: the patient investor earns a structural premium
because they face less competition at long time horizons. Bezos's
argument: long-term management decisions serve customers better, which
eventually translates into superior financial results. Buffett's
argument: short-term institutional pressure systematically distorts
both management and investment decisions in ways that reduce long-run
value.

Each argument is independently valid. The combination suggests that
long-term thinking is advantaged from multiple directions simultaneously:
as an investor (Sleep), as a manager (Bezos), and as an owner of
businesses run by managers (Buffett).

## Thiel's framing: last mover advantage

Thiel's contribution to the long-term thinking argument is structural rather
than financial or operational. In *Zero to One*, the argument is that first-
mover advantage — the standard strategic logic of competitive positioning —
is a tactic, not a goal. What matters is not who enters a market first but
who dominates it last. *"The goal is to be the last mover — to make the final
great improvement in a market and then enjoy years or decades of monopoly
profits."*

The valuation implication Thiel draws is precise: a technology company's
value lies primarily in cash flows expected ten to fifteen years in the
future. A startup that optimises for near-term metrics — growth rate, margin,
revenue run rate — while ignoring whether its position will be defensible in
a decade is optimising for the wrong variable. This is the same observation
Sleep makes from the investor's side: conventional analysis weights near-term
earnings too heavily and systematically undervalues long-duration compounders.

The [[Definite vs Indefinite Optimism]] framework from the same book makes
the psychological counterpart of this argument: indefinite optimism — keeping
options open, accumulating credentials, diversifying into process — is
structurally hostile to the long-term planning that last-mover advantage
requires. To build something durable requires a specific vision of what you
intend to build and the patience to pursue it against short-term headwinds.
Thiel's prescription — design over chance, definite plans over optionality —
is the operator-side equivalent of Sleep's and Bezos's insistence on genuine
time-horizon extension.

## Where there is tension

Fisher's [[When to Sell]] framework identifies three conditions under
which a long-held position should be sold despite the general preference
for holding. Fisher is explicitly a long-term investor, but his
framework is more conditional than Sleep's near-permanent holdings
or Bezos's "long-term" management framing. Fisher's third reason —
the availability of a clearly better opportunity — conflicts most
directly with Sleep's reinvestment risk argument. See
[[Selling vs Holding Forever]] for the full treatment.
