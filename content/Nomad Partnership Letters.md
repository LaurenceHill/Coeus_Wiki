# Nomad Partnership Letters

**Authors:** Nicholas Sleep and Qais Zakaria
**Published:** 2001–2013 (25 letters); postamble written Spring 2021
**Type:** Investor letters from the Nomad Investment Partnership — an
offshore fund managed first under Marathon Asset Management (2001–2006)
and then under Sleep, Zakaria and Company (2006–2013).

## Why this source matters

The Nomad Letters are among the most analytically serious investor
letters written in English. Sleep and Zakaria (Zak) spent thirteen years
articulating, testing, and refining a single investment thesis — that a
small class of businesses earns permanent competitive advantages by
sharing their scale economies with customers rather than extracting them
as margin. Every letter is an iteration on that core idea, tested against
real portfolio decisions, made in public, and preserved in full.

The letters are also unusual in what they are willing to say about the
investment industry: that fund management in aggregate does not add value;
that the management fee should not be a profit centre; that the patient
investor base of a fund is as important to long-run performance as the
investment process itself. Sleep and Zakaria were not trying to build a
franchise. They closed the Partnership when they had done what they
wanted to do.

## Performance record

A dollar invested at inception (September 10th, 2001) had grown to
$$10.21 before fees, or $$4.93 after fees, by the end of 2013. The
annualised pre-fee return over the period was 20.8% per annum versus
6.5% for the MSCI World Index. Net of fees, 18.4% per annum against the
same benchmark. The best calendar year was 2003 (+79.6%); the worst was
2008 (–45.3%, against the index's –40.7%). The Partnership closed in
2014\.

The performance came from a highly concentrated portfolio — typically
seven to ten positions — in businesses Sleep had analysed with
exceptional depth and was prepared to hold for very long periods. The
most important holdings across the fund's life included Costco, Amazon,
AirAsia, Berkshire Hathaway, and ASOS.

## Key concepts introduced chronologically

**2001–2003 (early letters).** Sleep begins with a Graham-influenced
approach: statistically cheap stocks, cigar butts, distressed situations.
The Costco observation arrives early. Sleep notices that Costco's business
model is structurally different from other retailers — it shares its
buying power with members rather than converting it to margin. See
[[Cigar Butt to Compounder]].

**2004.** The Growth vs Value False Dichotomy is named. Sleep argues that
the distinction between "growth" and "value" investors is a category
error — all investing is an attempt to pay less than intrinsic value, and
intrinsic value incorporates future earnings whether you label them
"growth" or not. See [[Growth vs Value False Dichotomy]].

**2005.** The most analytically dense year. The Appendix "Measuring the
Moat" introduces the [[Robustness Ratio]] — a quantitative measure of
moat strength defined as customer savings divided by shareholder
earnings. The [[Scale Economics Shared]] model is named and distinguished
from other scale-benefit businesses. The [[Destination Analysis]]
framing — focus on the terminal state, not the quarterly path — is
developed in the same letter. Sleep also discusses Richard Zeckhauser's
work on "investing in the unknown and unknowable" and the behavioural
advantages of a patient investor base.

**2006.** Sleep and Zakaria leave Marathon to manage the Partnership
independently as Sleep, Zakaria and Company. The operational and
philosophical independence allows them to set the management fee at
cost-reimbursement only — explicitly rejecting the management fee as a
profit source.

**2007.** The scaling laws argument: Sleep uses the metabolic
scaling literature (mouse-to-elephant) to argue that scale produces
non-linear efficiencies. The internet model versus the high-street
model is examined in detail. Amazon is named as the canonical
scale-economics-shared business. Sleep estimates e-commerce at 3.1%
of retail in 2006 and asks where the ceiling is.

**2008–2009.** The financial crisis produces the clearest articulation of
[[Denial, Anchoring, Drift]] — the three insidious psychological mistakes
that keep investors in wrong positions. The portfolio pivots to "near
permanent" holdings; Sleep argues that character in businesses reduces
[[Reinvestment Risk]]. The 2009 letter's Wal-Mart chart — showing that
Wal-Mart traded at an apparent discount to intrinsic value for thirty
years — is the canonical illustration of why great businesses stay cheap
longer than investors expect.

**2010–2011.** The Welsh insurance company letter: Sleep shows that the
same scale-economics-shared model operates in seemingly unrelated
industries. AirAsia examined as the lowest-cost airline globally through
the same branch-by-branch feedback mechanism. The TAM argument: internet
retail growth is constrained by the rate of consumer habit change, not
by physical capacity, which implies that conventional TAM estimates for
e-commerce businesses are systematically too small. See [[Scale Economics Shared]].

**2012–2013.** The information-as-food metaphor: Sleep argues for
prioritising high-shelf-life information (axiomatic truths about business
models) over high-frequency data. Inactivity as a source of value-added:
the decision not to trade is an active decision that accounting systems
don't capture. The paradox of choice (Barry Schwartz) applied to
investing: too many alternatives creates chronic dissatisfaction and
excessive portfolio turnover. Sleep's final full letter.

**2021 postamble.** Written after the wind-down, Sleep explains why the
Partnership closed: regulatory burden, the desire to work without
ongoing justification to a revolving cast of intermediaries, and the
realisation that the portfolio had reached a near-terminal state — a few
superb compounders that didn't need to be sold to fund the purchase of
lesser ideas.

## How to read this source

The letters reward multiple readings, but a first-time reader should
start with the 2005 letter and its Appendix (the Robustness Ratio and
scale-economics framing), then read the 2009 letter (Wal-Mart chart,
near-permanent portfolio, reinvestment risk), then fill in the earlier
and later letters as context. The postamble, being written with
hindsight, is the clearest statement of what the whole enterprise was
about.

Unlike the Bezos letters, which are annual and largely standalone, the
Nomad letters are cumulative: ideas introduced in 2004 are refined in
2005, tested in 2008, and confirmed in 2013. The full collection is what
Sleep called the "magnum opus"; individual letters are better understood
as chapters than as self-contained essays.
