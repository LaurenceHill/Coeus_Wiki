# Flywheel

**Source:** [[The Bezos Letters]] (Anderson, Chapter 6); [[Invent and Wander]]
— referenced across multiple letters from 1997 onward. The concept
originates in Jim Collins's _Good to Great_ (2001); Amazon's specific
flywheel was sketched by Collins in collaboration with Bezos at an Amazon
executive retreat shortly before publication.

## Summary

A flywheel is a self-reinforcing loop of business activities, each element
of which amplifies the next. Collins's original mechanical metaphor: a
flywheel is a heavy wheel that requires great initial effort to set moving,
but once turning, generates its own momentum and becomes progressively
easier to accelerate. In business, the flywheel metaphor describes a system
where the activities that create customer value also generate the commercial
resources needed to expand that customer value further.

Amazon's flywheel, as sketched by Collins and Bezos in approximately 2001
and sustained largely unchanged since, has six spokes, with Growth at the
centre:

1. **Greater selection and convenience**
2. **Customer experience**
3. **Traffic to the website**
4. **Number of sellers (Marketplace)**
5. **Lower cost structure**
6. **Lower prices**

The chain: lower prices lead to more customers. More customers increase
sales volume and attract more third-party sellers to Marketplace. More
sellers expand selection and convenience. Increased traffic and scale allow
Amazon to extract more from fixed costs (fulfilment centres, server
infrastructure), lowering the cost structure. A lower cost structure enables
lower prices. And so the loop continues. Make any element turn faster, and
the entire system accelerates.

## Why the flywheel matters as a decision tool

Bezos uses the flywheel not only as a descriptive framework but as a filter
for investment decisions. From the 2014 letter: _"FBA \[Fulfilled by Amazon]
completes the circle: Marketplace pumps energy into Prime, and Prime pumps
energy into Marketplace."_ The question applied to any potential investment
is: does this turn one or more spokes of the flywheel? If not, it is a
distraction, regardless of its standalone profitability.

This makes the flywheel a discipline against scope drift. A large organisation
with substantial resources can pursue many things. The flywheel provides
the constraint: only pursue things that feed the loop. Amazon's 2017
acquisition of Whole Foods is a worked example. Amazon's CFO's explanation
of the deal returned repeatedly to how the acquisition turned multiple
flywheel spokes simultaneously — more Prime-eligible delivery locations,
lower Whole Foods prices (reducing the "Whole Paycheck" reputation),
additional selection for Amazon Fresh, and Amazon Locker installation
points. An acquisition that turned zero flywheel spokes would have been
inconsistent with the framework.

## The flywheel and the dreamy business test

Bezos identifies a related but distinct filter for _new_ business ventures.
From the 2014 letter, a dreamy business offering has four characteristics:
customers love it; it can grow to very large size; it has strong returns on
capital; and it is durable in time. AWS, Marketplace, and Prime all pass
this test. Physical retail failed it in 2006 — _"we don't know how to do it
with low capital and high returns"_ — but passed it by 2017 once the
Amazon Go technology existed. The flywheel and the dreamy-business test
work together: the flywheel describes the existing system; the dreamy-business
test is applied to candidate additions.

## The flywheel and [[Scale Economics Shared]]

The structural relationship between Bezos's flywheel and Sleep's [[Scale Economics Shared]] is close but not identical. Both describe self-reinforcing
loops in which lower prices attract more customers, who generate the scale
that enables lower prices. Sleep's framework (developed independently,
through the Nomad letters from 2005 onward) is more focused on the moat
implications: a business sharing scale savings with customers builds a
competitive position that is expensive to replicate because the challenger
must simultaneously match the price proposition and achieve the scale
needed to afford it.

Bezos's flywheel is more focused on the growth implications: the same
structure that makes the business defensible also makes it capable of
compounding faster than a system where the components aren't mutually
reinforcing. The [[Robustness Ratio]] (Sleep's customer savings ÷
shareholder earnings) is a quantitative measure of how well a given company
is executing the customer-sharing imperative that sits at the centre of both
frameworks.

The most important empirical case where the two frameworks converge is
Amazon itself. Sleep's Nomad Letters discussed Amazon's scale-economics-
shared structure explicitly, and Nomad held Amazon for most of the fund's
life. Sleep was investing in the flywheel, in Sleep's terminology, even though
Sleep called it something different.

## What the flywheel isn't

The flywheel is not a guarantee of success, and it is not self-activating.
The initial effort to get the flywheel moving is substantial: Amazon lost
money for years before the loop had enough momentum to become self-
sustaining. The flywheel is a description of _what happens when the system
works_, not a method for making it work in the first place.

The flywheel also doesn't capture everything important about a business.
It says nothing about management quality, capital allocation, or the
conditions under which the loop could break. [[Buffett's Four Criteria]]
and [[Tailwinds vs Headwinds]] address the durability of the underlying
customer need and the competitive structure; the flywheel assumes that
durability and asks what the self-reinforcing mechanism looks like given it.
