# Six-Page Narratives

**Source:** [[Invent and Wander]] — 2017 shareholder letter,
"Building a Culture of High Standards."

## Summary

Amazon's organisational practice of replacing PowerPoint
presentations with six-page narrative memos. Meetings begin with
roughly thirty minutes of silent reading — a "study hall" — during
which everyone reads the memo. Discussion follows. Memos are
written without bylines: by tradition, the document is from the
team, not the author.

The practice is deeply tied to Bezos's argument about high
standards being teachable. The 2017 letter uses the writing example
to make a broader point about how standards work in any domain.

## Why Bezos chose this format

Three structural reasons in the 2017 letter:

- **PowerPoint hides incomplete thinking.** Bullet points let an
  author skip over the connective tissue between ideas. A
  narrative paragraph forces the writer to articulate the
  reasoning.
- **PowerPoint creates a presenter-audience asymmetry.** The
  presenter knows what they meant; the audience reads only what
  was on the slide. A memo gives the same information to
  everyone simultaneously.
- **The silent-reading discipline reduces deference.** When
  everyone reads the same text at the same time, status
  hierarchies have less effect on early reactions. The senior
  person doesn't get to set the interpretation before others
  have engaged with the material.

## The teachable-standards argument

The 2017 letter is structured around the question: *can high
standards be taught?* Bezos's answer is yes, but the teaching
operates through three components:

1. **Recognition** of the standard. People can usually recognise
   excellent work in their domain even when they can't yet
   produce it. The football coach doesn't need to throw; they
   need to recognise a great throw.
2. **Realistic scope expectations.** Bezos's specific point
   about memos: the most common reason a memo is bad is that the
   writer expected to write it in a few hours when it actually
   takes a week. Teaching scope is teaching how long the work
   should take to do well.
3. **Skill** — but distributed across the team. Someone has to
   have the writing skill, but it doesn't have to be every
   individual. The collective produces the memo; the individual
   produces a contribution to it.

The implication: organisational excellence comes from teaching
the first two components broadly. The third component takes
care of itself if the first two are in place.

## What "narrative" actually means

The memos are not bullet points expanded into sentences. They
are *narratively structured*: an argument with a thesis,
supporting reasoning, anticipated objections, and a conclusion.
The structure forces:

- **A thesis worth stating.** Bullet points let an author
  imply a thesis without committing to one. A narrative
  forces the writer to state what they actually claim.
- **Coherent reasoning.** The argument has to flow from
  premises to conclusions. Gaps become visible because
  paragraphs require connection.
- **Anticipation of disagreement.** A narrative addressing a
  controversial recommendation has to engage with the
  counterarguments to be persuasive. PowerPoint doesn't
  require this and usually doesn't deliver it.

The format is harder to write than slides — substantially
harder. That difficulty is the point. Bezos: "they're trying
to perfect a handstand in just two weeks, and we're not
coaching them right."

## The press-release-and-FAQ variant

For new product proposals, Amazon's working-backward
([[Working Backward]]) discipline uses a specific narrative
form: the team writes the *press release* and *FAQ* for the
product before building it. The press release describes the
customer experience and value proposition concretely. The FAQ
anticipates the questions customers and reviewers will ask.
Both must be written in plain language that non-engineers can
evaluate.

If the press release reads poorly — vague benefits, unclear
differentiation, jargon — the product idea is probably weak.
The discipline catches bad ideas before engineering effort is
committed.

## Why this is hard to copy

The mechanism Bezos describes works because of the surrounding
culture, not just the format. Three things have to be in place:

- **Senior leaders read the memos seriously.** A culture where
  the leader has skim-read the memo before the meeting starts,
  or where the silent reading is performative, defeats the
  purpose.
- **Disagreement is welcomed in the discussion.** The memo
  format gives everyone the same starting information; the
  discussion has to actually use that. Otherwise the memo
  becomes a status document and the discussion is theater.
- **The author isn't the focal point.** No bylines is
  important. The team's collective ownership of the memo
  removes the dynamic where critique of the document is read
  as critique of the writer.

Companies that adopt the format without these surrounding
conditions get the cosmetic benefits without the underlying
function.

## Connection to Fisher

Fisher's [[Scuttlebutt]] requires the kind of careful synthetic
thinking that narratives demand and bullet points obscure. The
writing discipline at Amazon is internal scuttlebutt — the
team is forced to articulate its understanding well enough to
be evaluated by colleagues who weren't in the room when the
work was done. Both authors are pointing at the same skill:
making implicit reasoning explicit so it can be tested.

## Connection to Sleep

Sleep's letters are themselves examples of narrative discipline
applied to investment communication. The
[[Nomad Partnership Letters]] don't use bullet points. They
build arguments paragraph by paragraph. The 2007 letter on
[[Destination Analysis]] is a particularly clean example —
the framework is developed through narrative reasoning, not
listed as principles. Sleep would have approved of the Amazon
memo discipline.
