# When to Sell

**Source:** [[Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits]] — Chapter 6, "When to
Sell — and When Not To"; Part Three, "Developing an Investment Philosophy"
(the three-year rule)

## Summary

Fisher identifies exactly three legitimate reasons to sell a stock. He states
them as exhaustive: if none of the three applies, selling is almost certainly
a mistake. The framework is deliberately restrictive because Fisher regards
the impulse to sell — triggered by price gains, by market nervousness, or
by short-term news — as one of the most reliable sources of permanent
investor error.

The three reasons are: the original purchase was a mistake and the facts
now confirm it; the company no longer qualifies under the [[Fifteen Points]];
or a clearly superior opportunity exists to which the capital can be
redeployed.

## The three reasons

**1. The original purchase was a mistake.**

When a company was bought, it appeared to qualify on the [[Fifteen Points]].
Subsequent investigation — through the [[Scuttlebutt]] network, through
direct observation of the company's behaviour, or through analysis of
results — reveals that the original assessment was wrong. The management
is weaker than it appeared, the competitive position is not as strong, the
research pipeline is thinner. This is a legitimate reason to sell. Fisher is
not sentimental about past decisions: if the investment thesis was wrong,
the position should be exited without waiting for price recovery. Holding a
mistake out of reluctance to crystallise a loss is not discipline; it is the
avoidance of accountability.

Fisher's nuance here is important: the new information must specifically
contradict the original investment thesis. A company that is having a
difficult year because of a cyclical downturn, or that has experienced a
product setback in one area while the rest of the business remains sound,
does not necessarily fail to qualify on the Fifteen Points. The question is
whether the underlying management quality, competitive position, and
growth prospects that motivated the original purchase are still intact.

**2. The company no longer qualifies under the Fifteen Points.**

Businesses change. A company that fully justified purchase five years ago
may have changed in ways that no longer make it an outstanding holding.
Management that was excellent may have deteriorated through retirements,
complacency, or the failure to adapt management style as the company
grew. A competitive position that was strong may have been eroded by new
technology or better-funded competitors. A research pipeline that was
compelling may have dried up.

Fisher distinguishes this from temporary setbacks carefully. His example
from Part Three: when one of his major electronics investments ran into
management problems in the early 1960s, he made a thorough investigation,
concluded that the management deficiency was genuine and unlikely to be
remedied, and sold — even though the position was down from its peak.
The sale was not triggered by the decline in price but by the deterioration in
management quality. In contrast, he held Texas Instruments through an 80%
decline from its 1962 peak because his investigation concluded that the
underlying company remained excellent and the decline was caused by
excessive valuation rather than business deterioration.

**3. A clearly superior opportunity exists.**

If an investor has thoroughly investigated a new situation and found it
materially more attractive than the least attractive holding in her current
portfolio — after accounting for taxes and transaction costs — it may be
rational to sell the weaker position and fund the stronger one. Fisher sets
a high bar: the new opportunity must be clearly and substantially superior,
not merely marginally better. The bar is higher because the investor already
has established positions in companies she understands well; switching into
a new company means starting the research cycle again and accepting
uncertainty that does not exist in the current holding.

This is the rarest legitimate trigger. In practice, Fisher's own portfolio
turned over very slowly. Most of his major positions were held for ten to
twenty years or longer. The third reason is not a licence for frequent
switching; it is a recognition that occasionally a genuinely exceptional
opportunity will make the weakest current holding worth exiting.

## What are not legitimate reasons to sell

Fisher's Chapter 6 spends more space on the invalid reasons than the valid
ones. The most common are:

**Price appreciation.** A stock that has risen significantly is not more
likely to fall than one that has been flat. If the underlying business remains
excellent and the price has risen because the business has grown, the correct
response is to hold, not to take profits. Fisher's formulation: "A profit
should never be taken just for the satisfaction of taking it." The investor
who sells Texas Instruments after it has doubled, on the grounds that it
"looks expensive," and then cannot bring herself to buy it back when it
continues rising, has permanently capped her gains from that position.

**Fear of a market decline.** Fisher's most sustained argument against a
common behaviour: investors should not sell excellent holdings in
anticipation of a general market downturn. The reasoning is probabilistic.
An investor can identify an excellent company with roughly 90% confidence
using thorough analysis. She cannot identify a short-term market top with
more than about 60% confidence regardless of how skilled she is. It does
not make sense to abandon a position where she has high confidence
because of an event about which she has low confidence. Fisher also notes
that investors who sell to wait for a better re-entry point almost never buy
back: they wait for a decline bigger than materialises, watch the stock
recover without them, and miss the subsequent gains entirely.

**Short-term bad news.** A single bad year, a product failure, a difficult
quarter — none of these are, by themselves, reasons to sell if the investment
thesis remains intact. Fisher explicitly addresses the case of a company that
fails on one major research project and experiences a sharp drop in earnings
as a result. If the same management that has produced excellent results over
many years is responsible for the setback, and if the setback is isolated
rather than symptomatic of a broader deterioration, the fundamentals of the
case for holding are unchanged. The price decline that follows such a setback
often creates an opportunity to add rather than a reason to sell.

## The three-year rule

Part Three adds a practical discipline Fisher applied to his own holdings.
When he bought a position and it failed to perform within three years —
not because anything specific had changed the investment thesis, but simply
because the market had not recognised the value he saw — he would sell.
The rule served two purposes. It limited the opportunity cost of being wrong
without knowing it, and it imposed a discipline of regular reconsideration
that prevented him from holding positions indefinitely out of inertia or
stubbornness.

The three-year rule is a backstop, not a primary trigger. It applies only in
the specific case where there is no new information — where the investment
thesis appears intact but the stock has not moved. It does not apply to cases
where new information has changed the thesis (reason 1 or 2 applies
instead) or where the stock has performed poorly because of a market
decline that has nothing to do with the specific company.

Fisher notes he violated the rule exactly once (the Rogers Corporation,
where he extended his holding beyond three years of poor performance
because of specific confidence in the management and a specific diagnosis
of what was temporarily wrong). The Rogers investment subsequently
performed well. Fisher does not draw the lesson that the rule should be
abandoned; he draws the lesson that exceptions require unusually strong
positive conviction, not merely reluctance to sell.

## Relationship to Selling vs Holding Forever

The [[Selling vs Holding Forever]] page in this wiki was written primarily
from the Nomad letters. Sleep's version of the same argument is structurally
similar to Fisher's — both emphasise that selling excellent companies
prematurely is one of the most reliable ways to destroy long-run returns —
but the two sources arrive at the conclusion from different starting points.
Fisher's framework is more explicitly rule-based (three reasons, exhaustive);
Sleep's is more empirical and contextual, grounded in his specific analysis
of Amazon, Costco, and similar businesses.

The shared underlying claim is that the investment question most investors
spend the least time on — when to sell — is the one that most often
determines long-run outcomes. Both Fisher and Sleep argue that the correct
answer to "should I sell?" is almost always no, and that the impulse to sell
is almost always driven by behavioural factors (fear of loss crystallisation,
discomfort with volatility, the desire to take a profit) rather than by the
underlying investment analysis.
