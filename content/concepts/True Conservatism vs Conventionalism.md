# True Conservatism vs Conventionalism

**Source:** [[sources/Buffett Partnership Letters]] — most directly stated
in the 1965 annual letter; present implicitly throughout the partnership
letters from 1961 onward. The distinction also underlies the discussion
of institutional pressure in the 1967 letter and the [[Concentration]]
policy in [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] (1984).

## Summary

In the 1965 annual letter, Buffett draws a distinction that is one of the
most important and least-quoted ideas in the entire corpus: genuine
conservatism and conventional behaviour are not the same thing, and
frequently point in opposite directions.

Conventional behaviour is doing what other informed, experienced investors
are doing — holding what institutional money holds, concentrating on the
same large, familiar companies that appear in every managed portfolio, and
avoiding positions that would attract attention or embarrassment if they
went wrong. Conventional behaviour feels safe because it is socially
protected: if you lose money holding IBM in 1965, no one thinks you made
a bad decision. If you lose money holding a small, obscure, statistically
cheap company, the same loss raises questions about your judgment.

Genuinely conservative behaviour is making an independent judgment about
intrinsic value and acting on it — regardless of whether the resulting
portfolio looks like everyone else's. A portfolio filled with statistically
cheap, unfashionable, low-profile businesses, held at significant
discounts to conservatively estimated intrinsic value, is _more
conservative_ in the meaningful sense than a portfolio of large, well-
known companies held at or above their intrinsic value. The first
portfolio has a genuine margin of safety. The second has social
protection for its owner but limited economic protection for the capital.

## The precise distinction

Buffett's formulation in the 1965 letter: _"We define 'conservative' as
being unlikely to suffer permanent capital loss. A business purchased
at half its conservatively appraised value is conservative even if it
has a short history and low earnings. A business purchased at twice its
value is unconservative even if it has a long history of earnings
growth."_

The relevant question is not whether a position looks safe to an observer
but whether it _is_ safe as an economic matter. Safety is a function of
the relationship between price and value, not of the familiarity of the
issuer or the quality of its recent earnings record. A well-known company
with a strong earnings history, purchased at a very high multiple of
those earnings, is not conservative — it is merely familiar. An obscure
company with a modest earnings history, purchased at 40 cents on the
dollar relative to its assets, is genuinely conservative.

The social dimension is the crucial point. Conventional behaviour is
optimised for the appearance of safety — the ability to point to a
position and say "this is what everyone holds; I made the obvious
decision." Genuinely conservative behaviour is optimised for the reality
of safety — the ability to say "I paid materially less than this is
worth, so my downside is limited even if I am wrong about the upside."

## Why the distinction matters for the investor

The practical implication follows directly from the definition. An
investor who conflates conventional behaviour with conservative behaviour
will systematically overpay for familiarity. The premium for owning the
large, well-known company rather than the obscure, statistically cheap
one is the social comfort of doing what everyone else is doing. That
comfort is real and has real value to professionals whose career risk
depends on not looking wrong. But it has no economic value to the capital
being managed.

Buffett notes that this distinction was one of his earliest and most
important departures from the conventional investment culture of the
1950s. The Graham-and-Dodd tradition he was trained in was genuinely
contrarian in exactly this sense: it was optimised for economic safety
(margin of safety, discount to intrinsic value) without any regard for
social safety (does this look like what sensible investors are supposed
to own). The two frameworks produce very different portfolios.

The partnership's Generals positions in the 1957–1967 period were
frequently in companies that conventional portfolio managers would not
have considered: obscure manufacturers, declining map publishers,
farm implement companies in Nebraska. Each position was held at a
significant discount to conservatively estimated value. None of them
looked "safe" in the conventional sense. All of them were genuinely
safe in the economic sense — the purchase price provided enough cushion
that the investor could be wrong about the upside and still not lose
material capital.

## Connection to Munger's psychology

Munger's framework in [[sources/Psychology of Human Misjudgment]] provides
the mechanism underlying the conventional/conservative confusion. Tendency
\#15 (Social-Proof Tendency) is the force that makes conventional behaviour
attractive: in conditions of uncertainty, people look to the behaviour of
others as evidence about what is correct. If other informed professionals
are holding IBM, the social proof is that IBM is the correct holding —
regardless of what the relationship between IBM's price and its intrinsic
value actually is.

The investor who succumbs to Social-Proof Tendency will end up holding
what the crowd holds, at the prices the crowd has bid it to. The investor
who resists it will hold what is independently cheap, regardless of who
else is holding it or what the market currently thinks of it. The
resistance requires what Munger calls genuine independent analysis —
arriving at a conclusion about value through first-principles reasoning
rather than by polling what others have concluded.

This is also the mechanism behind the [[Institutional Imperative]].
Manifestation (4) of the imperative — mindless imitation of peer
companies — is Social-Proof Tendency operating at the corporate level.
The corporation that makes an acquisition because competitors are making
acquisitions is not conservative; it is conventional. The distinction
between genuinely conservative and merely conventional behaviour applies
to corporate capital allocation as well as to portfolio construction.

## The Concentration corollary

The true conservatism distinction has a direct implication for the
[[Concentration]] policy. An investor who conflates conventional with
conservative will resist concentration, because a concentrated portfolio
looks riskier than a diversified one — each position is larger, and a
failure is more visible and more consequential to the overall result.

An investor who understands the genuine conservatism distinction will
reach a different conclusion: a portfolio of five positions, each held
at a meaningful discount to independently assessed intrinsic value, is
_more_ conservative than a portfolio of fifty positions held at or near
fair value. The diversified portfolio has the appearance of safety but
offers limited economic protection; its positions have been bid up by
the crowd to a point where the margin of safety is thin. The concentrated
portfolio sacrifices the appearance of safety in exchange for genuine
economic protection.

Buffett's Ground Rule 7 — the willingness to put up to 40% of the
partnership in a single security when the conviction and the margin of
safety are both high enough — is the direct practical application of
this principle. The position that represents 40% of capital is not
reckless; it is the most conservative outcome possible if the margin
of safety is large enough. The position that represents 2% of capital
in a company held at fair value is not conservative; it is merely
diversified.

## True conservatism and the cigar-butt era

The distinction also illuminates why the cigar-butt approach of the
1957–1967 period was genuinely conservative rather than merely
speculative. Buying Dempster Mill Manufacturing at a substantial discount
to its liquidation value, in a company whose operating results were poor,
looks unconventional to the point of recklessness. But the price paid
provides genuine economic protection: even in a liquidation scenario, the
investor does not lose material capital. The investment that looks safe —
buying a large, profitable, well-managed business at a high multiple —
offers less economic protection because there is no margin between price
and value.

When Buffett transitions from the purely quantitative cigar-butt approach
to the more qualitative approach of buying excellent businesses at fair
prices, the conservatism argument shifts but the structure remains. The
new conservatism is not asset-value cushion but competitive-moat
protection: the business is conservatively purchased at a price that
leaves room for the moat to be narrower than estimated, the growth to be
slower than estimated, or the transition to be messier than anticipated.
The margin of safety has changed form but not function.

## Failure modes

Two failure modes follow from the conventional/conservative confusion.

The first is the investor who confuses _unfamiliarity_ with _risk_.
Unfamiliar companies, operating in industries they haven't followed, or
purchased at price levels that look alarming relative to recent earnings,
are avoided because they feel risky — not because they are risky in
economic terms. This investor ends up systematically overpaying for
familiarity and underpaying for genuine but obscure value.

The second failure mode is the reverse: the investor who confuses
_contrarianism_ with _conservatism_. Buying an unloved company is not
inherently conservative; it is conservative only if the price paid is
below independently assessed intrinsic value. An unloved company at a
full price is neither conservative nor contrarian — it is just a company
that is temporarily out of fashion at a price that reflects its current
unpopularity rather than its underlying value.

Buffett is explicit about this in the [[Mr. Market]] framework: the
contrarian position is not to buy whatever Mr. Market hates, but to
have an independent prior estimate of value and to buy when the price
is below that estimate. Without the prior, buying on pessimism is as
wrong as buying on optimism. The genuine conservatism argument is the
same: without the independent assessment of intrinsic value, avoiding
conventional stocks and buying unconventional ones is not conservative;
it is just a different form of following a crowd.
