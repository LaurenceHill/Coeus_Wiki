# Stocks and Inflation

**Source:** [[Paths to Wealth Through Common Stocks]] — Part I.A

## Summary

Fisher's central claim is that inflation is structurally unavoidable in democratic economies, but that most common stocks do not, in fact, protect against it. Only the subset of stocks whose per-share earnings grow steadily faster than the rate of currency depreciation provide genuine inflation protection — and identifying those stocks requires exactly the same discipline as finding outstanding investments for any other reason.

The argument has two parts: a structural claim about why inflation is permanent, and an analytical claim about which investments respond to it.

## Why inflation persists

Fisher argues that fiscal deficit spending during economic downturns is politically unavoidable in democracies. When business turns down, tax revenues fall and unemployment rises; the resulting political pressure makes it almost impossible for governments to maintain balanced budgets. Deficit spending then produces inflation. While scientific R&D — what Fisher calls "the Industry of Discovery" — creates a powerful countervailing force by continually reducing the cost of producing goods, it cannot fully offset the monetary pressure. The result is a structural tendency toward mild, persistent inflation rather than runaway hyperinflation or genuine price stability.

This claim should be read in its historical context: Fisher was writing in 1960, with the experience of significant post-war inflation fresh in investors' minds. The structural argument about democratic fiscal politics has held up reasonably well, though the degree of inflation it produces has varied considerably across periods.

## The analytical error: asset ownership does not hedge inflation

The common investor belief Fisher is attacking holds that owning productive assets — factories, land, inventory, equipment embodied in corporate equity — provides inflation protection because assets rise in nominal value when currencies depreciate.

Fisher argues this is wrong in a crucial way. A company that owns fixed assets and earns a steady absolute profit on them is not inflation-protected. If the business earns $5 million per year in 1960 and earns $5 million per year in 1975, the real value of those earnings has been eaten away by fifteen years of inflation. The shares in that business have depreciated in real terms even if they nominally appreciate in line with the general asset price level. Asset ownership by itself changes the denomination of your investment but does not improve its real return.

What matters is not the assets but the earnings growth. A company whose per-share earnings grow consistently at a rate above the rate of inflation is producing a genuinely increasing real return for its shareholders. That growth in earnings is what protects against inflation — not the fact that the earnings happen to be produced by tangible assets.

## The prescription: same rules apply

The practical implication is sobering for investors who believed stock ownership was generically protective against inflation. Not all stocks work. Not most stocks, probably. Only growing companies work — and growing companies are the same companies the [[Fifteen Points]] framework is designed to identify in the first place.

Fisher's position: if you have done the work correctly and own outstanding growth businesses, you have inflation protection as a by-product. If you own mediocre or stagnant businesses because you believed stock ownership in itself was an inflation hedge, you have been misled.

This puts inflation protection entirely inside the standard investment selection problem. There is no separate class of "inflation hedge stocks" requiring different analysis. The question "does this company protect against inflation?" reduces to "is this company a genuine growth business?" which in turn reduces to "does this management meet the Fifteen Points criteria?"

## What Fisher does not say

Fisher does not claim that the rate of inflation materially affects *when* to buy outstanding growth stocks. He is explicitly sceptical of using macroeconomic conditions — including inflation — as a timing guide. His consistent position is that fundamentals (the quality of management, the growth potential of the product line) are the primary determinant of investment success, and that macro timing adds little relative to getting the fundamentals right.

He also does not claim that all debt or fixed-income assets are destroyed by inflation, though he is clearly more interested in equities than bonds. His argument is narrowly that the equity inflation hedge story is oversimplified in a way that leads investors to hold inadequate businesses.

## Relationship to other sources

Buffett makes a closely related argument in the 1979 Berkshire letter with his "investor's misery index," arguing that inflation extracts a substantial real tax from nominal equity returns and that most businesses cannot fully protect shareholders against it. See [[Inflation Tax]]. The two arguments are complementary: Fisher identifies which businesses are inflation-protective (growing ones); Buffett quantifies how much inflation costs even good businesses by measuring the additional retained earnings needed merely to sustain existing operations.
