# Institutional Buying

**Source:** [[Paths to Wealth Through Common Stocks]] — Part I.B and Part II

## Summary

Fisher's central claim in this section is that the post-war growth of institutional investment — pension funds, bank trust departments, insurance companies, educational endowments — has permanently altered the structure of the equity market. Institutional demand is not spread evenly across stocks. It is, and will remain, concentrated on a relatively small number of companies for reasons that are partly economic and partly legal. This concentration creates a specific dynamic: a small group of stocks sell at a persistent premium to the rest of the market, and that premium is self-reinforcing as long as the underlying quality of the companies warrants it.

Understanding this structure, Fisher argues, allows an alert investor to identify and act on one of the more reliable sources of major capital gain available in the equity market.

## The structural shift

Before World War II, the net bias in equity markets was downward. Estate tax liquidations, combined with relatively thin institutional buying, meant that shares were more often being sold to raise cash than purchased to hold. Post-war, this reversed. Pension funds grew rapidly as corporations adopted defined-benefit retirement programmes; trust departments at major banks were managing large and growing pools; educational endowments and foundations were allocating increasing fractions of their assets to equities. The aggregate effect was that institutional buyers were permanently taking shares off the market, creating a steady background bid that had not previously existed.

Fisher's prediction in 1960 — that this structural demand would continue to grow through the decade — proved correct and has remained substantially correct for the following half-century, with institutional investors now owning a dominant share of US equity market capitalisation.

## Why demand concentrates

The concentration of institutional demand on a small number of stocks has two causes, each reinforcing the other.

The economic cause: institutional investors are managing funds on behalf of beneficiaries who cannot bear speculative losses — widows, pension holders, university endowments. The right securities for such investors are, sensibly, the highest-quality equities available: the largest companies, with demonstrated management depth, diversified revenue, high profit margins, and the financial strength to weather economic setbacks. This already narrows the universe considerably.

The legal cause is less intuitive but in some ways more powerful. The men managing most institutional funds are trustees, subject to trustee liability rules built up through decades of court decisions. A trustee who loses money for beneficiaries can be held personally liable — but only if he violated certain rules. The operative rule is the "prudent man" standard: a trustee is protected if he did what any prudent man would have done at the time. In practice, the courts and lawyers have interpreted this to mean: what were the largest and most prominent trustees (the trust departments of the major New York banks) holding? A trustee who holds the same securities as these institutions in roughly the same proportions has essentially no personal liability exposure even if those securities perform poorly. A trustee who holds something different, however sound the reasoning, is personally exposed if the investment fails.

The result is a self-reinforcing system. The leading trust departments hold a relatively small group of approved stocks. Other trustees follow those holdings for legal protection. This concentrates demand on the approved group, which bids up their P/E ratios, which further validates the selection and makes them even more clearly "prudent man" holdings. Stocks outside this group, however sound, are passed over.

## The "stilts" dynamic

Fisher describes the resulting price structure through the image of stilts. Institutionally accepted stocks are up on stilts — they sell at significantly higher P/E ratios than equally profitable businesses without that acceptance. This is not inherently irrational: the premium partly reflects genuine quality (these are often excellent businesses) and partly reflects the steady bid from concentrated institutional demand.

The stilts are stable as long as quality is maintained. A company that continues to deserve institutional acceptance — growing earnings, excellent management, strong competitive position — will stay elevated and rise further as its earnings grow. The premium P/E ratio does not need to come down; it persists.

The danger is when a company begins to lose the qualities that put it on stilts. This rarely happens suddenly in the market price; institutions are slow to recognise deterioration, and the "prudent man" logic that caused them to buy also makes them reluctant to sell before others do. The decline typically lags the deterioration by years. But when it comes, it is severe — the company falls not merely in proportion to its earnings decline but by the additional amount that the premium P/E unwinds. The high-stilt stock that has lost its quality becomes dramatically overvalued before the market acknowledges it, and then falls further than a stock that was never at a premium.

## The opportunity: the edge of institutional acceptance

Fisher's investment thesis follows directly from this structure. The highest-risk, highest-return position is owning a company before it earns institutional acceptance — a medium-sized, rapidly growing company with outstanding management that is beginning to prove itself but has not yet been identified by the major institutional buyers. Such a company is available at a low P/E ratio reflecting its unrecognised status. As the company continues to grow and its management quality becomes apparent, two things happen simultaneously: earnings rise, and the P/E ratio expands toward institutional norms. The investor who bought before this recognition captures both gains.

Fisher gives the example numerically: a company with $2 in earnings selling at 12x earns 5 years later at $4 per share. The earnings alone would push the stock from 24 to 48. But if in those five years the company gained institutional acceptance and its P/E expanded from 12x to 24x, the stock goes not to 48 but to 96. The investor has quadrupled his investment from a doubling of earnings.

This is not a strategy available to everyone. It requires the analytical skill to identify the exceptional management early — which is exactly the skill the [[Fifteen Points]] and [[Scuttlebutt]] method are designed to develop. Fisher is explicit that finding these situations is not easy; he is equally explicit that the reward justifies the difficulty.

## Holding decisions for fully accepted institutional stocks

Fisher also addresses what to do once a stock has reached peak institutional acceptance and the P/E expansion opportunity is largely exhausted. His argument is nuanced. At this point, growth in share value can only come from earnings growth, not from further P/E expansion. The temptation to sell and reinvest the proceeds in a pre-acceptance opportunity is understandable but often wrong.

His reasoning: the very fact that a stock has earned institutional acceptance usually means it is managed by people of extraordinary quality who will continue to grow earnings at above-average rates. Furthermore, the tax cost of selling an appreciated position is substantial, and the risks of finding and correctly evaluating the next pre-acceptance opportunity are significant. For investors who have already accumulated meaningful wealth and have less need for spectacular further gains, holding peak institutional stocks provides growth with safety — and the stilts are only dangerous when quality genuinely deteriorates, which a watchful investor should be able to detect with ample warning.

## Relationship to Financial Community Appraisal

The Institutional Buying concept is a structural explanation for the mechanism behind [[Financial Community Appraisal]]. The Appraisal concept (from _Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits_) argues that stock prices are driven by the financial community's appraisal of a company, its industry, and the market — not directly by the underlying facts. The Institutional Buying chapter explains _why_ that appraisal is sticky, why it concentrates on a narrow set of companies, and why the P/E divergence between approved and unapproved stocks can persist for years. The two concepts should be read together.
