# Buffett's Three Investment Categories

**Source:** [[sources/Buffett Partnership Letters]] — introduced in the
early letters (1957–1961) and refined across the partnership's twelve years;
most formally stated in the 1961, 1964, and 1966 annual letters. A
four-way version appears in the 1966 letter, splitting the original
Generals category into two. The framework is specific to the partnership
era and does not appear in the [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]].

## Summary

In his first full-year letter (January 1958, covering 1957), Buffett
organised his investment activities into three categories:

1. **Generals** — undervalued securities held as a passive minority
   position, with no special relationship to management and no
   control over the timing of value realisation
2. **Workouts** — special situations with a defined corporate event
   (merger, liquidation, recapitalisation, tender offer) that sets a
   rough time horizon and a roughly calculable probability distribution
   of outcomes
3. **Controls** — positions large enough to exercise influence over
   management, either by owning a controlling interest directly or by
   working alongside other large holders

The taxonomy is not merely descriptive — it carries different portfolio
weights depending on market conditions, produces different risk/return
profiles, and requires different analytical methods. Buffett managed
all three simultaneously throughout the partnership's life, with the
relative weighting shifting as market conditions and the size of the
partnership changed.

## Generals

Generals are the most familiar category to a reader of the Berkshire
letters: buy a business worth materially more than the price asked, in
sufficient quantity that the position matters to the portfolio, and wait
for the gap to close. The analytical work is primarily quantitative —
asset values, earnings power, private-market comparison prices — rather
than qualitative. The holding period is indefinite and depends on when
the market's appraisal corrects.

Within the partnership's investment-category accounting, Generals were the
category most affected by overall market levels. A falling market creates
more Generals opportunities; a rising market reduces them. Buffett wrote
explicitly that his inability to find enough Generals at attractive prices
in 1967–1969 was a primary reason for dissolution — not his selection
ability within the category, but the market environment's reduction of
the available supply.

The 1966 letter refines Generals into two sub-categories:

**Generals — Private Owner Basis:** positions in companies where a private
buyer would pay substantially more than the current market price to own
the whole business. The analytical method is to establish what a
reasonable, informed buyer would pay for 100% of the enterprise and to
purchase a minority interest at a significant discount to that figure.
The return comes from either the market eventually recognising the value,
or from a corporate transaction at a price closer to private-market value.
This is the most traditional Graham-and-Dodd category.

**Generals — Relatively Undervalued:** positions in companies that are not
cheap in absolute terms but are cheap relative to a directly comparable
business trading at a higher multiple. The analytical method is relative:
find two businesses with similar economic characteristics and similar
prospects, establish that the market is pricing one substantially below
the other, and buy the cheap one. This category requires the analyst to
hold two analyses simultaneously and is therefore more demanding.
Buffett notes that his largest single holding in 1966–1967 (unnamed,
but clearly American Express after the Salad Oil scandal) was in this
category — a business not statistically cheap by asset value but
dramatically cheap relative to its earning power and competitive position.

## Workouts

Workouts are investments whose return is driven by a specific corporate
event rather than by the market's general reassessment of a business.
The prototypical workout is a merger: if Company A announces it will
acquire Company B at $40/share and Company B is trading at $36, the
investor can buy Company B and earn the $4 spread when the deal closes.
The relevant questions are the probability the deal closes, the expected
time to close, and whether alternative uses of the capital produce a
higher expected return.

Workouts serve two functions in the partnership structure. First, they
produce returns that are largely _uncorrelated_ with the stock market.
When the Dow falls 15%, the spread in a pending acquisition deal narrows
or widens based on deal-specific factors, not market psychology. This
makes Workouts a genuine diversifier against Generals in down markets.
Second, they provide an _arbitrage_ return that can be calculated
roughly in advance, giving the portfolio a component of more predictable
income.

The risk in Workouts is not market risk but event risk: the deal does not
close, or closes at a lower price, or takes longer than expected. Buffett
notes in the 1969 dissolution letter that the partnership's Workout
experience in 1969 was "atrocious" — not because of bad luck but because
of his failure to anticipate a government enforcement trend in antitrust
that caused several deals to fail. This was, in his phrasing, "100% my
fault" — an improper assessment of a developing external force. It is
a clean illustration of the category's distinctive risk: the relevant
unknown is not "what is this business worth" but "will this specific
event occur on this specific timeline."

Workouts were typically diversified across a larger number of smaller
positions than Generals, precisely because individual event risk is
harder to analyse to a high degree of confidence. The diversification
principle Buffett articulates in the Berkshire letters (see
[[Concentration]]) — that diversification is appropriate when individual-
position risk cannot be independently analysed away — is stated in embryo
in the partnership treatment of Workouts.

## Controls

Controls are the most active category: Buffett accumulates enough of a
business to exert influence on management, the board, or the deployment
of corporate assets. The return can come through management change,
capital redeployment (liquidating subsidiary assets, paying special
dividends, buying back shares at below intrinsic value), or sale of the
business to a strategic buyer at a price reflecting full private-market
value.

Two controls dominate the partnership's history:

**Sanborn Map (1958–1960):** Sanborn produced detailed fire-insurance
maps of American cities. Its primary business was declining as insurance
underwriting became more sophisticated, but it had accumulated an
investment portfolio worth more per share than the entire stock price
— meaning investors were getting the map business for free, at a
negative price. Buffett accumulated a large position, joined the board,
and worked toward separating the investment portfolio from the operating
business through a buyback programme. The resolution was successful.
Sanborn illustrates the cleanest Controls thesis: the market is pricing
a combination of assets at less than the sum of parts, and a sufficiently
large holder can unlock the value through corporate action.

**Dempster Mill Manufacturing (1961–1963):** A Nebraska farm implement
and water supply business, acquired at a significant discount to book
value — a classic cigar-butt. After accumulating a controlling interest,
Buffett brought in Harry Bottle as manager. Bottle's achievement over
two years was comprehensive: he reduced inventory from $4 million to
$1 million, eliminated marginal product lines, cut receivables, sold
non-essential assets, and redeployed the liberated capital into
marketable securities managed by Buffett. The business that had been
earning a poor return on a large capital base was restructured so that
the residual operating business required far less capital and what
remained was far better run.

The Dempster experience is the paradigm case of the Controls strategy:
you acquire a business at a price below its liquidation value, install
the right operator, shrink the capital base to its most productive
configuration, and redeploy the freed capital elsewhere. The total
return is not just the business's operating improvement but the
compounding that occurs when liberated capital is redeployed at good
rates. This is also the clearest illustration of why Controls are
time-consuming: Dempster required substantial management attention
from Buffett across two years.

**Berkshire Hathaway (acquired 1962):** First purchased in November 1962
at $7.60/share (average cost $14.86), originally as a Generals position
and then consolidated into a Controls position. The textile business was
the cigar-butt: declining, capital-intensive, with a workforce that
Buffett described as hard-working and willing to improve, but facing
structural headwinds in the American textile industry that no amount
of good management could overcome. The strategy over the 1960s was
the same as Dempster's: redeploy capital from the low-returning textile
operation into higher-returning businesses. National Indemnity Company
(insurance) and Illinois National Bank and Trust of Rockford were the
first beneficiaries of this redeployment. See \[\[Cigar Butt to
Compounder]] for the full arc.

## The four-way split (1966)

By 1966, Buffett had split the original three categories into four: the
two Generals sub-categories (Private Owner Basis and Relatively
Undervalued) plus Workouts and Controls. The refinement reflects his
growing emphasis on qualitative factors — a business in the Relatively
Undervalued sub-category requires a different analytical frame from one
in the Private Owner Basis sub-category, even though both are passive
minority positions. The distinction matters because it determines what
evidence you are looking for: asset value and liquidation proceeds in
the first case; competitive position and earnings relative to a
comparison company in the second.

## How the categories interact

The three-category framework is not just a taxonomy for reporting; it is
a portfolio management structure. Generals provide the primary return
driver but are most sensitive to general market levels. Workouts provide
a roughly market-uncorrelated return stream. Controls provide the
potential for transformational returns but require significant capital
and management attention.

As the partnership grew, the portfolio weight in Generals had to fall
— not because Generals became less attractive analytically, but because
the partnership had grown too large to acquire meaningful positions in
small, statistically cheap companies without moving the price against
itself. This is the _capital size constraint_ that Buffett cites explicitly
in the 1967 and 1969 letters as a structural reason for dissolution.
The cigar-butt Generals category is inherently capacity-constrained.

Controls, by contrast, become _more_ natural as capital grows: you need
scale to accumulate a controlling interest in a meaningful business. The
Berkshire era that began in 1965 is in many ways just the Controls category
pursued at scale — except that the Controls Buffett began acquiring after
1970 were not cigar-butts to be restructured but excellent businesses
to be held permanently.

## Connection to the later Berkshire framework

The three investment categories do not appear by name in the Berkshire
letters, but their logic persists. What Buffett calls "marketable
securities" in the Berkshire era is the heir to Generals. The acquisition
of wholly-owned subsidiaries is Controls at scale. The arbitrage programme
Berkshire ran in the 1980s (documented in the 1987 and 1988 letters) is
the Workouts category — with Buffett noting explicitly that his arbitrage
method had not changed materially from his partnership days. The categories
evolved in scope and form, but the underlying logic is continuous.

The transition from cigar-butt Generals to quality-compounder Generals is
the single biggest evolution in Buffett's thinking across this period.
It is documented in the 1967 letter's discussion of the qualitative-
quantitative spectrum and crystallised in the December 1969 letter's
"businesses not stocks" statement. See [[Cigar Butt to Compounder]] for
the full narrative.

## The failure mode

The Workouts failure in 1969 illustrates the category-specific failure mode:
confusing expertise in security analysis with expertise in predicting
external events. Buffett was an excellent analyst of whether a business was
cheap; he was not infallible as a predictor of whether a government
enforcement trend would accelerate. The 1969 Workouts losses were the result
of applying a skill-set in an area where it was not sufficient. Buffett's
self-diagnosis — "I never believe in mixing what I think should happen
(socially) with what I think will happen in making decisions" — is a clean
statement of the circle-of-competence boundary. See [[Circle of Competence]].
