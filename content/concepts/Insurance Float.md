# Insurance Float

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1997 and 1998 letters;
extended in 2000, 2001, 2002, 2004, 2006, and 2010 letters

## Summary

Float is money an insurer holds but does not own. It arises because premiums are collected before losses are paid, an interval that sometimes extends over many years. During that window, the insurer invests the funds. Most insurers pay a price for this privilege — premiums typically do not cover all eventual losses and expenses, so the insurer runs an underwriting loss. That underwriting loss is the *cost of float*. An insurance business creates value if its cost of float is lower than the cost of obtaining funds through any other means; it is a lemon if the cost of float exceeds market rates for money.

Buffett introduced float as a central analytical concept in the 1997 letter and expanded it the following year after the General Re acquisition made insurance an even larger proportion of Berkshire's business. His working definition is sharp: *"Float is money we hold but don't own."*

## Why it matters

The mechanism is powerful enough that Buffett calls it an accounting irony. Float appears as a liability on the balance sheet — technically it is, because the insurer owes the money to claimants — yet it has had greater economic value to Berkshire than an equivalent amount of net worth would have had. The reason is that float, invested at good returns with little or no cost, is effectively free capital. When the cost is negative — meaning underwriting is profitable — the insurer is being *paid* to hold and invest money. That is an extraordinary structural advantage.

Berkshire's float grew from $17 million in 1967 to $7.1 billion in 1997 and $22.8 billion by year-end 1998, compounding at roughly 21–25% per year. Over that period the average cost of float was "well under zero." The combination of scale, growth, and negative cost is the primary engine behind Berkshire's investment portfolio: float funded the purchases of Coke, Gillette, American Express, and Wells Fargo.

## The three variables that determine value

Buffett specifies three things to assess when evaluating any insurance operation:

1. **Amount of float** — size matters, because larger float produces larger investment income.
2. **Cost of float** — the underwriting loss divided by average float. If this figure is consistently below market rates, the business earns its keep. If it is above market rates, the business destroys value even when it appears superficially profitable.
3. **Long-term outlook for both** — a single year of good underwriting does not tell you much. The question is whether the competitive position, pricing discipline, and reserving conservatism that produce low-cost float are durable.

A caution Buffett repeats in both years: because loss costs must be estimated, insurers have enormous latitude in figuring underwriting results. Errors — innocent or otherwise — flow directly into reported earnings. An experienced observer can usually detect large-scale reserving mistakes, but most investors must accept what is presented. "Virtually all surprises in insurance are unpleasant ones."

## Structural advantage of permanent ownership

The 1998 letter makes explicit why Berkshire could offer General Re something its public ownership could not provide. A publicly-held reinsurer is graded by shareholders and credit agencies on the smoothness of its earnings. Wide swings hurt P/E ratios and credit ratings even when the volatile business has a satisfactory long-run expectation. This creates a perverse incentive: reinsurers lay off profitable-but-lumpy business through retrocessions, or simply refuse to write it. Berkshire, by contrast, *"happily accepts volatility, just as long as it carries with it the expectation of increased profits over time."*

The preference Buffett states is direct: *"We prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%."* A company that refuses volatility in order to smooth reported earnings is making a permanent sacrifice of economic value for a cosmetic presentation benefit. Berkshire's permanent capital base and long-term shareholder register remove the pressure to manage appearances.

## Three principles of underwriting discipline (2001)

The 2001 letter provides the most systematic statement of what low-cost float
actually requires at the operating level. Winners in insurance, Buffett
argues, are those who unfailingly stick to three principles:

First, they accept only risks they are able to properly evaluate — staying
within their [[Circle of Competence]] — and that, after evaluating all
relevant factors including remote loss scenarios, carry an expectancy of
profit. They ignore market-share considerations and are sanguine about losing
business to competitors offering foolish prices.

Second, they limit the business they accept to guarantee that no single event
or related set of events will threaten solvency through loss aggregation. They
ceaselessly search for possible correlation among seemingly unrelated risks.

Third, they avoid business involving moral risk. *"No matter what the rate,
trying to write good contracts with bad people doesn't work."* While most
policyholders are honourable, the exceptions are expensive enough to warrant
absolute exclusion.

September 11th, 2001 exposed that General Re had broken all three principles
at once: it had priced without evaluating terrorism exposure (rule 1 failure),
had accumulated an aggregation of risks that could have been fatal under a
nuclear attack scenario (rule 2 failure), and had grown in ways that ignored
both at the price level (rule 3 failure by omission). The three principles are
therefore not aspirational — they are the minimum conditions for an insurance
business to function as described in the economics section above.

## Experience versus exposure (2001)

The 2001 letter introduces a distinction between two approaches to pricing
risk that extends well beyond insurance into any probabilistic domain.

Experience-based pricing looks backward: how many earthquakes occurred in
California in the past century? What was the average underwriting loss for
D&O insurance in the prior decade? This approach is sensible when the past
is a reliable guide to the future — which it often is for high-frequency,
low-variance events.

Exposure-based pricing looks at the current structure of risk, regardless of
historical frequency. The 2001 letter's most important example is D&O
insurance in a bull market. Late in a rising market, D&O losses are
historically low: there are few targets to sue, and accounting irregularities
go undetected when stock prices are climbing. Experience data looks
excellent. But exposure is exploding — *"ridiculous public offerings, earnings
manipulation, chain-letter-like stock promotions and a potpourri of other
unsavory activities"* are accumulating in the background. When the market
turns, these sins surface simultaneously, and the correct premium for D&O
excess coverage *"might well, if based on exposure, be five or more times the
premium dictated by experience."*

Terrorism is the extreme case: no significant historical precedent existed,
so experience-based pricing implied near-zero cost. But exposure to a
correlated, intentional catastrophe was real and growing. *"All of us in
the industry made a fundamental underwriting mistake by focusing on
experience, rather than exposure, thereby assuming a huge terrorism risk
for which we received no premium."*

The general principle: whenever the conditions generating future losses are
structurally different from the conditions that generated historical data,
experience-based pricing is not merely imprecise — it is actively misleading.
The more unusual the risk, the more important the distinction becomes.

## Portrait of a disciplined underwriter: NICO (2004)

The 2004 letter provides the most detailed published account of what
disciplined underwriting looks like at the operating level, using National
Indemnity Company (NICO) as the model. NICO's record is striking enough to
serve as a stand-alone argument for the principles the rest of this page
articulates.

The table Buffett reproduces covers 1980–2004 and shows premium volume rising
from $79.6M in 1980 to $343.8M in 2004. But the interior of the series
matters more than the endpoints: premiums shrank from $366M in 1986 to
$54M by 1999, a contraction of 85%, before expanding again. The contraction
was deliberate. NICO was unwilling to write business at prices it judged
inadequate, and for thirteen years it lost most of its premium volume rather
than compromise on price.

The operational principles behind NICO's record are: price before volume —
no policy is written unless the expected return on it is acceptable, regardless
of what competitors are charging; and a no-layoff policy — because NICO will
not guarantee employment continuity by cutting prices to generate volume. This
is an unusual commitment: it removes the natural organisational incentive to
underwrite at sub-economic rates in order to keep staff occupied. Manager
Don Wurster built float of $950M off a modest premium base by letting the
float serve the investment operation rather than forcing the investment
operation to subsidise underwriting losses. The lesson is structural: an
insurer that writes bad business to keep its workforce busy destroys the
economics of the float model. The discipline is one-directional: *"it's
impossible to always do well, but it's always possible to behave well."*

## The Equitas/Lloyd's retroactive reinsurance deal (2006)

The 2006 letter describes Berkshire's most unusual single insurance
transaction: the acquisition of Equitas, the special-purpose vehicle created
in 1996 to resolve Lloyd's of London's asbestos and environmental liabilities.

Lloyd's, tracing to Edward Lloyd's coffee house in 1688, had accumulated
catastrophic liabilities during the 1970s and 1980s, primarily from
asbestos-related claims in the United States. The traditional structure of
unlimited personal liability for individual "Names" threatened to bankrupt
thousands who had invested in Lloyd's syndicates expecting modest supplemental
returns. In 1996, Lloyd's pooled £11.1 billion of assets into Equitas to
ring-fence the pre-1993 liabilities; Names who transferred their claims to
Equitas received partial cash relief but retained residual uncertainty.

In 2006, Berkshire agreed to provide definitive finality. Berkshire received
$7.12 billion of cash and investments from Equitas, assumed all of the pooled
liabilities up to a cap of $13.9 billion, and paid a cash settlement to each
Name. For the Names, the deal extinguished the unlimited personal liability
that had hung over them for two decades. For Berkshire, the deal produced
immediate float of approximately $7 billion available for investment, with
coverage of $5.7 billion above Equitas's own actuarial estimates.

The accounting treatment is DCRA: Deferred Charges on Retroactive Reinsurance
Arrangements. Because the contract covers losses that have already occurred
rather than future events, GAAP recognises the assumed liabilities at
acquisition but amortises the premium over the life of the run-off. In the
near term this produces an annual DCRA charge — initially approximately $450
million — that depresses reported earnings without representing a real economic
deterioration. The economic question is simply whether the $7.12B of assets,
invested over decades at Berkshire's long-run rates, is sufficient to cover
whatever the ultimate claims prove to be.

## Four commandments of underwriting discipline (2010)

The 2010 letter provides the most compressed statement of what sound insurance
underwriting requires, presented in the context of General Re's revival under
Tad Montross. Four disciplines are necessary, and an insurer that passes the
first three but flunks the fourth is not sound:

1. **Understand all exposures** that might cause a policy to incur losses — no
   pricing without full evaluation of what could go wrong.
2. **Evaluate conservatively** the likelihood of any exposure actually causing
   a loss and the probable cost if it does — err toward realism rather than
   optimism.
3. **Set a premium that delivers a profit**, on average, after both prospective
   loss costs and operating expenses are covered — pricing discipline must be
   embedded in the premium, not hoped for from subsequent investment returns.
4. **Willingness to walk away** if the appropriate premium cannot be obtained.

The fourth discipline is where most insurers fail. "The urgings of Wall
Street, pressures from the agency force and brokers, or simply a refusal by a
testosterone-driven CEO to accept shrinking volumes has led too many insurers
to write business at inadequate prices. 'The other guy is doing it so we must
as well' spells trouble in any business, but none more so than insurance." NICO's
voluntary 85% premium contraction over thirteen years (described above) is the
paradigmatic example of the fourth commandment functioning correctly.

## Float growth: the record (1967–2010)

Float has grown from $20 million in 1967 to $65.8 billion at year-end 2010,
compounding at roughly 25% annually for over four decades. The cost of that
float, in aggregate over the period, has been well below zero in most years —
Berkshire has actually been *paid* to hold other people's money across the
full run. Individual years have been terrible: 1984 (float cost 19%), 2001
(12.8%, driven by September 11th losses at General Re), 2005 (hurricane
losses from Katrina, Rita, and Wilma produced a meaningful float cost).
These bad years are built into the long-run average and do not alter the
structural conclusion.

By 2010, Berkshire had recorded eight consecutive years of underwriting
profit, with cumulative underwriting gains of $17 billion over that run.

The float figures by year-end:

- 1997: $7.4B ($2.9B GEICO, $4.0B Other Reinsurance, $0.5B Other Primary)
- 1998: $22.8B (General Re acquisition adds $14.9B)
- 1999: $25.3B
- 2000: $27.9B (float cost 6%; $1.6B underwriting loss)
- 2001: $35.5B (float cost 12.8%; September 11th)
- 2002: $41.2B (float cost 1%; underwriting discipline restored at General Re)
- 2003: $44.2B ($23.7B Gen Re, $13.9B B-H Reinsurance, $5.3B GEICO, $1.3B Other)
- 2004: $46.1B ($23.1B Gen Re, $15.3B B-H Reinsurance, $6.0B GEICO, $1.7B Other)
- 2005: $49.3B ($22.9B Gen Re, $16.2B B-H Reinsurance, $6.7B GEICO, $3.4B Other)
- 2006: $50.9B ($22.8B Gen Re, $16.9B B-H Reinsurance, $7.2B GEICO, $4.0B Other); plus the Equitas portfolio (~$7B) arriving early 2007
- 2007: $58.7B ($23.0B Gen Re, $23.7B B-H Reinsurance, $7.8B GEICO, $4.2B Other); underwriting profit $3.374B
- 2008: $58.5B ($21.1B Gen Re, $24.2B B-H Reinsurance, $8.5B GEICO, $4.7B Other); underwriting profit $2.792B
- 2009: $61.9B ($21.0B Gen Re, $26.2B B-H Reinsurance, $9.6B GEICO, $5.1B Other); underwriting profit $1.559B
- 2010: $65.8B ($20.0B Gen Re, $30.4B B-H Reinsurance, $10.3B GEICO, $5.1B Other); underwriting profit $2.013B

The 2002 letter notes the appropriate caveat about the 1% cost figure: no
megacatastrophe occurred in 2002, which inflated the apparent underwriting
result. A normalised cost would be somewhat higher, though still very low by
any historical standard. The 2005 hurricane losses are the clearest
illustration in the record of the super-cat risk inherent in reinsurance:
even so, GEICO's continuing underwriting profitability kept the overall 2005
float cost modest.

## Caveats

Float growth is not inherently good. Buffett is explicit: if the cost of float rises toward or above market rates, growth in float becomes a curse rather than a blessing. The businesses that grew float fastest in the 1970s and 1980s often did so by underpricing risk, which produced the spectacular losses that eventually destroyed them. Super-cat insurance illustrates the point: Berkshire writes it knowing that a truly terrible year is not a possibility but a certainty. What matters is that the expected value over a run of years is positive, not that every individual year is profitable.
