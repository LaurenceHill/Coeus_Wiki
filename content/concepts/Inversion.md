# Inversion

**Source:** [[sources/Poor Charlie's Almanack]] — stated most explicitly in
Talk 4 ("Practical Thought About Practical Thought?", 1996) and Talk 10
(USC Gould School of Law, 2007); the Harvard commencement speech (Talk 1,
1986) is the earliest application without naming it; appears in every talk in
the Almanack as a structural habit of mind. Related to Munger's first talk
("prescription for misery") and to the algebraic tradition of Carl Jacobi.

## Summary

Inversion is Munger's most frequently applied problem-solving technique: when
a problem resists a direct forward attack, turn it around and ask what you are
trying to avoid rather than what you are trying to achieve. "Invert, always
invert" — the instruction of the nineteenth-century German algebraist Carl
Jacobi — is Munger's shorthand for the habit.

The technique has two distinct applications. The first is logical: some problems
are simply easier to solve backward than forward. Finding the square root of 2
is irrational is much easier by assuming the contrary and deriving a
contradiction than by constructing a positive proof. The second is strategic:
focusing on failure modes is more tractable than optimising for success,
because failure has fewer causes and those causes are more reliably
identifiable.

## The algebraic foundation

Jacobi's rule arose in mathematics: many problems that resist direct solution
yield when you assume the opposite of what you want to prove and then show
that the assumption leads to an impossibility. The Pythagoreans proved the
irrationality of √2 this way. Euler proved various results in number theory by
assuming their negation and deriving contradictions.

Munger's claim is that this algebraic technique transfers directly to the messy
practical problems of business, investment, and life. It is not a metaphor; it
is a literally applicable method. When you cannot see a clear path from where
you are to where you want to be, ask what reliably leads away from where
you want to be and construct your approach to avoid those paths.

## Application 1: Prescriptions for misery (Talk 1)

The earliest demonstration in the Almanack is the 1986 Harvard Law School
commencement address. Munger does not tell the graduates how to succeed.
He inverts the question and tells them how to reliably guarantee failure and
misery:

- Ingest chemicals that will alter your moods and behaviour
- Envy and resent others' success; let those feelings fester
- Be unreliable; fail to do what you say you will do
- Learn only from your own experience, never vicariously from others
- When you first fail at anything, give up; do not persist through difficulty
- Avoid all association with people more competent than yourself

Munger's point: these prescriptions for misery are so reliable that inverting
them gives you, by subtraction, a robust set of principles for a decent life —
not a guaranteed route to the top, but a reliable route away from the bottom.
The negative is easier to specify than the positive.

## Application 2: The five problem-solving notions (Talk 4)

Talk 4 codifies inversion as the third of five general problem-solving notions,
alongside numerical fluency, multidisciplinary wisdom, lollapalooza-awareness,
and deciding big "no-brainer" questions first.

The formulation: "It is not enough to think problems through forward. You
must also think in reverse, much like the rustic who wanted to know where
he was going to die so that he'd never go there." The rustic's logic is
ridiculous in form but profound in substance: if you cannot fully specify the
conditions for success, you can often fully specify the conditions for certain
failure, and avoiding those is a tractable and productive substitute.

In the Coca-Cola thought experiment of Talk 4, inversion appears in the
"reality check" section. After building the positive case — Pavlovian
conditioning, operant conditioning, social proof, scale — Munger then inverts:
what must Coca-Cola avoid at all costs?

1. Aftertaste that limits per-sitting consumption — avoid by design
2. Loss of trademark control (allowing a "peppy cola") — avoid by aggressive
   trademark defence
3. Envy from competitors and regulators — avoid by plainly deserving success
   through quality and fair pricing
4. Sudden large changes in the established flavour — avoid because the
   Deprival Super-Reaction Tendency of consumers makes this catastrophic
   (the New Coke fiasco proved the theory)

The inversion check catches dangers that the positive analysis does not
surface. Forward thinking builds the case; backward thinking tests it.

## Application 3: Investing — what reliably destroys wealth?

Munger applies inversion most consequentially to investment. Rather than
asking "how do I get rich?" he asks "what reliably makes people poor?" and
constructs investment discipline around avoiding those causes.

From the Almanack and other Munger sources, the reliable destroyers of
investment wealth are: leverage combined with forced selling; excessive
transaction costs (the croupier's take from overactive management);
overconfidence in any single model or analytical framework; ignoring
incentive-caused bias in the people you rely on for information; concentrating
in businesses you do not understand; and failing to distinguish between a
temporary price decline (which is an opportunity) and a genuine deterioration
in business quality (which is a signal to exit).

The inversion discipline is why Munger spends so much effort on the
psychology of misjudgment: it is not a catalogue of interesting quirks but
a checklist of the specific mechanisms by which intelligent people reliably
destroy value. Knowing the mechanisms is the precondition for avoiding them.

## Application 4: The iron prescription (Talk 10)

In the USC Law commencement address, Munger states the most demanding
application of inversion to opinion formation:

*"I feel that I am not entitled to have an opinion unless I can state the
arguments against my position better than the people who are in opposition."*

This is inversion applied to the intellectual process itself. Before holding
any strong view, deliberately construct the strongest possible case against it.
If you cannot do this, you do not yet understand the subject well enough
to have a reliable opinion. If you can, and the view still survives, you hold
it with justified confidence.

The same principle is what Darwin practiced: he spent his life specifically
seeking evidence that would overturn his own theories. The Almanack notes
that Darwin is buried in Westminster Abbey next to Newton — a man whose
results were built on genius, whereas Darwin's were built on methodical
self-criticism. Munger treats the Darwin method as the operational standard for
anyone who wants to think clearly.

## The asymmetry of inversion's usefulness

Inversion is more reliably useful when the space of failures is smaller than
the space of successes. There are many ways to get rich; there are fewer
reliable ways to go broke. There are many possible sources of business
success; there are a manageable number of reliable causes of business
failure. This asymmetry means that identifying and avoiding the reliable
failure modes often produces more value per unit of analytical effort than
constructing and optimising the positive case.

This is the logic behind Charlie Munger's famous description of his method
as primarily "avoiding stupidity" rather than "seeking brilliance." "You don't
have to be brilliant, only a little bit wiser than the other guys, on average,
for a long, long time." Consistent avoidance of the reliable failure modes —
combined with patience — produces exceptional results over decades without
requiring exceptional forecasting ability.

## Inversion and the psychology system

Inversion intersects with the psychology of misjudgment in a specific way.
Many of the 25 tendencies are difficult to observe and correct in oneself
directly, because they operate subconsciously and are reinforced by the same
cognitive shortcuts that make them powerful. But they are often easier to
identify when you ask: "What tendencies would reliably cause me to reach
a wrong conclusion in this situation?" Running the psychology checklist in
inversion mode — not "what am I missing?" but "what would cause me to
be wrong?" — is more productive because it forces active search rather than
passive availability.

## Contradictions and limits

Inversion is a powerful technique but not a complete decision system.
Avoiding the known failure modes does not guarantee success; it only
eliminates some of the reliable paths to failure. There remain many ways to
be mediocre without committing any of the identifiable errors. The positive
case still needs to be made; inversion tests and refines it but does not replace it.

Munger acknowledges this: Berkshire's investment approach requires both
the positive analysis (finding genuinely good businesses at fair prices) and
the negative discipline (avoiding leverage, overactivity, and misunderstood
businesses). Inversion strengthens the negative side without displacing the
positive work.
