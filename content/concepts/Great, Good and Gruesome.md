# Great, Good and Gruesome

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 2007 letter

## Summary

In the 2007 letter, Buffett introduces a three-part taxonomy of businesses that he frames as three types of savings accounts. The analogy is precise: a savings account that earns a high rate while requiring no additional deposits is worth far more than one that earns adequately only if you keep feeding it money, which is in turn worth far more than one that pays a poor rate no matter how much you deposit. The same arithmetic applies to businesses.

The taxonomy cuts through a great deal of valuation noise. Revenue growth, brand recognition, and even accounting earnings can all mislead. The question that matters is how much incremental capital a business must consume to produce each additional dollar of earnings, and what rate of return it earns on that capital. Wonderful businesses are not always obvious; they often look modest. Terrible businesses are not always obvious either — airlines spent a century consuming capital at extraordinary scale while producing near-zero aggregate returns for shareholders.

## The three categories

**Great businesses** earn very high returns on capital and require little or no incremental capital to grow. Buffett's primary illustration is See's Candy, acquired in 1972 for $25 million. By 2007, See's was earning $82 million pre-tax on roughly $40 million of net tangible assets. Over 35 years, it had required only $32 million of additional capital — yet had produced $1.35 billion of cumulative pre-tax earnings. That $1.35 billion was entirely available for Berkshire to redeploy elsewhere. See's grew by raising prices and widening its brand, not by consuming capital. The return on incremental investment is effectively uncapped because incremental investment is barely required.

Buffett's near-miss with See's is itself instructive. He almost walked away from the purchase because the seller wanted $25 million and Buffett was reluctant to go above $25 million. Had he succeeded in paying $20 million less, he would have been $1.35 billion richer in cumulative earnings. Had the deal fallen through over that $5 million gap, the cost to Berkshire would have been, as Buffett put it, "incalculable" — a compounding machine forgone for the cost of one mediocre acquisition.

**Good businesses** earn attractive returns on capital but must reinvest substantial sums to grow. FlightSafety is Buffett's example. The business earns good returns, but simulators are expensive: since Berkshire's acquisition, FlightSafety has invested $1.635 billion in capital expenditures. Those investments earn good returns, but the earnings cannot simply be harvested and redeployed the way See's earnings can. The business grows by consuming capital, and the owner must keep funding that consumption to capture the growth.

Good businesses are worth owning, but they occupy a structurally different position from great ones. The capital they need tends to offset the compounding advantage — the owner must put money in to get money out, rather than simply collecting the surplus that flows from a business that runs itself.

**Gruesome businesses** require large and growing amounts of capital and earn low or negative returns on it. The airline industry is Buffett's definitive example. Since Kitty Hawk in 1903, airlines as a class have destroyed more capital than they have created. Routes expand, fleets expand, employees expand — capital consumption is relentless. Yet pricing power is minimal, competitors are numerous, and the aggregate return to shareholders across the industry's history has been catastrophic. Buffett's remark that "a farsighted capitalist present at Kitty Hawk would have done his successors a huge favour by shooting Orville down" is only partly a joke: the invention created an industry that consumed capital on a civilisational scale while producing near-zero aggregate returns to its owners.

## What it does

The taxonomy converts a qualitative observation — "this is a good business" — into an analytical discipline. It forces the question: good at what? A business that grows earnings impressively may be destroying value if it requires $2 of capital for every $1 of additional earnings it produces. A business that appears to stagnate may be creating extraordinary value if it demands no capital and generates cash freely.

The framework relates directly to [[Owner Earnings]]: what matters is not reported earnings but cash that can actually be taken out of the business or reinvested elsewhere without impairing future earnings. Great businesses have owner earnings that approximate or exceed reported earnings, because they require little capex beyond maintenance. Gruesome businesses have owner earnings that fall far short of reported earnings, because growth capex is massive and unavoidable.

The taxonomy also illuminates why intrinsic value and book value diverge so dramatically. A great business accumulates [[Economic Goodwill]] — excess earnings power above what the capital base would predict — that accounting never records. A gruesome business may carry a large book value (assets consumed but not written down) while its intrinsic value is a fraction of it.

Buffett's earlier work distinguishes [[Franchise vs Business Economics]] — businesses that possess pricing power from those that do not — which is a necessary but not sufficient condition for greatness. Many franchise businesses are good rather than great because they must still reinvest heavily to grow. The great business combines franchise economics with low capital intensity: it does not need capital to defend its position, and it does not need capital to expand it.

## Contradictions

The framework creates a mild tension with [[Long-Term Thinking]] as framed by Sleep, who argues that the best businesses often *choose* to reinvest heavily to strengthen their competitive positions — Scale Economics Shared being the archetype. Sleep's compounders look somewhat like Buffett's "good" category: attractive returns but ongoing capital consumption. The resolution is that reinvestment by choice into a business with high and rising returns on that capital is categorically different from reinvestment by necessity into a business earning low returns on it. Sleep's ideal is a business that could harvest cash freely but strategically chooses not to; Buffett's taxonomy is about what happens when that choice is removed.
