# Investment Risk vs Beta

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1993 letter, "Common Stock
Investments" section; extended in the 2006 letter, Walter Schloss tribute;
2008 and 2010 letters, Black-Scholes critique and long-dated options.

## Summary

The 1993 letter contains Buffett's most sustained critique of academic risk
theory and his most explicit alternative. The academic definition of
investment risk is the relative volatility of a stock compared to a market
index — beta. Buffett argues this definition is not merely incomplete but
actively misleading, and that the risk an investor should actually care about
is something else entirely: *"the possibility of loss or injury."* More
precisely, real investment risk is the possibility that aggregate after-tax
receipts from an investment will, over the investor's prospective holding
period, fail to give at least as much purchasing power as they had to begin
with, plus a modest rate of interest on the initial stake.

This is not a minor reframing. The two definitions produce opposite
conclusions in cases that matter.

## The beta paradox: Washington Post and Coke

When Berkshire purchased Washington Post shares in 1973, the stock had
dropped sharply compared to the market. By beta-based theory, this made it
*riskier* than before. *"Would that description have then made any sense to
someone who was offered the entire company at a vastly-reduced price?"*

The paradox runs the other way too. Coca-Cola and Gillette have betas
similar to those of ordinary, undistinguished businesses. Coke sells roughly
44% of all soft drinks worldwide; Gillette commands more than 60% of the
global blade market by value. These competitive positions had strengthened,
not weakened, in recent years. *"Should we conclude from this similarity
that the competitive strength of Coke and Gillette gains them nothing when
business risk is being measured?"* A beta that cannot distinguish between
such businesses and commodity businesses with no competitive protection is
measuring something other than the risk that matters.

The reason beta fails is that it treats price history as a proxy for
underlying business risk. Buffett argues the reverse relationship is the
correct one: a business with durable competitive advantages and stable
economics poses low real risk, and its price history is irrelevant to that
judgment. *"We'll happily forgo knowing the price history and instead will
seek whatever information will further our understanding of the company's
business."*

## Five factors bearing on real investment risk

The 1993 letter identifies five factors relevant to evaluating whether an
investment will preserve and grow purchasing power:

**First**, the certainty with which the long-term economic characteristics
of the business can be evaluated. A business operating in a stable industry
with durable competitive advantages is more evaluable than one facing rapid
technological change. Buffett and Munger *"can't determine whether we are
dealing with a 'pet rock' or a 'Barbie'"* in fast-moving industries, which
is why they avoid them. The circle of competence constraint is also a risk
management constraint.

**Second**, the certainty with which management's ability to realise the
full potential of the business can be evaluated. This is about whether
management will execute — not merely whether the business economics are
attractive.

**Third**, the certainty with which management can be counted on to channel
the rewards from the business to shareholders rather than to itself. A
business can have excellent economics and a capable manager yet still
produce poor investor outcomes if that manager systematically diverts value.
This connects directly to the [[Institutional Imperative]] and to the
compensation design problem.

**Fourth**, the purchase price of the business. A low purchase price
relative to intrinsic value is itself a risk-reduction mechanism, independent
of the quality of the business. This is the operational expression of the
[[Margin of Safety]] principle.

**Fifth**, the levels of taxation and inflation that will be experienced.
These reduce the purchasing-power return from any nominal gain.

These factors *"will probably strike many analysts as unbearably fuzzy, since
they cannot be extracted from a data base of any kind."* Buffett's response:
*"Just as Justice Stewart found it impossible to formulate a test for
obscenity but nevertheless asserted 'I know it when I see it,' so also can
investors — in an inexact but useful way — 'see' the risks inherent in certain
investments without reference to complex equations or price histories."*

## The error of false precision

Beta gives the appearance of precision. It is a number, calculable to
several decimal places from readily available data. The five factors above
are qualitative and resist quantification. Buffett does not treat this
asymmetry as a reason to prefer beta. The correct principle is: *"It is
better to be approximately right than precisely wrong."*

A beta purist evaluating a potential investment will *"disdain examining what
a company produces, what its competitors are doing, or how much borrowed
money the business employs. He may even prefer not to know the company's
name."* This is precisely backward from what an investor actually needs to
know. The number produced is precise; the thing it measures is not the right
thing.

This connects to the broader epistemological point about circle of competence:
restricting analysis to businesses one genuinely understands produces more
reliable estimates of real risk, even if those estimates cannot be compressed
into a single statistic. See [[Buffett's Four Criteria]].

## Concentration as a risk-reduction tool for the know-something investor

The 1993 letter also draws a distinction between two types of investor that
determines when concentration is appropriate:

The *know-nothing investor* — one who cannot evaluate the economics of
specific businesses — is correctly served by diversification, specifically by
periodically investing in an index fund. *"Paradoxically, when 'dumb' money
acknowledges its limitations, it ceases to be dumb."* For this investor,
diversification is not just fine; it is the correct strategy.

The *know-something investor* — one who can understand business economics
and identify *"five to ten sensibly-priced companies that possess important
long-term competitive advantages"* — operates under different logic.
*"Conventional diversification makes no sense for you. It is apt simply to
hurt your results and increase your risk."* The reason: concentration raises
the intensity with which the investor thinks about each business and the
comfort level required before buying. Both effects reduce real risk (the
possibility of a permanent loss of purchasing power), even as they increase
volatility.

*"I cannot understand why an investor of that sort elects to put money into
a business that is his 20th favourite rather than simply adding that money
to his top choices — the businesses he understands best and that present the
least risk, along with the greatest profit potential."*

The know-something/know-nothing distinction also applies to diversification
for genuinely risky single transactions. Where the risk in any individual
position cannot be analytically reduced — as in arbitrage, venture capital,
or genuinely opaque situations — a portfolio approach is correct: *"adopt
the outlook of the casino that owns a roulette wheel, which will want to see
lots of action because it is favored by probabilities, but will refuse to
accept a single, huge bet."* The logic for diversification applies only where
individual-position risk cannot be independently evaluated to a high degree
of confidence.

## What this means for volatility

Accepting that volatility is not risk has a practical implication: the
investor who correctly understands risk will *welcome* volatility, because
it periodically produces prices that diverge from value. *"The more manic-
depressive this chap [Mr. Market] is, the greater the opportunities available
to the investor."* See [[Mr. Market]].

The investor who equates volatility with risk will be tempted to act at
precisely the wrong moments: selling when prices drop (apparent "risk" rising)
and buying when prices rise (apparent "risk" falling). This is the
behavioural failure that the Mr. Market parable is designed to prevent.

## Walter Schloss as empirical refutation of EMT (2006)

The 2006 letter closes its investment-Helper critique with a tribute to Walter
Schloss that functions simultaneously as the most concrete available refutation
of the Efficient Market Theory and as a worked example of real-risk investing
rather than beta-based investing.

Schloss ran an investment partnership from 1956 to 2002 — 47 years. He held
no college degree. His office contained one file cabinet in 1956 and four by
2002; he employed no secretary, no clerk, no bookkeeper; his only associate was
his son Edwin. He and Edwin *"never came within a mile of inside information."*
They used publicly available information only sparingly, selecting securities
by simple statistical methods learned from Ben Graham: buying stocks that
appeared cheap by straightforward value measures. When asked in 1989 to
summarise their approach, Edwin replied: *"We try to buy stocks cheap."* The
investment philosophy requires no further elaboration.

Following *"a strategy that involved no real risk — defined as permanent loss
of capital — Walter produced results over his 47 partnership years that
dramatically surpassed those of the S&P 500."* The breadth of the record
matters: he typically held approximately 1,000 securities, spread across
lackluster types. A few large winners did not explain his record. Buffett
estimates that had millions of managers drawn stock names from a hat, bought
in amounts comparable to Schloss's, and sold when Schloss sold, the luckiest
of them would not have matched his record. *"There is simply no possibility
that what Walter achieved over 47 years was due to chance."*

Buffett first discussed Schloss's record publicly in 1984, when Efficient
Market Theory was the central tenet of business school investment education.
EMT, as then taught, held that no investor could be expected to outperform
using only publicly available information. Schloss was using only publicly
available information and had outperformed for nearly three decades. *"And
what did members of the academic community do when they were exposed to this
new and important evidence? Unfortunately, they reacted in all-too-human
fashion: Rather than opening their minds, they closed their eyes."*

No business school teaching EMT attempted to study Schloss's performance in
a way that might challenge the theory. Finance instructors who questioned EMT
had, Buffett notes, roughly the same chance of promotion as *"Galileo had of
being named Pope."* The practical consequence of the theory's dominance: tens
of thousands of students were sent into markets believing that evaluating
businesses was useless. Buffett's observation on the selection effect this
creates: *"After all, if you are in the shipping business, it's helpful to
have all of your potential competitors be taught that the earth is flat."*

The Schloss case bears on the five-factor real-risk framework from the 1993
letter in a specific way. Schloss's strategy was explicitly about avoiding
permanent loss of capital (factor four: purchase price relative to value;
factor one: economic evaluability through simple statistical screening). It
required no ability to forecast future earnings or evaluate competitive moats
with precision. The strategy worked not because Schloss had superior insight
into specific businesses but because he bought cheaply and held patiently,
giving price reversion time to operate. This is a different variant of the
real-risk framework — less reliant on qualitative business assessment, more
reliant on systematic cheapness — but it rests on the same foundational
rejection of volatility as risk.

## The Black-Scholes critique and the long-dated option paradox (2008–2010)

The 2008 letter extends the beta critique into a parallel domain: the widespread use of the Black-Scholes formula to price long-dated equity options. Berkshire had sold equity puts — contracts obligating it to purchase stock market indices at today's levels on specified future dates, some up to fifteen years out. The pricing of these contracts was set by Buffett's independent assessment rather than by Black-Scholes, and the difference was not trivial.

The core problem with Black-Scholes for long-dated options is that the formula was designed for short-duration contracts, where volatility-based assumptions are reasonable approximations. Applied to contracts spanning decades, the model produces *"wildly inappropriate values."* The mechanism is that Black-Scholes treats risk as increasing proportionally with time — the longer the option, the more the price can drift against the writer. But for equity markets specifically, the empirical record runs in the opposite direction: over any fifteen-year period in recorded history, the S&P 500 has never declined on a real-return basis. The risk of being long equities over very long time horizons is not captured by short-term volatility scaled upward.

The 2008 letter makes this concrete through a thought experiment about a 100-year put on the S&P 500. If someone had sold such a contract at the S&P's current level for exercise in 100 years, Black-Scholes would price it based on historical volatility and would produce a very large premium — implying a substantial probability that the S&P would be lower in 2108 than it is today. Buffett's argument: this is not a plausible risk for any investor with a coherent view of long-run economic development. The S&P 500 represents the productive capacity of the American economy; the claim that it would be lower in 100 years implies an economic regression without precedent in the historical record. An investor who truly believed the formula's implied probability distribution would, by the same logic, be predicting that US Treasury borrowing rates would fall to near zero over that period — the discount rate implied by the formula's risk-free assumption in that scenario is approximately 0.7%. This would mean the government could borrow essentially for free. No one believes this. The formula is producing numbers that are internally inconsistent with beliefs rational investors hold about adjacent quantities.

The 2010 letter develops the critique further. *"Beware of geeks bearing formulas."* The intellectual authority of a precise mathematical model can seduce boards, risk managers, and investors into treating the model's output as objective fact when the model's assumptions — particularly about the time-scaling of risk for long-duration instruments — are not applicable to the situation being priced. The preference Buffett states explicitly is *"approximately right rather than precisely wrong."* A qualitative assessment that the S&P will be higher in fifteen years than today, while unable to specify a confidence interval, is more practically useful than a formula that produces a large, precise, and probably incorrect value for a put premium on that question.

The connection to the broader beta critique is direct. Both errors arise from the same root: the use of short-term price variance as a proxy for long-term economic risk. Beta uses recent price volatility relative to the market; Black-Scholes uses historical volatility scaled by time. Neither has a well-grounded relationship to the thing an investor actually wants to know: what is the probability that this investment will produce a real return below the investor's minimum acceptable threshold over the investor's actual holding horizon? That question requires a view on underlying business economics — which is exactly what the academic frameworks attempt to avoid.
