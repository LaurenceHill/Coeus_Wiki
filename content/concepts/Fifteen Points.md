# Fifteen Points

**Source:** [[Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits]] — Chapter 3, "What to
Buy: The Fifteen Points to Look for in a Common Stock"

## Summary

Fisher's Fifteen Points are the complete checklist he developed for
evaluating whether a company is worth holding as a long-term growth
investment. They are not a mechanical scoring system. Fisher is explicit
that few companies will rate highly on all fifteen simultaneously and that
the investor must use judgement about which points matter most for a
given business. The list is a structured reminder of what to investigate —
a set of questions that, taken together, are designed to surface both the
genuine strengths and the hidden weaknesses of a company before a
position is taken.

The points divide roughly into two categories: those concerned with the
business and its competitive position, and those concerned with the people
running it. Fisher's view, stated repeatedly across Parts One and Two, is
that the two categories are equally important and that strong business
characteristics without strong management, or strong management in a
poor business, are both insufficient. The fifteen points operationalise this
dual requirement.

## The fifteen points

**1. Does the company have products or services with sufficient market
potential to make possible a sizable increase in sales for at least several
years?**

Fisher is looking for companies that can grow their sales substantially over
many years, not just in the current cycle. He distinguishes between
companies in markets that are still expanding (where growth requires only
execution) and companies in mature markets (where growth requires taking
share from competitors at higher cost). The former are vastly preferable.

**2. Does the management have a determination to continue to develop
products or processes that will still further increase total sales potentials
when the growth potentials of currently attractive product lines have largely
been exploited?**

Point 1 asks whether the current market is large enough. Point 2 asks
whether management has the discipline to replace that market when it
matures. Fisher regards the willingness to invest in next-generation products
before the current ones have peaked as one of the most reliable indicators of
long-term management quality. Companies that wait until existing products
are declining before beginning to develop replacements are structurally late.

**3. How effective are the company's research and development efforts in
relation to its size?**

Research effectiveness is not the same as research spending. Fisher wants to
know whether the research department produces commercially viable
products efficiently, not merely whether it is large. He notes that some
companies with large R&D budgets produce relatively little of commercial
value, while others produce disproportionately with modest outlays. The
relationship between the research department and the commercial side of
the business — whether scientists are thinking about markets as well as
problems — is a key indicator.

**4. Does the company have an above-average sales organisation?**

Fisher's formulation here is unusually strong: even the best product will not
produce maximum returns without an excellent sales force. He regards
sales effectiveness as an underappreciated differentiator, partly because it
is invisible in the accounts and partly because many technically-oriented
companies underinvest in it. An above-average sales organisation means not
just that the company can sell existing products but that it can introduce new
ones effectively and maintain relationships through product transitions.

**5. Does the company have a worthwhile profit margin?**

The question is not merely whether margins are currently high but whether
they are defensible. Fisher is looking for companies whose margins reflect
genuine competitive advantage — proprietary products, cost leadership, or
customer relationships — rather than temporary conditions. A company with
high margins for accidental reasons (a competitor who has not yet entered,
a commodity cycle at its peak) is a different animal from one whose margins
are structural.

**6. What is the company doing to maintain or improve profit margins?**

Point 5 asks what margins are; point 6 asks what management is doing
about them. Fisher wants to know specifically about cost-reduction
programmes, pricing strategy, and the pipeline of lower-cost processes
being developed. A company that is not actively working to maintain its
cost position is likely to see margins erode as competitors improve.

**7. Does the company have outstanding labour and personnel relations?**

Fisher's reasoning here is partly operational and partly diagnostic. A
company with poor labour relations will suffer higher costs and periodic
disruptions. But more importantly, poor relations are a diagnostic indicator
of management quality. Companies that treat employees as costs to be
minimised rather than assets to be developed tend to underperform over long
periods. Fisher notes that the best companies he knows of are not necessarily
the highest-paying ones, but they consistently score highly on measures of
whether employees feel heard, treated fairly, and given reasonable
opportunity for advancement.

**8. Does the company have outstanding executive relations?**

Distinct from labour relations, this point concerns the relationship between
top management and the executives two or three levels below. Fisher is
looking for evidence that executive talent is developed internally, that
capable people are retained, and that authority is genuinely delegated rather
than concentrated at the top. A company where the chief executive makes all
significant decisions and the layer beneath is purely administrative will not
scale beyond the capacity of one person.

**9. Does the company have depth to its management?**

This is the succession question. Fisher wants to know not just whether
current management is excellent but whether there are capable people at
lower levels who could take over key roles if necessary. Companies with
thin management depth are fragile: the departure of a few key people can
impair performance significantly. Companies with genuine depth in the
management team are more robust to individual departures and to the
growth that will require delegating more authority as the company scales.

**10. How good are the company's cost analysis and accounting controls?**

Fisher is looking for the presence of accurate internal cost accounting —
the ability of management to know, by product line and by activity, where
costs are running high or low and to act on that information. Companies
without this capability cannot efficiently allocate capital among competing
uses and cannot identify where operational improvement is most needed.
Fisher treats the absence of good cost controls as a disqualifying defect
because it means management is making investment decisions with
inadequate information.

**11. Are there other aspects of the business, somewhat peculiar to the
industry involved, that will give the investor important clues as to how
outstanding the company may be in relation to its competition?**

This is Fisher's deliberately open-ended point — an acknowledgement that
every industry has its own key success factors that do not appear on a
generic checklist. For a pharmaceutical company it might be the quality of
the regulatory submission pipeline. For a retailer it might be inventory
turn. Fisher is prompting the investor to think about what the one or two
most important competitive determinants are in the specific industry and
then to investigate those specifically, rather than treating the checklist as
exhaustive.

**12. Does the company have a short-range or long-range outlook in regard
to profits?**

Fisher's concern here is whether management is willing to incur near-term
costs for long-term benefit — to invest in products whose payoffs are years
away, to recruit people ahead of the need, to spend on training that will not
improve this year's results. Companies managed for quarterly earnings tend
to underinvest in exactly the activities that produce above-average long-run
growth. This point is the precondition for points 1, 2, and 3: a company
that will not sacrifice near-term profits to develop future products cannot
sustain growth through multiple product cycles.

**13. In the foreseeable future will the growth of the company necessitate
sufficient equity financing so that the larger number of shares then
outstanding will largely cancel the existing stockholders' benefit from this
anticipated growth?**

Fisher's concern is dilution. A company that must repeatedly issue equity to
fund its growth transfers value from existing shareholders to new ones. The
ideal company generates enough internal cash flow to finance its growth
without meaningful dilution, or can borrow at rates that leave the equity
economics intact. This point anticipates the Bezos preference for [[Free Cash Flow Over Earnings]]: a business that grows from its own cash
generation is structurally superior to one that must perpetually return to the
capital markets.

**14. Does management talk freely to investors about its affairs when things
are going well but "clam up" when troubles and difficulties develop?**

Fisher treats this as a diagnostic of integrity rather than merely a question
about disclosure. Management that is forthcoming about bad news is
management that is actively monitoring the business for problems,
acknowledging them early, and thinking clearly about remediation. Management
that goes quiet when things go poorly is management that either is not in
control of the situation or is not willing to be held accountable for it.
Fisher also notes that companies which clam up typically offer investors an
implicit premium for the uncertainty — their stocks tend to trade at
discounts that can be attractive once the problem is understood — but that
the discounts are often justified.

**15. Does the company have a management of unquestionable integrity?**

Fisher places this last but regards it as foundational. The reasoning is
structural: management is always closer to the facts of the business than
outside investors are. Without genuine integrity, all other positive
characteristics of a company are at risk of being diverted for management's
benefit at the expense of shareholders. Fisher notes that the absence of
integrity is rarely visible until it has already caused damage; the time to
evaluate it is before an investment is made, through conversations with
former employees, customers, competitors, and others who have had direct
dealings with the people involved.

## How the points work together

Fisher warns against two misapplications. The first is treating the list as a
scoring mechanism: a company that rates 12 out of 15 is not necessarily
better than one that rates 9 out of 15, because the weight of any given point
depends on the business. A retail company that rates poorly on point 3
(research and development) may still be excellent; a pharmaceutical company
that rates poorly on the same point is probably disqualified. The second
misapplication is using the checklist as a substitute for judgement. Fisher
intends the points as a structured guide to what questions to ask, not as a
set of criteria that, if checked off, guarantee a good investment.

The fifteen points address two distinct risks. Points 1 through 6 and 11
address the risk that the business itself is not genuinely superior. Points 7
through 10 and 12 through 15 address the risk that the business is being run
by people who will not develop, or will divert, its potential. A company
that passes on the business criteria but fails on the people criteria is not a
safe investment; a company that passes on people criteria but operates in a
structurally mediocre business is unlikely to produce exceptional returns.
Both conditions must be satisfied.

## Relationship to the Four Dimensions

Part Two of the source (*Conservative Investors Sleep Well*) refactors the
Fifteen Points into four dimensions without substantively changing the
underlying thinking. Points 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 10 map broadly onto the first
dimension (operating excellence). Points 7, 8, 9, 12, 14, and 15 map onto
the second dimension (people and management quality). Points 11 and 13
are partially addressed by the third dimension (inherent business
characteristics). The fourth dimension — the financial community's
appraisal — is not represented in the Fifteen Points at all; it is the new
contribution of the 1975 work (see [[Financial Community Appraisal]]).
