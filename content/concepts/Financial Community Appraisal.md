# Financial Community Appraisal

**Source:** [[Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits]] — Part Two (*Conservative
Investors Sleep Well*), Chapters 4–6; Part Three, "Is the Market Efficient?"
and the Raychem Corporation case study; extended in [[Paths to Wealth Through Common Stocks]] — Part I.E and Part II

## Summary

Fisher's central claim about stock prices, stated as a rule in Part Two:
*"Every significant price move of any individual common stock in relation
to stocks as a whole occurs because of a changed appraisal of that stock by
the financial community."* This is not merely an observation about market
psychology. It is Fisher's complete theory of what determines the relative
price of any given stock at any given moment — and therefore what the
investor must understand to find stocks that are genuinely cheap or genuinely
expensive.

The financial community's appraisal is not the same as the underlying facts
of the business. Appraisals can diverge from facts for months or years in
either direction. They eventually correct, but the timing of correction is
unpredictable. The investor with a better understanding of the underlying
facts than the financial community has at a given moment is positioned to
benefit from the eventual correction, provided she can hold through the
period during which the appraisal remains wrong.

## The three-part appraisal

The financial community's appraisal of any individual stock is a composite
of three separate appraisals:

**1. The appraisal of the company itself.** This is the most important of the
three for determining the relative price-earnings ratio of one stock against
another. It reflects the financial community's current assessment of the
company's management quality, competitive position, growth prospects, and
financial characteristics — broadly speaking, its assessment of the first
three dimensions of conservative investment. The more closely this
appraisal approaches what Fisher describes in the [[Fifteen Points]], the
higher the price-earnings ratio will be. The degree by which the company
falls below those standards, the lower the ratio will tend to be.

**2. The appraisal of the industry.** The financial community assigns
price-earnings ratios partly on an industry basis, reflecting its current
assessment of the long-run attractiveness of the business environment.
Industry appraisals can shift significantly based on changes in fashion
rather than changes in fact. Fisher's extended example is the American
chemical industry: in the 1950s chemical stocks commanded high P/E ratios
because the financial community associated the industry with breathtaking
new compounds and expanding markets. In the 1960s the same industry sold
at materially lower ratios, not because the underlying characteristics of
the business had changed, but because the prevailing image had shifted
toward seeing chemicals as a capital-intensive commodity business. "What
had shifted was the emphasis, not the facts."

**3. The appraisal of stocks as a whole.** The general level of the market
reflects both the financial community's overall assessment of common stocks
as an investment class and certain real-world financial factors, of which
interest rates are the most important. When interest rates are high, capital
migrates toward fixed-income markets and the overall demand for stocks
declines; when rates are low, the reverse occurs. Fisher treats the general
market level as a real factor — unlike the company and industry appraisals,
which are purely subjective — because it is partly driven by the actual
mechanics of capital flows rather than purely by opinion.

## Why appraisals diverge from facts

Fisher's explanation for why appraisals can be spectacularly wrong for
extended periods is not about irrationality in the pejorative sense. It is about
the inherent difficulty of assessing complex businesses from the outside,
the lag between the development of underlying conditions and the
publication of evidence that would allow an outside observer to evaluate
them, and the tendency of the financial community to follow consensus
opinions that were formed on the basis of earlier evidence.

The chemical industry example illustrates this: the unfavourable factors
that dominated the industry's image in the 1960s — capital intensity,
commodity pricing — had always been present. They were simply not
emphasised when the image was dominated by the excitement of new
compounds. Similarly, the favourable factors — the continuing opportunity
to produce proprietary higher-margin products from commodity inputs —
were present throughout the period of lower P/E ratios but were not
emphasised. The facts did not change; the emphasis did.

Fisher adds that appraisals tend to overshoot in both directions. A company
that has been seen as excellent will sometimes trade at a premium that
exceeds even what strong fundamentals justify. When the premium is
reversed — whether by a genuine disappointment or by simple exhaustion
of the buyers who held the old image — the new appraisal frequently
overestimates the negative, producing a price that is materially below what
the facts warrant. This overshooting is not irrational in a simple sense; it
reflects the emotional dynamic of falling prices, which causes a re-
examination of fundamentals under conditions that tend to weight the
negative. The patient investor who can read the underlying facts without
being influenced by the direction of the price enjoys an advantage in exactly
this situation.

## Company G and the 400% gain

Fisher's illustration of a correctly re-rated company: Company G was
regarded as ordinary, earning $1 per share and trading at ten times earnings.
Over two years, through a combination of new products and improved
margins, the company grew earnings to $1.82 while competitors in the same
industry showed declining profits. The financial community re-appraised the
company, the P/E moved from 10x to 22x, and the stock rose 400%. The
underlying business improvements had been in progress for some time
before the market recognised them; the investment opportunity lay in
identifying those improvements before the re-rating occurred.

Fisher's generalisation: the largest gains available in common stocks come
from the combined effect of growing per-share earnings and a simultaneous
expansion of the price-earnings ratio. The earnings growth alone would have
produced a meaningful return. The P/E expansion — the moment at which
the financial community's appraisal catches up with the underlying facts —
produces the exceptional return. Investors who hold through the period when
the facts are visible but the appraisal has not yet moved capture both.

## The risk hierarchy

Fisher uses the fourth dimension to construct a hierarchy of investment risk,
running from least to most dangerous:

Least risky: a company with genuinely strong first-three-dimension
characteristics that the financial community is currently undervaluing. The
investor is paying below what the facts warrant and will benefit from two
sources of return — the growth of the underlying business and the
correction of the appraisal.

Moderately risky: a company with strong characteristics whose appraisal
is roughly in line with those characteristics. The investor pays a fair price
and benefits from ongoing growth, without the additional tailwind of a
re-rating. Still a suitable investment; simply requires patience rather than
the additional element of the appraisal correction.

Retention-worthy but not purchase-worthy: a company with strong
characteristics whose appraisal has become legendary and whose P/E ratio
is somewhat above what even strong fundamentals justify. Fisher argues
these should usually be retained (the switching risk into a new situation
is often greater than the overvaluation risk in the existing holding), but
should not be purchased with new funds.

Most dangerous: a company whose appraisal is far above what current facts
justify. This is the source of the largest losses. Fisher's list of examples
from the 1970s bear market — Memorex, Ampex, Levitz Furniture, Mohawk
Data Sciences — illustrates what happens when expectations embedded in
a very high P/E fail to materialise.

## The captive balloon model

In *Paths to Wealth Through Common Stocks*, Fisher refines the mechanics of how the appraisal works through the image of a captive balloon. The price of a stock at any moment resembles a balloon tethered not to the ground but to a moving line — the line of intrinsic value. As the company's earning power and underlying prospects improve, the intrinsic value line rises; if they deteriorate, it falls. At any given moment, the psychological attitudes of the financial community — mass enthusiasm, fear, or indifference — cause the price to float anywhere from well above the intrinsic value line to well below it. But the tether holds: the price can never permanently escape the line. Sooner or later it is pulled back.

Fisher is careful to acknowledge that intrinsic value cannot be pinpointed precisely — it is, in his words, "a broad line, not a number." Comparing a company with others of similar growth rate and quality can establish a range, but not an exact figure. For rapidly growing companies commanding high P/E ratios, the difficulty is greater still, because so much of the value depends on how long an abnormal growth rate will persist, and the further one projects into the future the wider the margin of error becomes. His practical instruction: if a stock is within 25–30% of a reasonable estimate of intrinsic value and the underlying growth is genuine, the precision problem is not material. The danger is paying prices several times real worth — the territory where psychological overenthusiasm has stretched the balloon so far from the intrinsic value line that the tether snaps.

## P/E ratio alone tells you nothing

Building on the captive balloon model, Fisher makes an argument in *Paths to Wealth* that corrects a common misreading of his framework. Whether a stock is selling at a low P/E ratio or a high one has nothing by itself to do with whether it is cheap or expensive. The vastly different quality and growth prospects of different companies fully justify wide variations in P/E ratios. The only thing that matters is whether the *facts* warrant the current ratio.

This has two practical implications that the appraisal framework produces. A stock selling at a very high P/E ratio may still be materially undervalued if the financial community has yet to recognise the full extent of the company's growth trajectory — the balloon is below the intrinsic value line despite being nominally "expensive." Conversely, a stock at a very low P/E ratio may be precisely appropriately priced or even overpriced if its economics are poor and the financial community is already too optimistic about its limited prospects. The investor's task is to assess whether the current appraisal appropriately reflects the facts, not to use the P/E ratio as a shortcut.

## P/E expansion and the institutional acceptance cycle

The *Paths to Wealth* treatment of [[Institutional Buying]] provides a structural mechanism that explains the most dramatic form of Financial Community Appraisal change: the transition from an unapproved to an institutionally accepted stock. Fisher illustrates with a detailed example of a fictional but realistic company (Pioneer Metals Corporation) that begins as an obscure small fabricator and ends as a recognised institutional holding. 

The arithmetic Fisher develops: in a company that doubles its earnings *and* has its P/E ratio expand from 12x to 24x as institutional recognition is earned, the investor captures a 4x gain where a pure earnings analysis would suggest only a 2x gain. The P/E expansion is, in Fisher's phrase, "the most overlooked source of major investment gain" — and it is produced by a change in Financial Community Appraisal, not by a change in the underlying business. The underlying business provides the ground for the appraisal shift; but the shift itself is a psychological and institutional event.

This is the positive version of the risk hierarchy: the Company G example earlier in this page illustrates a single re-rating event. The Pioneer Metals / institutional acceptance cycle shows how a company can undergo a *series* of re-rating events over a decade or more, each one driven by a wider circle of financial community participants recognising what was always there in the underlying facts.

A Boston trust company quote Fisher records captures the priority well: "Management is 90 per cent, the industry is 9 per cent, and all other factors are 1 per cent." The statement is not mathematically exact, but it correctly identifies that Financial Community Appraisal of a company is primarily a question about management — and that the investor who can correctly assess management quality before the financial community has formalised that assessment is positioned to capture the appraisal correction as it occurs.

## The Efficient Market fallacy

Part Three contains Fisher's explicit rejection of the Efficient Market
Hypothesis. The EMH as understood in the 1970s held that stock prices at
any moment reflect all publicly available information, so that only illegal
insider information could provide an informational edge. Fisher's case against
this is empirical rather than theoretical.

The Raychem Corporation: in 1975–76, Raychem was hit by two setbacks
— the failure of a major research project (the Stilan polymer) and a planned
management reorganisation that temporarily reduced earnings. The stock
fell from over $42 to approximately $15. The Raychem management was
forthcoming about both developments, explaining the temporary nature of
the earnings impact to any investor who asked. On January 26, 1978, at a
public meeting attended by representatives of all interested institutions and
brokers, senior management laid out in unusual detail the company's
prospects, problems, and current status. Over the following year or two,
earnings grew exactly as described at that meeting, and the stock more than
doubled from the meeting-day price of $23. Yet in the weeks immediately
following the meeting, the stock showed no particular movement. Investors
who were present, who heard the same presentation, and who had access to
the same information the management provided, did not act on it — because
they were still under the influence of the double shock of the prior two
years and mistrusted what they were being told.

Fisher's conclusion: the market is not efficient in the sense that matters for
the long-term investor. It is perhaps efficient for the short-term trader,
for whom the difficulty of extracting consistent profit from rapid in-and-out
transactions is real. But for the investor who holds through multi-year
periods, the financial community's appraisal of a company at any given
moment frequently misrepresents the underlying facts, sometimes
spectacularly. The investor who can assess those facts more accurately,
and hold through the period of mispricing, is in a position to capture the
difference.

## Relationship to other sources

All three other major sources in this wiki — Buffett, Sleep, and Bezos —
implicitly share Fisher's scepticism of the Efficient Market Hypothesis,
though none of them addresses it as directly.

Buffett's most sustained treatment appears in the 1988 letter, where he
delivers a direct refutation of EMT using the record of investors in the
Graham-and-Doddsville tradition. His epistemological critique is precise:
proponents of EMT "observing correctly that the market was frequently
efficient...went on to conclude incorrectly that it was always efficient.
The difference between these propositions is night and day." The 63-year
arbitrage record of investors consistently exploiting appraisal gaps — the
same divergence between price and underlying value that Fisher's framework
describes — is Buffett's empirical counter-evidence. Buffett's [[Mr. Market]] parable (1987 letter) is the psychological mechanism underlying
the same observation: a financial community with incurable emotional
problems will periodically misprice securities, creating the appraisal gaps
that patient investors exploit.

The 1987 letter also adds a structural reason why the financial community's
appraisal of CEO quality is systematically unreliable: most CEOs rise
through functional excellence in marketing, engineering, or manufacturing,
none of which develops capital allocation skill. Once in the CEO role, they
face a responsibility — deploying retained earnings — that they have never
practised. Their first-order response is often to turn to investment bankers
and consultants whose structural incentives favour activity. The result is
that the financial community appraises management quality on the basis of
functional track records that may be irrelevant to the one decision —
capital allocation — that actually compounds or destroys value at scale. This
is the corporate-governance dimension of the appraisal problem and is
developed further in the [[Institutional Imperative]] page.

Sleep's [[Patience as Competitive Advantage]] similarly depends on the
observation that the financial community's appraisal of a business's
long-run prospects is frequently wrong, and that the investor who can hold
for years without being forced to sell by client redemptions or institutional
mandates is positioned to benefit from the eventual correction.

Fisher's contribution relative to these parallels is the explicit structural
analysis of what the appraisal consists of (company + industry + general
market), why it diverges from facts (lag, emphasis shifts, emotional
dynamics), and how to use that divergence to construct a risk hierarchy
for investment decisions.
