# Earnings Integrity

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1997 and 1998 letters;
extended in 2000, 2001, 2002, and 2005 letters; 2008 and 2009 letters,
investment confessions and option-expensing Senate-vote history; 2016 and
2018 letters, mark-to-market GAAP rule and operating earnings as the only
reliable metric; 2022 letter, "bold imaginative accounting" and GAAP
quarterly acrobatics table; 2023 letter, "worse-than-useless" and EBITDA
ban; 2024 letter, operating earnings record

## Summary

Earnings integrity is Buffett's term — implicit if not always explicit — for the gap between what corporations report and what is economically true. The 1997 and 1998 letters contain his most sustained critique of the accounting conventions and managerial incentives that systematically corrupt reported earnings. The argument is not that accounting is always wrong but that a significant and growing number of managers treat reported figures as an output to be manufactured rather than a measurement to be made honestly.

The critique runs on two tracks: option expensing, where accounting convention fails by design, and earnings management more broadly, where managers and auditors actively exploit the flexibility that convention provides.

## The option expensing problem

Standard accounting, as of the 1998 letter, ignored the cost of stock options when calculating earnings. Buffett identifies this as "an Alice-in-Wonderland outcome." Options are compensation. Compensation is an expense. Expenses belong in earnings calculations. The logic is unanswerable, and he poses it as three questions: *"If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?"*

The absurdity is made vivid by analogy. Suppose Berkshire, rather than paying cash for its $190 million GEICO advertising budget, paid the media in ten-year at-the-money Berkshire options. Would anyone argue that no advertising cost had been incurred? Bishop Berkeley — the philosopher who wondered whether a tree falling unobserved made a sound — would believe that an expense unseen by an accountant does not exist. Buffett does not share this faith.

The practical consequence for Berkshire's investment process is material: *"The earning revisions that Charlie and I have made for options in recent years have frequently cut reported per-share figures by 5%, with 10% not all that uncommon. On occasion, the downward adjustment has been so great that it has affected our portfolio decisions, causing us either to make a sale or to pass on a stock purchase we might otherwise have made."* This is not a theoretical complaint — it is a standing haircut applied to reported earnings before any valuation work begins.

The political history of option expensing illuminates why the practice persisted so long. In 1994 the Senate voted, under intense lobbying pressure from corporate America, to discourage the Financial Accounting Standards Board from requiring option expensing. Following that vote, the FASB retreated. Buffett notes the result: out of approximately 500 S&P companies, only about two voluntarily chose to expense their options. The near-unanimous refusal was not a coincidence — managers who would have had to disclose the true cost of their own compensation plans predictably declined to do so when exempted from compulsion. The 94 Senate vote is the clearest historical illustration that the option-expensing failure was not an innocent accounting oversight but a deliberately maintained concealment.

## Earnings management and "Son of Gresham"

Beyond options, Buffett identifies a broader cultural decay: *"Bad accounting drives out good."* Once the view that earnings management is acceptable — that everybody does it — takes hold among a management cohort, ethical resistance collapses. He calls this Son of Gresham.

The instrument most often used is the restructuring charge. A large one-time charge is taken in a quarter already expected to disappoint, absorbing years of costs into a single period. Future periods are then shown clean of those costs, producing a run of quarterly earnings that consistently "beats expectations" by a few cents. The mechanism is illustrated by a golf scorecard analogy: a golfer who turns in a 140 for his first round, calls it "restructuring his swing," and thereafter counts only his good holes will show a record of 80, 80, 80, 80 rather than his real 91, 94, 89, 94, 92. Wall Street ignores the 140 as stemming from a "discontinued" swing and grades him as an 80-shooter.

Buffett is careful to distinguish this from honest mistakes. The problem is purposeful manipulation: *"In the acquisition arena, restructuring has been raised to an art form: Managements now frequently use mergers to dishonestly rearrange the value of assets and liabilities in ways that will allow them to both smooth and swell future earnings."*

## Auditor capture

A structural problem compounds the managerial one: auditors are selected and paid by the management they are supposed to scrutinise. *"Whose bread I eat, his song I sing."* Auditors who bless aggressive accounting face few professional consequences so long as the practice is widespread, but auditors who challenge management risk losing the engagement. The system's incentives point in one direction.

Major auditing firms have at deal time actually pointed out the possibilities for "accounting magic." First-class people will frequently stoop to third-class tactics when the strategy is auditor-blessed and benefits the managers who have retained them.

## The CEO/advisor dynamic

A related pattern in acquisitions: once a CEO has decided on a transaction, staff and outside advisors will produce whatever projections are needed to justify the stance. *"Only in fairy tales are emperors told that they are naked."* This is not a cynical observation about individual dishonesty — it is a structural one about hierarchy. People whose economic interests depend on pleasing the decision-maker will filter their analysis to match the preferred conclusion. The defence is not better advisors but more honest self-interrogation by the principal.

## What Berkshire does instead

Berkshire does not manage earnings. Buffett states plainly: *"If we are to disappoint you, we would rather it be with our earnings than with our accounting."* In acquisitions, Berkshire leaves loss reserve figures exactly as found. When it acquires an option-issuing company, it promptly substitutes a cash compensation plan of equivalent economic value, bringing the true cost out of hiding. The goal is to give shareholders the information that Berkshire would want if their positions were reversed.

## Bad terminology is the enemy of good thinking (2001)

The 2001 letter broadens the accounting-language critique beyond options and
restructuring charges. *"Bad terminology is the enemy of good thinking. When
companies or investment professionals use terms such as 'EBITDA' and 'pro
forma,' they want you to unthinkingly accept concepts that are dangerously
flawed."*

"Loss development" is the specific example. When an insurer finds it had
reserved too little for past losses, the industry describes the correction as
losses "developing." But the losses did not develop — they were there all
along. *"What developed was management's understanding of the losses (or, in
the instances of chicanery, management's willingness to finally fess up)."*
The honest label would be: "loss costs we failed to recognise when they
occurred." The euphemistic label obscures a management error as a natural
event. Insurance investors who accept "loss development" as a routine feature
of the business are training themselves to underreact to a concrete signal of
poor reserving.

The companion term "reserve strengthening" is equally distorted: it implies
that adequate reserves are being further buttressed, rather than that a
systematic underestimate is being corrected. Buffett proposes "oops" as a
more accurate substitute.

## CEO growth predictions as corruption vector (2000)

The 2000 letter extends the integrity argument into forward guidance. *"It is
both deceptive and dangerous for CEOs to predict growth rates for their
companies."* The danger is not primarily that predictions will be wrong — it
is that the commitment to hit a number corrupts subsequent behaviour.

The sequence Buffett describes: a CEO announces a 15% annual growth target →
operations fall short → the CEO exhausts all legitimate operating adjustments
trying to reach the number → having exhausted those, the CEO turns to
accounting adjustments → accounting adjustments snowball because each
shortfall requires the next to be "more heroic" → the cumulative adjustment
becomes fraud. *"More money, it has been noted, has been stolen with the
point of a pen than at the point of a gun."*

The practical heuristic: *"Charlie and I tend to be leery of companies run by
CEOs who woo investors with fancy predictions."* Of those who make and
consistently hit ambitious forecasts, some are genuinely prophetic; others are
manipulating. The investor who cannot distinguish the two would be better off
applying a blanket discount to guidance from any company that regularly
provides tight numerical targets.

## EBITDA and the tooth fairy (2000, 2002)

The 2000 letter delivers Buffett's most memorable formulation of the EBITDA
critique: *"References to EBITDA make us shudder — does management think the
tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?"*

The 2002 letter provides the analytical elaboration. Depreciation is not
merely a non-cash charge to be added back; it represents a real economic
cost, and a peculiarly front-loaded one:

> *"In truth, depreciation is a particularly unattractive expense because the
> cash outlay it represents is paid up front, before the asset acquired has
> delivered any benefits to the business. Imagine, if you will, that at the
> beginning of this year a company paid all of its employees for the next ten
> years of their service (in the way they would lay out cash for a fixed asset
> to be useful for ten years). In the following nine years, compensation would
> be a 'non-cash' expense — a reduction of a prepaid compensation asset
> established this year. Would anyone care to argue that the recording of the
> expense in years two through ten would be simply a bookkeeping formality?"*

The prepaid-wages analogy makes the fallacy vivid: adding back depreciation
to reach "cash flow" is exactly equivalent to arguing that nine years of
salaries are free because they were prepaid. No analyst would accept that
argument for compensation; the consistent application of the same logic to
capital assets produces a number that understates true costs just as
reliably. See [[Owner Earnings]] for the formal treatment.

## Selective disclosure and full reporting (2000)

The 2000 letter extends the integrity argument to the distribution of
information. Selective disclosure — the practice of guiding analysts and large
holders to earnings expectations slightly below what management truly expected
— had become *"virtually standard practice"* by 2000. Through *"selectively
dispersed hints, winks and nods,"* speculatively-minded institutions gained an
information edge over investment-oriented individuals. Buffett applauds the
SEC's crackdown but notes the revealing detail: *"the fact that this reform
came about because of coercion rather than conscience should be a matter of
shame for CEOs and their investor relations departments."*

Berkshire's counter-practice: post annual and quarterly financials to the
internet on Friday evening, so that all shareholders have the same
information simultaneously before markets open Monday.

## Four audit committee questions (2002)

The 2002 letter proposes four specific questions that audit committees should
ask auditors before each earnings release, treating the answers as prophylactics
rather than post-mortems:

1. If the auditor were solely responsible for preparation of the financial
   statements, would they have been prepared any differently? (Both material
   and nonmaterial differences should be covered.)
2. If the auditor were an investor, would he have received — in plain English
   — the information essential to understanding the company's financial
   performance?
3. Is the company following the same internal audit procedure that the auditor
   himself would follow as CEO? If not, what are the differences and why?
4. Is the auditor aware of any actions — accounting or operational — that have
   had the purpose and effect of moving revenues or expenses from one
   reporting period to another?

The mechanism matters: if auditors know they must affirmatively endorse
rather than merely acquiesce to management's choices, they will resist
misdoings early, before specious figures become embedded in the books.
Fear of personal liability, under these conditions, works in the right
direction.

## Three investor heuristics (2002)

The 2002 letter closes with three compressed diagnostics for investors
assessing corporate reporting:

First, *"beware of companies displaying weak accounting. If a company still
does not expense options, or if its pension assumptions are fanciful, watch
out. When managements take the low road in aspects that are visible, it is
likely they are following a similar path behind the scenes. There is seldom
just one cockroach in the kitchen."*

Second, unintelligible footnotes usually indicate untrustworthy management.
*"If you can't understand a footnote or other managerial explanation, it's
usually because the CEO doesn't want you to."*

Third, be suspicious of companies that trumpet earnings projections and growth
expectations. *"Managers that always promise to 'make the numbers' will at
some point be tempted to make up the numbers."*

## Berkshire's inverse pro-forma (2002)

In a gesture of deliberate contrast, the 2002 letter announces that Berkshire
will disclose its own pro-forma earnings at *lower* than reported. Two
favourable factors — absence of a megacatastrophe and bond-market gains in the
finance business — inflated reported earnings. In a year when virtually every
other major company was reporting pro-forma earnings that exceeded reported
ones, Buffett volunteers a downward adjustment. *"We've yet to see a
pro-forma presentation disclosing that audited earnings were somewhat high.
So let's make a little history."*

## The Fred Futile case: options and the hidden dividend-withholding incentive (2005)

The 2005 letter makes the option-accounting critique concrete through a
specific worked example that moves beyond the abstract assertion that options
are expenses. The fictional CEO is Fred Futile, running Stagnant Inc., who
holds a ten-year fixed-price option on 1% of the company.

Fred's rational self-interest under this plan is to skip dividends entirely
and repurchase shares with all retained earnings. The arithmetic: Stagnant
earns $1 billion annually on $10 billion of net worth, or $10 per share on
100 million shares. Over ten years of compounding repurchases at a constant
P/E of 10, shares outstanding fall to 38.7 million, earnings per share rise
to $25.80, and the stock appreciates 158%. Fred collects $158 million on his
1% option — *"despite the business itself improving not at all."* Fred could
have collected more than $100 million even if Stagnant's earnings fell 20%
during the period.

The mechanism is fundamental, not technical. A fixed-price option is a free
call on the automatic per-share value build-up that occurs when earnings are
retained. The higher the retention rate, the more valuable the option becomes,
independent of any improvement in the underlying business. This is an
incentive structure that is simultaneously anti-shareholder (shareholders
receive no dividends and no return on the retained capital) and pro-CEO
(options become more valuable precisely because capital is retained). The
conflict is not trivial: *"CEOs understand this math and know that every dime
paid out in dividends reduces the value of all outstanding options. I've never,
however, seen this manager-owner conflict referenced in proxy materials that
request approval of a fixed-priced option plan."*

The remedy Buffett proposes is a strike price that rises each year to reflect
retained earnings, removing the artificial benefit of retention. Boards
could design such options easily. They almost never do.

## Pay-for-performance as the only honest standard: Hank Greenberg (2005)

The 2005 letter offers a single image that summarises what the alternative to
the ratchet-and-comparative-data compensation process looks like. Hank
Greenberg was the Detroit Tigers slugger and Buffett's boyhood hero. When
Hank's son Steve, then a player's agent, asked his father what signing bonus
to request for an outfielder he was representing, Hank asked what the player
had hit the prior year. Steve answered ".246." Hank's response was immediate:
*"Ask for a uniform."*

The standard is binary in spirit: performance determines pay, and the
fact that other managers at peer companies are being compensated generously
is analytically irrelevant. Compensation consultants — the *"ever-
accommodating firm of Ratchet, Ratchet and Bingo"* — are structurally
incapable of applying this standard because their engagement depends on
recommending higher pay, which means their professional incentives run in
precisely the wrong direction. Genuine alignment requires that the board ask
"What did this manager accomplish?" before it asks anything else. See the
[[Institutional Imperative]] page for the detailed treatment of comp committee
dynamics.

## Honest confession and personal accountability (2008–2009)

The integrity standard Buffett applies to other companies' reporting he applies with equal rigour to his own investment decisions. The 2008 and 2009 letters contain the most candid personal confessions in the corpus, offered not as ritual self-deprecation but as a demonstration that the willingness to acknowledge mistakes without excuse is itself a discipline.

The ConocoPhillips error is described in the 2008 letter as a *"major mistake of commission."* Berkshire purchased a large position in ConocoPhillips when oil and gas prices were near their peak in 2008. Buffett did not anticipate the subsequent sharp decline in energy prices. The loss was substantial and the transaction was unforced — no deadline, no competitive pressure, no external compulsion. Berkshire bought a commodity business at cyclical peak prices using a judgment that turned out to be badly wrong. The confession is direct about cause: it was a mistake of Buffett's own making, not market misfortune. The distinction matters because an investor who attributes forced errors to bad luck, and bad luck to his own cleverness, is setting himself up for a pattern of uncorrected mistakes.

The Irish banks mistake in the same 2008 letter is starker. Berkshire invested approximately $244 million in Irish bank equity. By the time the 2008 letter was written, the position was worth approximately $27 million — an 89% loss in a very short period. Buffett calls these *"unforced errors"*: investments that were not compelled by any circumstance, where the analysis was simply wrong. The Irish banking sector was far more leveraged to the property bubble than Berkshire's analysis had credited, and the subsequent collapse was severe. The lesson drawn is not unique to banking — it is about the failure to weight catastrophic downside scenarios heavily enough when making concentrated investments in leveraged institutions.

The GEICO credit card episode in the 2009 letter is a smaller but instructive case. GEICO launched a credit card programme that Buffett endorsed. The programme lost approximately $50 million before being wound down. When asked what he had learned from the experience, Buffett offered a deliberately self-deprecating answer: *"I was just older."* The honest reading is that the loss was a product of overconfidence in GEICO's brand advantage extending to an unrelated financial product, and that no analytical insight had been achieved — only the negative evidence that the venture did not work. Claiming to have "learned a lesson" when one has simply lost money is a form of self-serving narrative. The more honest formulation acknowledges that sometimes the chief lesson is that the decision was wrong, full stop.

Taken together, the three confessions illustrate that the earnings-integrity standard is not a spectator sport. A manager who demands honest reporting from other companies but soft-pedals or rationalises his own investment errors is applying a selective standard. Buffett's willingness to call unforced errors by their name — and to refrain from constructing flattering narratives around them — is the personal version of the same discipline he advocates for corporate accounting.

## The "don't count this" managers and stock compensation (2016)

The 2016 letter extends the option-expensing critique into the broader
practice of adjusted earnings presentations. Buffett identifies a category
of managers who routinely instruct investors to ignore specific charges when
assessing performance. Two items recur.

The first is restructuring charges. As the 2000 and earlier letters
established, these are selectively taken and presented as non-recurring.
The 2016 letter notes that Berkshire itself has gone through dozens of
restructurings; the costs were always real, always borne by shareholders,
and always eventually appeared somewhere in the accounts. Classifying them
as non-recurring is a presentation choice with no economic content.

The second is stock-based compensation, still the most persistently
misrepresented expense in corporate reporting. Buffett's formulation is
stark: *"If compensation isn't an expense, what is it?"* The 2016 letter
introduces a specific image for what management is doing when it promotes
adjusted earnings that exclude stock comp: it is running *"an accounting
lab"* — a facility dedicated to manufacturing numbers that differ from what
actually happened. *"CEOs who have enthusiastically participated in the
adjusted-earnings game will eventually ask themselves when a charge is a
'real' charge and when it is not."* The honest answer is always: stock
compensation is a real charge. Cash that would have been required to produce
the same incentive is the correct baseline against which to measure the
option's cost.

## The mark-to-market GAAP rule and the case for operating earnings (2018)

A new GAAP rule taking effect in 2018 required companies to include
unrealised gains and losses on equity holdings in net income — not merely in
other comprehensive income. For most companies, the effect is modest.
For Berkshire, with a $173 billion equity portfolio at year-end 2018, the
effect was substantial: quarterly net income could swing by $10 billion or
more on the basis of stock price movements entirely unrelated to operating
performance.

The 2017 letter warned this was coming and described the anticipated result:
*"For analytical purposes, Berkshire's 'bottom line' will be useless."* The
2018 results confirmed the prediction. In the first and fourth quarters,
Berkshire reported GAAP losses of $1.1 billion and $25.4 billion
respectively. In the second and third quarters, it reported GAAP profits of
$12 billion and $18.5 billion. Behind these gyrations, operating earnings
from Berkshire's businesses exceeded their 2016 high of $17.6 billion by
41%. The two numbers — GAAP net income and operating earnings — were moving
in entirely different directions across the same year.

Buffett's instruction: *"Focus on operating earnings, paying little
attention to gains or losses of any variety."* This is not a preference for
ignoring investment performance; Berkshire's equity portfolio matters
enormously and will over time produce substantial gains. It is a statement
about the appropriate time horizon over which investment performance should
be assessed — not quarterly, based on price movements, but over years,
based on changes in intrinsic business value.

The 2018 letter also delivers the most memorable compressed formulation of
the adjusted-earnings critique. Berkshire's acquisition-related amortisation
of $1.4 billion is, in Buffett's assessment, mostly not a real economic cost
and should be added back by investors. But Berkshire's depreciation of $8.4
billion *understates* true economic cost, because real maintenance capex
exceeds the accounting charge. Abraham Lincoln's rhetorical question gets
applied: *"If you call a dog's tail a leg, how many legs does it have?"*
Lincoln's answer: *"Four, because calling a tail a leg doesn't make it
one."* The same logic applies to adjusted earnings presentations: relabelling
a real cost as non-recurring does not make it non-recurring, and relabelling
a balance sheet liability as free capital does not make it free. *"Abe would
have felt lonely on Wall Street."*

The practical test Buffett proposes: use the operating-earnings figure that
*"remains after all income taxes, interest payments, managerial compensation
(whether cash or stock-based), restructuring expenses, depreciation,
amortisation and home-office overhead."* That is the number that tells you
what the business actually earned for its owners. Everything else is a
presentation choice.

## The GAAP acrobatics table and "bold imaginative accounting" (2022)

The 2022 letter provides the starkest single-table illustration of the
GAAP distortion problem. Berkshire's quarterly figures for 2022 under
the mark-to-market rule introduced in 2018 were as follows:

| Quarter | Operating Earnings | GAAP Earnings Required to Report |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | $7.0B | $5.5B |
| Q2 | $9.3B | ($43.8B) |
| Q3 | $7.8B | ($2.7B) |
| Q4 | $6.7B | $18.2B |

The two columns move in opposite directions and bear no consistent
relationship to each other. Operating earnings were relatively smooth
and record-high for the year; GAAP earnings swung from a $43.8 billion
loss in Q2 to an $18.2 billion profit in Q4. *"The GAAP earnings are
100% misleading when viewed quarterly or even annually."* The swings
represent movements in Berkshire's equity portfolio, not changes in
operating performance.

The 2022 letter also contains Buffett's most unambiguous moral
condemnation of earnings manipulation in the corpus. Referring to
managers who manipulate even the operating-earnings figure — the metric
Buffett himself treats as the reliable one — he writes: *"That activity
is disgusting. It requires no talent to manipulate numbers: Only a deep
desire to deceive is required."*

A CEO had once described his own deception to Buffett as *"bold
imaginative accounting."* Buffett's assessment: this *"has become one
of the shames of capitalism."*

This extends the integrity argument from the 2018 letter, which had
instructed investors to focus on operating earnings while acknowledging
their reliability relative to GAAP. The 2022 letter completes the
picture: even operating earnings are vulnerable to deliberate
manipulation by managers who wish to *"beat expectations."* The only
complete defence is the investor's own scepticism — and the heuristics
from the 2002 letter (see above) remain the practical toolkit.

## "Worse-than-useless" and the Galileo formulation (2023)

The 2023 letter provides the most unsparing single-letter indictment of GAAP earnings in the corpus. Berkshire's operating earnings for 2023 were $37.4 billion. The GAAP figure for the same year was $96 billion — more than double, driven entirely by unrealised gains on the equity portfolio under the mark-to-market rule introduced in 2018. The prior year's GAAP figure had been a loss of $23 billion on the same operating base of $30.9 billion.

Buffett's characterisation: *"worse-than-useless 'net income' figure."* The Galileo reference is deliberate: when told his discovery that the Earth moved around the sun contradicted Church doctrine, Galileo is said to have muttered under his breath "and yet it moves." Buffett's analogue is that he is required by regulation to report GAAP net income, which is mandated but wrong as an indicator of economic performance, and yet Berkshire's operating earnings remain the only reliable measure — and move independently of whatever GAAP requires.

The 2023 letter also states directly what prior letters had only implied: *"EBITDA is a banned measurement at Berkshire."* The ban applies to internal reporting, acquisition analysis, and shareholder communication. The reasoning is unchanged from the 2000–2002 letters — EBITDA adds back depreciation, a real economic cost — but the direct assertion of a company-wide prohibition is new and represents the clearest single statement of the EBITDA critique in the corpus.

## Operating earnings record (2024)

The 2024 letter reports operating earnings of $47.4 billion for the year — a record, up from $37.4 billion in 2023. The two-year comparison is presented in a segment table:

| Segment | 2024 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Insurance-underwriting | $9.0B | $5.4B |
| Insurance-investment income | $13.7B | $9.6B |
| BNSF | $5.0B | $5.1B |
| BHE | $3.7B | $2.3B |
| Other controlled businesses | $13.1B | $13.4B |
| Non-controlled businesses | $1.5B | $1.8B |
| Other | $1.4B | ($0.2B) |
| **Total operating earnings** | **$47.4B** | **$37.4B** |

The 2024 letter notes that despite the record aggregate figure, 53% of Berkshire's 189 operating businesses reported a decline in earnings. The headline is a function of insurance performance (GEICO underwriting turnaround under Todd Combs) and investment income (Treasury bill yields on the cash position), not uniform improvement across the portfolio. This honest decomposition is consistent with the earnings-integrity standard: the headline figure is real, but its components deserve scrutiny.
