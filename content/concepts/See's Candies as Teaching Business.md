# See's Candies as Teaching Business

**Source:** [[sources/Buffett and Munger Unscripted]] — Part 11
(See's/Coca-Cola/Brands); [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 1983, 1991,
2007 letters; [[sources/Poor Charlie's Almanack]] — Talk 4

## Summary

See's Candies is the most important single business Berkshire ever acquired —
not because of its size (it is small) but because of what it *taught*.
Buffett and Munger have returned to See's in nearly every decade of annual
meeting discussions not as a nostalgia piece but as a live working example
of how to think about competitive advantage, capital efficiency, pricing
power, and the economics of great businesses versus good ones.

The teaching function is explicit. Buffett has said, without hyperbole, that
buying See's in 1972 was the *transition point* in his intellectual
development as an investor — the moment the Graham framework of buying
cheap assets gave way to the Fisher/Munger framework of buying wonderful
businesses at fair prices. Everything that followed — Coca-Cola, American
Express, Apple, the Berkshire operating model — traces back to the lesson
See's delivered.

## The Acquisition and the Near-Miss

Berkshire acquired See's Candy Shops in January 1972 for \$25 million.
At the time, See's had roughly \$29–30 million in annual revenues and
approximately \$7–9 million in tangible net assets. (The 1991 Chairman's Letter
specifies "tangible net worth of \$7 million"; Buffett at annual meetings has
cited approximately \$9 million in tangible assets — the difference likely
reflects net worth vs. gross tangible assets, or the precise measurement date.)
The pre-tax return on tangible equity was therefore exceptional — but neither
Buffett nor Munger fully appreciated what that meant at the time of purchase.

Buffett's admission, made publicly at multiple annual meetings, is
remarkable for its frankness:

*"If they'd asked \$100,000 more for See's, we'd have walked away.
That's how dumb we were."*

The intellectual framework for understanding why See's was worth far more
than its asset base had not yet been fully formed. What saved the acquisition
was that the price was reasonable enough even under the old framework.
What the acquisition then provided was the experience — the lived evidence —
that forced the new framework into being.

## The Capital Efficiency Numbers

The See's numbers, stated plainly across multiple Berkshire communications,
are among the most instructive in Buffett's public record:

- **1972 (acquisition):** ~\$30M revenues, ~\$9M tangible net assets
- **By the time revenues reached ~\$300M:** ~\$40M tangible net assets required
- **Cumulative pre-tax profits taken out:** more than \$1.5 billion

This means that from \$9 million of tangible capital, Berkshire extracted
more than \$1.5 billion in cash over roughly five decades — while the
business itself continued to grow and remained as competitive as before.
The tangible capital base grew only modestly (from \$9M to ~\$40M) despite
revenues growing tenfold. The difference between what the business earned
and what it needed to retain was available entirely for redeployment.

The contrast with capital-intensive businesses is stark. A manufacturer or
utility that earns similar revenues must continuously reinvest at or above
the rate of growth just to maintain its position. See's needed almost
nothing. Munger's phrase for this kind of business: it generates cash like
a "coupon-clipping" operation — you collect the proceeds without having
to invest more capital.

## The Royalty Formulation

The most precise description Buffett has given of what See's actually is
as an economic entity:

*"See's is essentially a royalty on somebody else's sales during inflation."*

The mechanism: See's earns its money through brand loyalty and price
increases. The price of chocolate boxes goes up. The underlying inputs
(sugar, cocoa, labour) also go up, but not faster than See's can raise
prices — because customers accept the increases. Meanwhile, the tangible
capital requirements don't grow proportionally. The result is that each
dollar of inflation effectively operates as a transfer from the purchasing
public into See's earnings without requiring additional capital to capture it.

This is the defining characteristic of a *great* business versus a merely
*good* one. A good business earns a fair return on its capital. A great
business earns a high return on low capital and can raise that return over
time through pricing power without reinvestment. See [[concepts/Great, Good
and Gruesome]] for the full taxonomy.

## The Pricing Power Test

A simple test for whether a business has genuine pricing power appears
repeatedly in the See's context: *"Would customers still buy if the price
went up 10%?"* For See's, the answer has been yes, reliably, for decades.
The brand carries enough emotional weight — California heritage, gift-giving
ritual, quality association — that modest price increases produce little
demand destruction.

The contrast Buffett draws is with businesses where pricing is a
"prayer session." Before a business raises prices even a penny, it holds
its breath and hopes it doesn't lose too much volume. For those businesses,
each price increase is a negotiation with customers who can easily substitute.
For See's, the price increase is announced and the customers follow.

The test applies at the moment of *acquisition consideration*, not just in
retrospect. Buffett now asks of every business he evaluates: *"If I raised
prices 10% tomorrow, what would happen to volumes?"* If the honest answer
is "I'm not sure" or "they'd probably fall significantly," that business
does not have the kind of pricing power that produces enduring economics.

## The Ira Marshall Moment and Ignorance Removal

Munger credits a colleague named Ira Marshall for pushing back on their
initial reluctance to pay more than asset value for See's:

Munger and Buffett were both hesitant to pay what they considered a
premium price for a candy company. Marshall argued they were wrong —
that the brand was worth more than the accounting showed. They listened.
They bought. They were right. Munger's summary:

*"Ignorance removal is the main thing we've done at Berkshire."*

This is a more radical statement than it first appears. The standard
narrative about Berkshire's success emphasises intelligence, discipline,
patience. Munger's formulation suggests a more humble mechanism: the
willingness to recognise specific forms of ignorance and correct them
when presented with evidence. Each major acquisition has corrected a
specific piece of ignorance about how businesses and competitive advantage
actually work.

See's corrected the ignorance that only cheap assets were worth buying.

## The Learning Chain to Coca-Cola

The explicit teaching sequence Buffett and Munger describe:

**See's (1972)** → *lesson: brand loyalty + pricing power + low capital = extraordinary economics*

**↓ learned**

**Coca-Cola (1988, \$1B+ initial position)** → *the same lesson at planetary scale*

Coca-Cola in 1988 had roughly \$40 of tangible assets per \$100 of revenues —
similar to See's in structure, but global in scope, with distribution in
virtually every country on earth. The intrinsic value was not in the
factories or trucks but in the brand: a "share of mind" asset that
reproduced itself through every purchase occasion.

Buffett's Coca-Cola case: one share of Coca-Cola at \$40 in 1919 (adjusted
for the Depression-era price collapse to ~\$19) would have compounded to
extraordinary wealth by 2024 even through two world wars, the Depression,
sugar rationing, New Coke, and every other disaster. The business earned
its returns not through asset accumulation but through the compounding
of brand value — the same mechanism See's demonstrated at small scale.

The Coca-Cola calculation Buffett presents: the \$40 share, compounded
at Coca-Cola's actual historical return including dividends reinvested,
produces a figure in the millions by the time he describes it. Every period
of disruption was a buying opportunity, not a reason to sell.

See also [[Owner Earnings]] for the quantitative mechanics that underlie
this teaching chain, and [[concepts/Great, Good and Gruesome]] for the
taxonomy into which both businesses fit.

## Regional Limitations and the Honest Assessment

Buffett is direct about what See's cannot do. Despite its extraordinary
economics in California and the western United States, See's has never
successfully expanded into other regions with full effect. The brand
carries strong emotional resonance for customers who grew up with it.
For customers in other geographies, it is simply a premium chocolate brand
competing against Godiva, Neuhaus, and dozens of others.

This is not a failure of management or marketing — it is a structural
feature of what the brand *is*. A regional heritage brand's pricing power
and customer loyalty is tied to the heritage. Export it to a market that
doesn't share the heritage and the brand becomes ordinary again. The honest
response is not to force geographic expansion but to harvest the economics
in the regions where the brand has genuine power and redeploy the cash
elsewhere. This is what Berkshire has done.

The limitation matters as an analytical lesson: not all competitive
advantages are infinitely extensible. Assessing the *geographic scope* of
a moat is as important as assessing its *depth*. A deep regional moat is
still highly valuable — See's cumulative \$1.5B+ makes this clear — but
it must be assessed correctly rather than assumed to be something it is not.
