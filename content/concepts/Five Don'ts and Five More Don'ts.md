# Five Don'ts and Five More Don'ts

**Source:** [[Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits]] — Chapter 8, "Five
Don'ts for Investors"; Chapter 9, "Five More Don'ts for Investors"

## Summary

Fisher devotes two chapters of *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* to
ten specific mistakes that investors repeat with sufficient regularity that
they deserve explicit prohibition rather than merely implicit discouragement.
The Don'ts are not abstract principles; each is grounded in Fisher's
observation of real losses suffered by real investors who knew better in
theory but failed to apply what they knew in practice. The ten rules are
structured around the same theme: the investor's enemy is almost always
herself, not the market.

## The first five Don'ts

**Don't buy into promotional companies.**

Fisher's definition of a promotional company is a company whose current
stock price is based primarily on future hope rather than demonstrated
operating performance, and whose management is more focused on
maintaining the stock price or the investment story than on building the
underlying business. The tell is a management that promotes aggressively
while reporting losses, and a valuation that depends almost entirely on a
future breakthrough that has not yet occurred.

Fisher's objection is structural: the investor in a promotional company is
depending on two simultaneous conditions being met — the underlying
breakthrough must actually occur, and the market must then appropriately
recognise it. The probability of both conditions being satisfied is the
product of two individually uncertain probabilities. In contrast, the investor
in a company with demonstrated operating results only needs the market
eventually to recognise what is already there.

This Don't is the source of the genuine tension with Bezos's asymmetric-bet
portfolio strategy, which is discussed in detail in [[Bet on Big Ideas vs Don't Buy Promotional Companies]]. The resolution is that Fisher and Bezos
are compatible but operating from different positions: Fisher is describing
the company that runs speculative bets without a profitable core to absorb
the failures; Bezos is describing the company that runs speculative bets
*from within* a profitable core. Fisher's Don't is directed at companies in
the former position. For the investor, the diagnostic question is whether the
losses reflect bounded experiments being financed by a genuinely excellent
core business, or whether they reflect promotional activity without
demonstrated underlying quality.

**Don't ignore a good stock just because it trades over the counter.**

In 1958, over-the-counter stocks carried a stigma among institutional
investors that Fisher regarded as irrational. Many of the most attractive
growth companies in his experience had either never listed on an exchange
or had grown large enough to list only recently. He urges investors to
evaluate companies on their merits rather than on the venue in which they
trade. This Don't is largely dated — the market structure that motivated it
no longer exists in its original form — but the underlying principle survives:
do not exclude categories of investment from consideration because of
format rather than content.

**Don't buy a stock just because you like the "tone" of its annual report.**

Fisher's concern here is that annual reports are marketing documents. A
well-written, attractively produced report with confident language about the
future is not evidence of anything except that the company has a competent
investor relations function. Fisher's sharp formulation: the best companies
he has known often have the most unassuming annual reports, while some
of the most promotional companies have the glossiest. The investor who
uses the annual report's tone as a signal is making a mistake that benefits
the promotional companies Fisher's first Don't is designed to exclude.

**Don't assume that the high price at which a stock may be selling in
relation to earnings is necessarily an indication that further growth in
those earnings has largely been discounted in the stock's price.**

This is Fisher's argument against reflexive valuation-based selling. A high
price-earnings ratio on a company that will sustain above-average growth
for many years is often a better investment than a low price-earnings ratio
on a company with flat or declining prospects. The investor who sells an
excellent company because it "looks expensive" by conventional metrics,
and then fails to buy it back after a temporary decline, frequently misses
most of the long-run gains the company produces. Fisher's related argument
about the further-into-the-future profits can grow, the higher the P/E the
investor can afford to pay, appears in the Fourth Dimension discussion (see
[[Financial Community Appraisal]]).

**Don't quibble over eighths and quarters.**

Fisher traces this particular mistake to his own early career and is unusually
self-critical about it. The investor who believes a stock is worth holding for
many years because the underlying business is excellent should not routinely
miss purchases because she placed limit orders a fraction of a point below
the market and the stock never reached them. Fisher illustrates this with the
case of a client who refused to pay $34¾ for a stock he could have bought
at $34½, watched it rise more than 50% over the following weeks, and then
never had the opportunity to buy it back at anything close to his original
limit. The potential gain from minor price negotiations is trivially small
compared to the potential loss from failing to establish a position in a
company that turns out to be excellent.

## The second five Don'ts

**Don't overdiversify.**

This is the most consequential of the ten Don'ts and the one that most
directly contradicts conventional portfolio advice. Fisher's formulation is
blunt: for individuals, any holding of over twenty different stocks is a sign
of financial incompetence. Ten to twelve is usually the right number.

The reasoning has two parts. First, genuine understanding of a company —
the kind of understanding that comes from the [[Scuttlebutt]] method and
sustained monitoring of the [[Fifteen Points]] — requires sustained
attention. An investor cannot maintain genuine understanding of forty or
fifty positions. What she calls diversification is actually ignorance spread
across many positions. Second, truly outstanding companies are scarce. An
investor who has found five to ten genuinely excellent businesses and holds
them is in a better position than one who has found five excellent businesses
and added fifteen mediocre ones for the sake of diversification.

Fisher's A/B/C classification (see [[Concentration vs Diversification]])
is the practical application of this Don't. Type A companies — outstanding
in both management quality and business characteristics — are rare, worth
holding in size, and worth understanding thoroughly. Type B companies are
average; they can be held but warrant smaller positions and more frequent
re-evaluation. Type C companies are speculative; they may merit a small
position as a call option on improvement, but a portfolio dominated by
them is not conservatively positioned. Fisher's portfolio is intentionally
concentrated in a small number of Type A companies.

**Don't be afraid of buying on a war scare.**

Fisher's observation is empirical: war scares cause sharp short-term
declines in stock prices that almost always reverse within months if the
underlying companies are excellent. The investor who sells a good holding
because of a geopolitical event, and then cannot bring herself to buy back
at higher prices once the scare has passed, pays a permanent cost for a
temporary risk. Fisher is not arguing that war is good for stock prices; he
is arguing that the investor's response to war scares — panicked selling —
is almost invariably wrong and expensive.

**Don't forget your Gilbert and Sullivan.**

Fisher is being oblique here. His reference is to the Gilbert and Sullivan
lyric in which a character insists he has never departed from the truth, but
then reveals he has said so many "Nos" that his courage became permanent.
Fisher's application: the investor should not be so preoccupied with being
contrary to the financial community's view that she reflexively takes the
opposite position from whatever is currently fashionable. Contrary opinion
alone is not an investment method. The investor must have independent
reason to believe the crowd is wrong, not merely that the crowd holds a
view (see [[Financial Community Appraisal]] for the related argument about
when the financial community's appraisal diverges from underlying facts).
This Don't is the companion to Fisher's "zigging and zagging" framework
from Part Three: zig when the financial community zags, but only when you
are very sure you are right.

**Don't fail to consider time as well as price in buying a true growth stock.**

This Don't addresses timing. A company that qualifies on the [[Fifteen Points]] may not be worth buying immediately if its products are in an
awkward phase — past the initial enthusiasm of a new product launch, when
the stock price was irrationally high, but before the steady growth of a
proven product cycle, when the price will be more rational. Fisher's
practical advice is to watch for the period between the speculative peak of
a new product announcement and the sustained growth that follows
successful commercial rollout. During this period, the stock often trades at
a discount to its eventual value without reflecting any deterioration in the
underlying business.

**Don't follow the crowd.**

Fisher's tenth Don't is the broadest, and in some ways a restatement of
what the other nine are protecting against. The financial community's
consensus at any given moment reflects collective appraisal, not underlying
fact. Appraisals are frequently wrong, sometimes spectacularly so in both
directions. The investor who forms her own view through the [[Scuttlebutt]]
method and the [[Fifteen Points]] framework and then acts on that view
when it diverges from the consensus will, over time, outperform the investor
who follows the consensus. Fisher's evidence for this is his entire career:
the Food Machinery purchase in 1931, the Texas Instruments and Motorola
purchases in 1955, and many others were made against the prevailing
consensus and proved profitable precisely because the consensus was wrong.

## What the Don'ts are protecting against

The Don'ts are not primarily about analytical mistakes. Most of them are
about behavioural failures — the impulse to follow the crowd, to take
profits because a stock looks expensive, to quibble over small price
differences, to diversify beyond the point of genuine understanding. Fisher's
implicit model of the investor's enemy is the investor's own psychology
rather than the difficulty of analysing businesses. The [[Fifteen Points]]
and the [[Scuttlebutt]] method are tools for getting the analysis right; the
Don'ts are tools for then acting on the analysis correctly.
