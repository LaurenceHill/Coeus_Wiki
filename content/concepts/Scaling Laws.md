## Summary

In the June 2007 interim letter, Sleep introduces a section of biological and complexity
science from the Santa Fe Institute as a lens for understanding which businesses can
survive long enough to compound meaningfully. The central question he poses is
deceptively simple: why is it predictable that some businesses grow from a mouse into an
elephant, while most stay mice or collapse under their own weight?

The answer Sleep borrows from the physicist Geoff West's work on biological scaling laws
is structural. Every organism's longevity is determined not by luck or willpower but by the
relationship between its skeletal architecture and its metabolic efficiency. The parallel for
businesses is direct: a firm's long-run survivability is a function of whether its basic
operating structure — its "skeletal structure," in Sleep's phrase — can support increasing
size without requiring fundamental re-engineering. Complexity is the killer. Simple
skeletons survive; complex ones buckle.

## The biological premise: Kleiber's Law and a billion heartbeats

West's research, conducted at the Santa Fe Institute, produced what is now called
Kleiber's Law: metabolic rate scales with body mass not in proportion (a power law of
1:1) but at a declining rate (a power law of 3/4). A kilo of mouse is metabolically more
expensive to maintain than a kilo of whale. Size, in organisms, is efficient — larger
animals burn less energy per unit of mass.

West combined this with the observation that heart rate also declines with mass, and
drew a striking conclusion: if longevity increases with mass and heart rate decreases with
mass, then all life shares roughly the same total number of heartbeats — approximately
one billion. A mouse exhausts its billion at roughly five hundred beats per minute over
four years. An elephant uses the same billion at twenty-five beats per minute over seventy
years. Evolution, across hundreds of millions of years, has not changed this basic
constraint.

The reason smaller animals cannot simply slow their hearts and live longer is architectural.
In large animals, blood vessels can fan out over greater distances, increasing their aggregate
cross-sectional area and reducing viscous drag. The heart works less hard because the
plumbing allows it to. In a mouse, there is no room for that circulatory expansion; the
heart must work harder to push blood through narrower pathways. The constraint is
skeletal and structural, not motivational.

Sleep's gloss is concise: *"There is a very basic function to longevity with skeletal
strength allowing for size, size allowing for circulatory efficiency, and efficiency allowing
for longevity."*

## What investors can learn: the skeletal structure test

Sleep's translation of Kleiber's Law into investment analysis produces three tests for
whether a business can grow from mouse to elephant:

**First, the business must be able to self-fund its growth.** If the opportunity set is large,
the return on incremental capital needs to be high enough to compound reinvestment
internally. A business that requires continuous external capital to grow is structurally
fragile in the same way a large organism with an undersized skeleton is fragile — external
support substitutes for a structural deficiency rather than resolving it.

**Second, barriers to entry must increase with size.** This is the moat-widening
criterion. A business whose competitive position weakens as it grows — because it
attracts better-resourced competitors, or because operational complexity erodes quality —
is one whose skeletal structure cannot support the weight of its own growth. The moat
must be self-reinforcing.

**Third, the basic building block of the business must be simple.** Complexity is, in
Sleep's view, one of the main reasons firms fail as they try to grow. A business with
many variable elements at its most expensive point — the high street retailer whose
service quality depends on staff levels, staff helpfulness, shop furnishings, product range,
and local property costs simultaneously — has a fragile skeleton. Each additional location
multiplies the number of things that can go wrong.

Contrast this with Amazon in 2007, Sleep's primary example. Goods flow from supplier
to central warehouse to customer; the expensive high street real estate is removed from
the chain entirely. The customer experience — web site quality, speed of delivery, breadth
of range — is inherently more controllable and more uniform. A web site viewed in New
York is identical to the same site viewed in London. Sleep's assessment: *"It seems to us
that the basic building block of internet retailing, its skeletal structure, is far more robust,
scalable and cheaper than the high street equivalent."* The power law is high.

## The connection to Scale Economics Shared

Scaling laws explain *why* [[Scale Economics Shared]] is such a durable model, not
merely that it is. The SES model combines a simple skeletal structure with a self-
reinforcing competitive dynamic: cost savings at scale are passed to customers, which
attracts more volume, which generates more scale, which enables further savings. Each
turn of the cycle widens the moat. This is the biological ideal — a business whose
barriers to entry increase with size, whose basic structure requires little re-engineering
as it grows, and whose metabolic efficiency improves rather than degrades at scale.

Sleep is explicit about the combination: *"When this basic building block is combined
with the scale efficiencies shared model (which increases the moat as the firm grows),
customer-centric orientation of the firm's founder, as well as his healthy disdain for Wall
Street, this combination makes us think that we may have a mouse that can turn into an
elephant."*

Most businesses achieve scale without the self-reinforcing loop — a drug manufacturer
achieves operating leverage but does not lower prices; a luxury goods company achieves
brand scale but raises prices. The moat expands in some dimensions while potentially
narrowing in others (customer goodwill, price sensitivity). The SES model is unusual
because the mechanism of scaling *is* the mechanism of moat-widening. Size and
defensibility compound together rather than trading off.

## Moat longevity: the danger of harvesting

From the 2005 "Measuring the Moat" appendix, Sleep adds a warning that connects
scaling laws to business survival in a different way. Early in a franchise's development,
rewarding customers disproportionately — keeping prices low, maintaining service quality
— is essential because referrals and repeat business are the engine of franchise
development. With maturity, shareholders can reasonably claim a greater share. But there
is a limit: *"Too much, however, and the moat is drained with negative consequences for
longevity."*

The temptation is large because capital markets will reward profiteering in the short term.
Sleep cites Coca-Cola leaning excessively on bottlers, Gillette cutting advertising, and
Home Depot boosting gross margins as examples of businesses entering what he calls
"growth purgatory" — the moat narrows just as shareholders discover that reported
profitability was being flattered by margin expansion that the franchise could not
permanently sustain. The scaling law analogy is apt: a large organism that reduces its
circulatory efficiency to spend less energy will not live longer; it will weaken faster.
Sharing the gains of scale is not generosity; it is the mechanism by which the moat
remains intact.

## The "longevity of compound" opportunity

The 2011 letter introduces a phrase that ties the scaling laws analysis directly to investment
opportunity. Sleep notes that *"investors know that in time average companies fail, and so
stocks are discounted for that risk. However, this discount is applied to all stocks even
those that, in the end, do not fail."*

Empirical Research Partners found that the chance of a growth stock maintaining growth
status for five years is one in five, and for ten years one in ten. The market is broadly
right that mean reversion is the norm. The error is applying the mean-reversion discount
uniformly — including to the minority of businesses whose skeletal structure genuinely
supports compound growth over decades. Sleep calls the resulting opportunity the
*"longevity of compound"*: the shares of genuinely great companies can be cheap, in some
cases, for decades, because the market assigns them the same probability of failure that
the average business deserves. The anomaly is not hidden or technical; it is structural.
Investors recognise success incrementally when the factors that produce it — a simple
scalable business model, a widening moat, a culture of sharing scale gains — may be
visible and largely constant from an early stage.

Sleep's example: the early investors in Wal-Mart who understood the SES model might
have held alongside the Walton family and become billionaires. The opportunity to hold
existed; the discipline to act on it, against the grain of conventional fund management,
was the scarce resource.

## What this is not

Scaling laws do not guarantee longevity. A simple skeletal structure is necessary but not
sufficient. The moat must genuinely widen with size, not merely survive intact. The
culture of sharing must be maintained under the persistent temptation to harvest.
Management must resist capital markets' preference for near-term profiteering. And the
addressable market must be large enough to allow compounding over the relevant horizon
— a mouse-to-elephant journey requires elephant-sized territory. See [[Destination
Analysis]] for Sleep's terminal-state framing of this last requirement.

The analysis also does not imply that complex businesses cannot be good investments.
It implies that complex businesses face a structural disadvantage in sustaining long-run
compound growth, and that investors who pay for such growth are taking more structural
risk than the near-term financials disclose.
