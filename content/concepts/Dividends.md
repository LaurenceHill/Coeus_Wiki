# Dividends

**Source:** [[Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits]] — Chapter 7, "The
Dividend Factor in Common Stock Investing"; Part Three, "Developing an
Investment Philosophy" (the long shadow of dividends)

## Summary

Fisher's position on dividends is a considered dissent from the conventional
wisdom of his era and most of ours: for investors who do not need current
income, dividend payments from growth companies are almost always the
wrong choice. The argument is not primarily about tax efficiency, though
that enters the calculation. It is about the opportunity cost of returning
capital to shareholders rather than reinvesting it in a business that has
demonstrated the ability to deploy capital at above-average rates of return.

The position is conditional. Fisher is not arguing that dividends are always
bad or that investors should never want them. He is arguing that the investor
who is capable of owning shares in genuinely excellent growth companies,
who does not need the dividend income to fund consumption, and who
would otherwise reinvest the dividends in similar or inferior assets, is
better served by letting the company retain and reinvest earnings on her
behalf.

## The argument

The core of Fisher's case is that growth companies with genuine reinvestment
opportunities are doing their shareholders a disservice by paying dividends.
The company can deploy capital internally — in new product development,
in expanded production capacity, in market development — at returns that
are, if the business truly qualifies under the [[Fifteen Points]], higher than
the investor can obtain elsewhere. When the company pays a dividend, it is
returning capital to the investor with a tax cost attached, and the investor
must then find somewhere to redeploy it. If the investor puts the dividend
back into the same company's stock, she has participated in a round-trip
that served no one except the tax authority.

Fisher's formulation in Part Three is blunt: "There is a natural fit of interest
between those firms with bountiful investment opportunities and certain
investors who sought to make the greatest possible profit in relation to the
risk involved and who neither needed additional income nor wanted to pay
unnecessary taxes." This natural fit is the case for zero or minimal dividends
from qualifying companies.

He also notes an asymmetry in the investment population. Companies with
the most attractive long-run reinvestment opportunities are typically under
the most pressure to deploy capital internally, because the opportunities are
genuine and numerous. These are precisely the companies that should pay
the least in dividends. Companies with flat or declining reinvestment
opportunities — those for which capital retention produces diminishing
returns — are the ones for which dividend payments are most rational,
because the alternative is retaining capital in a business that will not use
it well. Fisher's observation is that the market often prices this correctly,
but not always, and the investor who understands the reinvestment dynamics
of a specific company can take advantage of the mismatch when it exists.

## The qualification

Fisher is careful not to overstate the case. He distinguishes between
investors who need current income and those who do not. For the former,
dividends are a rational preference, and Fisher acknowledges this directly.
His framework is not a universal prohibition on dividends; it is a
recommendation about how to match investment objectives to company
characteristics. An investor who relies on dividend income to fund living
expenses should own companies that pay dividends. An investor who is
reinvesting all returns and paying the higher tax rate on income rather than
capital gains is choosing a structurally worse outcome by requiring
dividends.

He also notes by the time of the 1979 Part Three essay that the situation had
become less clear-cut than it was in 1958. Institutional holders —
particularly pension funds and profit-sharing plans that pay no tax on
dividends — had become a dominant force in share ownership. Many of
these institutions required a dividend payment, however small, as a
condition of ownership. This created pressure on some companies with
genuine reinvestment opportunities to initiate token dividend payments to
remain eligible for institutional ownership. Fisher's updated position
acknowledges this: the most he will say is that the dividend influence
"should be downgraded very sharply by those who do not need the income,"
and that "more attractive opportunities will be found among stocks with a
low dividend payout or none at all."

## Relationship to parallel arguments

Fisher's position on dividends is structurally identical to Bezos's case for
[[Free Cash Flow Over Earnings]] and to Buffett's [[Look-Through Earnings]] argument, though the three arrive at the same place from different
directions.

Bezos's argument is that GAAP earnings are not the right financial metric
for a business in heavy reinvestment mode, because GAAP penalises current-
period investment that will generate future cash flows. A management team
optimising for reported earnings will underinvest. Fisher's argument is the
same logic applied one step upstream: a board that maximises current-
period dividend payouts is making the same mistake as the management
team optimising for quarterly earnings — it is returning capital that could
have been compounding at high rates inside the business.

Buffett's Look-Through Earnings argument is different in mechanism —
Buffett is arguing about how to account for retained earnings in minority-
held companies — but shares the underlying premise with Fisher: the
economic value of a retained dollar inside an excellent business is higher
than the value of that same dollar distributed to shareholders, provided the
business has genuine reinvestment opportunities and the management will
use the capital well.

All three arguments converge on the same diagnostic question: does this
specific management team have the ability and the opportunities to deploy
retained capital at above-average rates of return? If yes, retention beats
distribution. If no, the case for dividends (or returning capital in other
forms) is much stronger.

## What this means in practice

The practical implication of Fisher's dividend framework, taken alongside
his [[Concentration vs Diversification]] argument, is that the investor
seeking maximum long-run returns should look for a small number of
companies that: (1) qualify on the [[Fifteen Points]]; (2) have genuine
reinvestment opportunities at attractive rates; (3) retain most of their
earnings to fund those opportunities; and (4) are held long enough that the
compounding of retained earnings becomes the dominant source of return.
This is the portfolio structure Fisher employed throughout his career, and
the Food Machinery Corporation investment — initiated during the
Depression and held for decades — is his primary illustration of the
mathematics it produces.
