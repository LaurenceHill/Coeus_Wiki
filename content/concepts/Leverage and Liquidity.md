# Leverage and Liquidity

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 2010 letter ("Life and Debt" section); reinforced in 2008 and 2009 letters

## Summary

In the 2010 letter's "Life and Debt" section, Buffett gives his fullest account of why Berkshire maintains extreme financial conservatism. The argument is not that leverage is always bad — Buffett acknowledges that some people have become very rich through borrowed money. The argument is that leverage is serially lethal at the tail: any sequence of positive numbers, however impressive, is obliterated by a single zero. Because severe financial crises occur with enough regularity to be near-certain over a long career, an organisation that cannot survive one will eventually not survive.

The practical consequence is a permanent commitment to surplus liquidity. Berkshire pledges to hold at minimum $10 billion in cash, and customarily keeps $20 billion or more. This is not a passive policy — it is an active competitive position. When the Lehman Brothers bankruptcy triggered the panic of September 2008, Berkshire was able to deploy $15.6 billion in 25 days as a supplier of capital to a system that could otherwise look only to the federal government. The willingness to act aggressively in crisis is only possible for an organisation that has never been at risk of needing to act defensively.

## The zero-multiplier argument

The core logic is arithmetic rather than probabilistic. Borrowers assume that a series of bad outcomes is unlikely; the historical record shows that leverage repeatedly produces zeroes, even for very smart people. "When leverage works, it magnifies your gains. Your spouse thinks you're clever, and your neighbours get envious. But leverage is addictive. Once having profited from its wonders, very few people retreat to more conservative practices." The psychological feedback loop — confidence reinforced by early success — is the mechanism by which leverage grows until the zero arrives.

For companies, the problem compounds. Businesses with large debts typically assume they can refinance as obligations mature, and usually they can. But refinancing depends on credit markets that occasionally close without warning: "Borrowers then learn that credit is like oxygen. When either is abundant, its presence goes unnoticed. When either is missing, that's all that is noticed. Even a short absence of credit can bring a company to its knees." September 2008 demonstrated this at civilisational scale — the overnight disappearance of credit in many sectors came "dangerously close to bringing our entire country to its knees."

The racing analogy captures the principle: "The fundamental principle of auto racing is that to finish first, you must first finish." An organisation optimising for performance at the cost of resilience is optimising for the wrong variable, because a single catastrophic event eliminates all prior gains.

## The cost of conservatism and why it is worth paying

Buffett is direct about the price: "By being so cautious in respect to leverage, we penalise our returns by a minor amount." The $20 billion-plus in cash equivalents earns a pittance, particularly in low-rate environments. This is a real cost, not a theoretical one. The 2009 letter notes that Berkshire entered 2008 with $44.3 billion in cash equivalents, much of it earning near zero throughout the crisis period.

The payoff is asymmetric. The cost is visible and continuous — foregone yield on idle cash. The benefit is intermittent but potentially enormous: the ability to act at scale precisely when prices are most dislocated and competitors are paralysed. In the 2009 letter, Buffett observes that "a climate of fear is their best friend" for investors, but this is only true for investors who have arranged their affairs so that fear in others cannot become fear in themselves. The 2008 investments — $14.5 billion in Goldman Sachs, General Electric, and Wrigley preferred securities with attached equity participation — were available only because Berkshire's liquidity was never in question.

The 2010 letter traces this conservatism to a family source: a 1939 letter from Buffett's grandfather Ernest to his son Fred, urging him to keep $1,000 in cash as a permanent reserve — "a necessity if you are to avoid worry when temporarily embarrassed." Ernest never finished high school and never attended business school, but he "understood the importance of liquidity as a condition for assured survival."

## The minimum cash commitment

Berkshire's formal pledge is to hold at least $10 billion excluding cash at its regulated utility and railroad businesses, and to customarily maintain $20 billion so as to be able simultaneously to absorb unprecedented insurance losses (the largest prior to the letter was approximately $3 billion from Katrina) and seize acquisition or investment opportunities during periods of financial turmoil. Cash is kept primarily in US Treasury bills — Buffett cites Ray DeVoe's observation that "more money has been lost reaching for yield than at the point of a gun."

The policy has never been suspended. "Not a dime of cash has left Berkshire for dividends or share repurchases during the past 40 years." Retained earnings over those four decades built net worth from $48 million to $157 billion; Buffett argues no other American corporation has built financial strength in this "unrelenting way."

## Relationship to the investment portfolio

The same logic governs Berkshire's equity and derivatives positions. When Berkshire entered long-dated equity put contracts, it structured them so it receives premiums upfront — "Berkshire therefore always holds the money, which leaves us assuming no meaningful counterparty risk." The absence of collateral-posting requirements was explicitly designed to ensure that a severe market decline could never create a liquidity emergency. Foregoing higher premiums to avoid collateral obligations "proved to be well worth it."

The 2009 letter is explicit: "We will never become dependent on the kindness of strangers. Too-big-to-fail is not a fallback position at Berkshire." This is presented not as risk-aversion but as a strategic posture — the ability to be a capital supplier rather than a capital supplicant during crises is a durable competitive advantage.
