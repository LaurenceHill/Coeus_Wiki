# Leverage and Liquidity

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 2010 letter ("Life and Debt" section); reinforced in 2008 and 2009 letters; extended in 2017 and 2018 letters with the four-dip table and the Russian-roulette formulation; 2021 letter (cash floor raised to $30B)

## Summary

In the 2010 letter's "Life and Debt" section, Buffett gives his fullest account of why Berkshire maintains extreme financial conservatism. The argument is not that leverage is always bad — Buffett acknowledges that some people have become very rich through borrowed money. The argument is that leverage is serially lethal at the tail: any sequence of positive numbers, however impressive, is obliterated by a single zero. Because severe financial crises occur with enough regularity to be near-certain over a long career, an organisation that cannot survive one will eventually not survive.

The practical consequence is a permanent commitment to surplus liquidity. Berkshire pledges to hold at minimum $10 billion in cash, and customarily keeps $20 billion or more. This is not a passive policy — it is an active competitive position. When the Lehman Brothers bankruptcy triggered the panic of September 2008, Berkshire was able to deploy $15.6 billion in 25 days as a supplier of capital to a system that could otherwise look only to the federal government. The willingness to act aggressively in crisis is only possible for an organisation that has never been at risk of needing to act defensively.

## The zero-multiplier argument

The core logic is arithmetic rather than probabilistic. Borrowers assume that a series of bad outcomes is unlikely; the historical record shows that leverage repeatedly produces zeroes, even for very smart people. "When leverage works, it magnifies your gains. Your spouse thinks you're clever, and your neighbours get envious. But leverage is addictive. Once having profited from its wonders, very few people retreat to more conservative practices." The psychological feedback loop — confidence reinforced by early success — is the mechanism by which leverage grows until the zero arrives.

For companies, the problem compounds. Businesses with large debts typically assume they can refinance as obligations mature, and usually they can. But refinancing depends on credit markets that occasionally close without warning: "Borrowers then learn that credit is like oxygen. When either is abundant, its presence goes unnoticed. When either is missing, that's all that is noticed. Even a short absence of credit can bring a company to its knees." September 2008 demonstrated this at civilisational scale — the overnight disappearance of credit in many sectors came "dangerously close to bringing our entire country to its knees."

The racing analogy captures the principle: "The fundamental principle of auto racing is that to finish first, you must first finish." An organisation optimising for performance at the cost of resilience is optimising for the wrong variable, because a single catastrophic event eliminates all prior gains.

## The cost of conservatism and why it is worth paying

Buffett is direct about the price: "By being so cautious in respect to leverage, we penalise our returns by a minor amount." The $20 billion-plus in cash equivalents earns a pittance, particularly in low-rate environments. This is a real cost, not a theoretical one. The 2009 letter notes that Berkshire entered 2008 with $44.3 billion in cash equivalents, much of it earning near zero throughout the crisis period.

The payoff is asymmetric. The cost is visible and continuous — foregone yield on idle cash. The benefit is intermittent but potentially enormous: the ability to act at scale precisely when prices are most dislocated and competitors are paralysed. In the 2009 letter, Buffett observes that "a climate of fear is their best friend" for investors, but this is only true for investors who have arranged their affairs so that fear in others cannot become fear in themselves. The 2008 investments — $14.5 billion in Goldman Sachs, General Electric, and Wrigley preferred securities with attached equity participation — were available only because Berkshire's liquidity was never in question.

The 2010 letter traces this conservatism to a family source: a 1939 letter from Buffett's grandfather Ernest to his son Fred, urging him to keep $1,000 in cash as a permanent reserve — "a necessity if you are to avoid worry when temporarily embarrassed." Ernest never finished high school and never attended business school, but he "understood the importance of liquidity as a condition for assured survival."

## The minimum cash commitment

Berkshire's formal pledge is to hold at least $10 billion excluding cash at its regulated utility and railroad businesses, and to customarily maintain $20 billion so as to be able simultaneously to absorb unprecedented insurance losses (the largest prior to the letter was approximately $3 billion from Katrina) and seize acquisition or investment opportunities during periods of financial turmoil. Cash is kept primarily in US Treasury bills — Buffett cites Ray DeVoe's observation that "more money has been lost reaching for yield than at the point of a gun."

The policy has never been suspended. "Not a dime of cash has left Berkshire for dividends or share repurchases during the past 40 years." Retained earnings over those four decades built net worth from $48 million to $157 billion; Buffett argues no other American corporation has built financial strength in this "unrelenting way."

## Relationship to the investment portfolio

The same logic governs Berkshire's equity and derivatives positions. When Berkshire entered long-dated equity put contracts, it structured them so it receives premiums upfront — "Berkshire therefore always holds the money, which leaves us assuming no meaningful counterparty risk." The absence of collateral-posting requirements was explicitly designed to ensure that a severe market decline could never create a liquidity emergency. Foregoing higher premiums to avoid collateral obligations "proved to be well worth it."

The 2009 letter is explicit: "We will never become dependent on the kindness of strangers. Too-big-to-fail is not a fallback position at Berkshire." This is presented not as risk-aversion but as a strategic posture — the ability to be a capital supplier rather than a capital supplicant during crises is a durable competitive advantage.

## The four-dip table and Kipling's "If" (2017)

The 2017 letter offers the most direct argument Buffett has made against
using borrowed money to own equities. He presents a table of the four
largest Berkshire share-price declines over the prior 53 years:

| Period | High | Low | Decline |
|---|---|---|---|
| March 1973 – January 1975 | $93 | $38 | −59.1% |
| October 2 – October 27, 1987 | $4,250 | $2,675 | −37.1% |
| June 1998 – March 2000 | $80,900 | $41,300 | −48.9% |
| September 2008 – March 2009 | $147,000 | $72,400 | −50.7% |

This is not a list of bad investments — it is a list of four periods in the
history of one of the most successful compounding businesses ever constructed.
Over those same 53 years, Berkshire's per-share book value grew from $19 to
$172,108. The declines are not evidence of business failure; they are the
normal experience of equity ownership. The point Buffett draws: _"There is
simply no telling how far stocks can fall in a short period. Even if your
borrowings are small and your positions aren't immediately threatened by the
plunging market, your mind may well become rattled by scary headlines and
breathless commentary. And an unsettled mind will not make good decisions."_

The table is thus simultaneously an argument about financing and about
psychology. A levered investor facing a 50% mark-to-market decline may be
technically solvent but operationally incapacitated — margin calls, covenant
breaches, or simple fear cause forced selling at the worst moment. An
unlevered investor facing the same decline has a different problem: an
opportunity to buy more at half price. The table demonstrates that these
opportunities have arrived with regularity even in the most admired businesses.

Buffett quotes Kipling's "If" as the psychological prescription:

> _"If you can keep your head when all about you are losing theirs…_
> _If you can wait and not be tired by waiting…_
> _Yours is the Earth and everything that's in it."_

The four-dip table and the Kipling passage together constitute the emotional
counterpart to the zero-multiplier argument. The arithmetic of leverage is
dangerous; the psychology of leverage is worse. The solution to both is to
arrange affairs so that a 50% price decline is never a crisis.

## The Russian-roulette formulation (2018)

The 2018 letter provides the most compressed statement of the leverage
argument in the entire corpus. Debt creates what Buffett calls _"a
Russian-roulette equation — usually win, occasionally die."_ He
acknowledges explicitly that this may _"make financial sense for someone who
gets a piece of a company's upside but does not share in its downside"_ —
a reference to managers, private equity sponsors, and others whose
compensation structure is convex. For an entity like Berkshire, which has
permanent capital and whose owners bear all the downside, the calculation
is different: _"Rational people don't risk what they have and need for what
they don't have and don't need."_

The same letter notes that most debt visible on Berkshire's consolidated
balance sheet resides at its railroad and energy subsidiaries — businesses
that are asset-heavy, generate durable cash flows through economic cycles,
and whose debt is not guaranteed by Berkshire. This is the acceptable form
of leverage: isolated, matched to the cash-flow characteristics of the
specific business, and non-recourse to the parent. The parent itself
remains a fortress. _"Berkshire will forever remain a financial fortress.
In managing, I will make expensive mistakes of commission and will also miss
many opportunities, some of which should have been obvious to me. At times,
our stock will tumble as investors flee from equities. But I will never risk
getting caught short of cash."_

By year-end 2018, Berkshire held $112 billion in Treasury bills and
equivalents plus $20 billion in other fixed-income instruments — far above
the $20 billion minimum it has pledged to maintain unconditionally.

## The cash floor raised to $30 billion (2021)

The 2021 letter records an upward revision to Berkshire's minimum cash
commitment. The formal pledge, previously set at $20 billion, was raised
to $30 billion. The letter notes that Berkshire held $144 billion at
year-end, including $120 billion in Treasury bills — far above the new
floor. The rationale for the increase was not a change in philosophy but
a recognition that Berkshire's scale and its potential insurance
liabilities had grown: a $20 billion floor that was appropriate for a
$500 billion company is different in character from a $30 billion floor
for a company whose float alone stood at $147 billion and whose insurance
subsidiary could conceivably face catastrophic losses exceeding any
prior historical benchmark.

The 2022 letter restates the commitment without quoting a figure:
_"Berkshire will always hold a boatload of cash and U.S. Treasury bills
along with a wide array of businesses. We will also avoid behavior that
could result in any uncomfortable cash needs at inconvenient times,
including financial panics and unprecedented insurance losses."_ The
continuity of the pledge across successive letters — first articulated
after the 1939 grandfather's letter, formalised in 2010 as $10B/$20B,
and tightened to $30B in 2021 — is itself evidence that the commitment
is a standing policy rather than a response to any particular crisis.

## Annual meeting additions: LTCM and the derivatives argument (1994–2024)

[[sources/Buffett and Munger Unscripted]] adds two important dimensions
to this page: the LTCM case study as the definitive illustration of leverage
risk, and a fuller treatment of derivatives as systemic leverage.

**LTCM: Russian roulette with no upside.** The Chairman's Letters contain
the Russian-roulette framing in abstract. The annual meetings contain the
LTCM case study as its concrete illustration. Long-Term Capital Management
in 1998 applied massive leverage to positions in sovereign and other bonds
whose correlations had been estimated from historical data. The models said
an event of the magnitude that actually occurred was essentially impossible.
It occurred.

Buffett's formulation at meetings:

_"When you play Russian roulette, it works 83.3% of the time. But 83.3%
is not good enough when you get no benefit from the risk and you can
lose everything."_

The extension: 99% is also not good enough, for the same reason. If the
consequence of the 1-in-100 scenario is complete destruction — as it would
be for a fund using 25:1 leverage — then the expected value calculation is
ruined by that tail regardless of the probability. This is the mathematical
core of the anti-leverage argument: the asymmetry between "you can lose
everything" on the downside and "you can make a little more" on the upside
means that leverage in the pursuit of enhanced returns is a bad trade in
expected value terms when bankruptcy is the tail.

**"You can make a lot of money on Sunday."** The LTCM crisis produced frantic
weekend calls among the world's largest financial institutions and the Federal
Reserve, who were attempting to organise a rescue before markets opened on
Monday. Buffett received one of these calls. His comment: _"You can make a
lot of money on Sunday"_ — meaning that when a highly levered counterparty
is desperate, the terms available to someone with liquidity and no leverage
are extraordinary. The Berkshire model is, in part, structured to ensure it
is always the Sunday buyer and never the Sunday seller.

**Derivatives as a combination of ignorance and borrowing.** Munger's
characterisation of derivative books at large financial institutions:
"a combination of ignorance and borrowing." The ignorance component: large
derivative books contain thousands of positions, many of which are valued
by model rather than by market, and which interact in ways that are not
fully understood even by the institutions holding them. The borrowing
component: derivatives are implicitly or explicitly levered. When the
ignorance and the leverage interact — as they did in 2008 — the result
can be systemic.

This is distinct from the individual company's leverage problem documented
in the Chairman's Letters. It is a systemic problem: when many large
institutions hold similar levered positions built on similar models, the
correlation of their failures during a stress event is high. Each individual
institution may have managed its risk by diversifying within its book; but
the books of all the institutions are correlated, which means the apparent
diversification is illusory. See [[concepts/Important and Knowable]] — the
distributions of extreme financial events are not knowable in the way
normal distributions are, which means model-based risk management
systematically underestimates tail exposure.

**The Berkshire counter-structure.** The annual meeting discussions make
explicit what the Letters describe more formally: Berkshire is deliberately
structured as the anti-LTCM. No leverage at the parent. Permanent capital.
A cash hoard sufficient to act in crises. Insurance float that is patient
and does not require margin. No outside investors who can demand redemption.
This structure is not an accident; it is a specific response to the known
failure modes of leveraged institutions.
