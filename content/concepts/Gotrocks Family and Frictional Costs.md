# Gotrocks Family and Frictional Costs

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 2005 letter, "How to
Minimise Investment Returns" section; extended in the 2006 letter;
The Bet partial results in the 2016 letter and final results in the
2017 letter. [[sources/Poor Charlie's Almanack]] — Talk 2 ("A Lesson on
Elementary Worldly Wisdom," 1994): the Galbraith bezzle and Munger's
extension to "febezzlement."

## Summary

The Gotrocks allegory is Buffett's most complete explanation of why the
aggregate returns earned by investors must always fall below the returns
earned by the businesses they collectively own — and why that gap has been
growing. The allegory is short, but its logic is airtight: every dollar paid
to a financial intermediary is a dollar subtracted from the principal's
returns, and the intermediaries have been multiplying.

The setup: imagine that all American corporations are permanently owned by a
single family, the Gotrocks. Generation after generation, the family grows
richer at the rate the underlying businesses earn — currently about \$700
billion per year after tax. No money is lost to trading, no fees leave the
family. The aggregate gain is the aggregate business earnings. *"In the
Gotrocks household everyone grows wealthier at the same pace, and all is
harmonious."*

Then the Helpers arrive.

## The four layers of extraction

The first set of Helpers are brokers, who persuade individual family members
to trade with each other in order to get ahead of their relatives. No wealth
is created by the trading — the family still owns all of corporate America —
but the commissions are real. *"The more that family members trade, the
smaller their share of the pie and the larger the slice received by the
Helpers. This fact is not lost upon these broker-Helpers: Activity is their
friend and, in a wide variety of ways, they urge it on."*

After a while, most family members recognise they are not doing well at the
"beat-my-brother" game. They hire professionals: the manager-Helpers, fund
managers who may even increase trading activity to allow the broker-Helpers
to prosper further. A bigger slice now goes to two classes of Helpers.

The family's results worsen. Enter financial planners and institutional
consultants, adviser-Helpers who counsel the Gotrocks on selecting managers.
The allegory notes the logical problem the family fails to notice: *"By now
its members know they can pick neither the right stocks nor the right
stock-pickers. Why, one might ask, should they expect success in picking the
right consultant? But this question does not occur to the Gotrocks."*

Finally, the hyper-Helpers appear. They explain that the existing Helpers are
unmotivated zombies, and that the solution is to pay more. *"Some of the
hyper-Helpers are really just manager-Helpers wearing new uniforms, bearing
sewn-on sexy names like HEDGE FUND or PRIVATE EQUITY."* The new arrivals
assure the family that this change of clothing confers magical powers. Calmed
by this explanation, the family decides to pay up.

## The 2-and-20 arithmetic

The 2006 letter extends the allegory into explicit arithmetic for the
dominant hyper-Helper fee structure. Two per cent of principal is charged
each year regardless of performance; twenty per cent of profits is taken when
the manager succeeds. The worked example: a manager who achieves a gross
return of 10% in a year keeps 3.6 percentage points — two off the top plus
20% of the residual 8 points — leaving only 6.4 percentage points for the
investor. On a \$3 billion fund, this delivers the manager \$108 million, even
if an index fund returned 15% to investors in the same period and charged a
token fee. The investor underperforms the market by 8.6 percentage points
and pays \$108 million for the privilege.

*"The inexorable math of this grotesque arrangement is certain to make the
Gotrocks family poorer over time than it would have been had it never heard
of these 'hyper-helpers.'"*

Wall Street's response to the family's disappointment is to assure everyone
that above-average performance is available to all investors simultaneously —
but only by paying ever-higher fees. The 2006 letter names this *"the adult
version of Lake Woebegon"* — the fictional town where all the children are
above average.

## Newton's Fourth Law

The 2005 letter closes the Gotrocks section with Isaac Newton. Newton lost a
fortune in the South Sea Bubble of 1720 and observed afterwards: *"I can
calculate the movement of the stars, but not the madness of men."* Had he
survived that trauma to continue his investigations, Buffett suggests, he
might have discovered a Fourth Law of Motion to complement his three laws of
physics: *"For investors as a whole, returns decrease as motion increases."*

The law is not a criticism of any individual Helper. It is a structural
observation. The aggregate returns available to all investors are fixed by
the returns of the underlying businesses. Every dollar redirected to
intermediaries is permanently removed from that aggregate. The only variable
is how much is redirected — and the evidence from recent decades is that the
amount has been growing very fast.

## What this does to the analytical problem

The allegory reframes the question investors face. The question is not whether
any individual can outperform by hiring good Helpers; some can. The question
is whether the Helpers as a class add enough value to justify their aggregate
take. Buffett's answer is no: *"in the Gotrocks household, frictional costs
of all sorts may well amount to 20% of the earnings of American business."*
Eighty per cent reaches the principals; twenty per cent is retained by the
intermediaries. The equilibrium result of each family member trying to beat
the others is that all of them collectively end up with less.

The implication is not nihilistic. A passive investor who simply owns the
Gotrocks' proportional share without trading or hiring Helpers captures the
full business return. The Berkshire preference for low-cost passive index
funds for ordinary investors, stated repeatedly across the letters, is the
practical consequence of this analysis.

## What this is not

It is not an argument against all professional advice. Some managers do
outperform by meaningful margins sustained over long periods; the [[Investment Risk vs Beta]] page documents Walter Schloss's 47-year record as the most
rigorous available counter-example to the claim that no outperformance is
possible. The Gotrocks allegory is an argument about the *aggregate*, not
about every individual case. The problem is structural: even if some Helpers
earn their keep, the system as a whole cannot deliver more than the
businesses produce, and the system charges substantially for trying.

## The Bet: proof by experiment (2016–2017)

The Gotrocks allegory is a theoretical argument. Beginning in the 2016
letter and concluding in the 2017 letter, Buffett presents a decade-long
controlled experiment that converts the theory into data.

On 19 December 2007, Buffett made a \$500,000 wager with investment firm
Protégé Partners: a single, virtually cost-free S&P 500 index fund against
five hand-selected funds-of-funds, each of which in turn held interests in
more than 200 underlying hedge funds. The 200-plus hedge funds were managed
by what Buffett calls *"an elite crew, loaded with brains, adrenaline and
confidence."* All actors were highly incentivised — fixed management fees
averaging 2.5% of assets, plus performance fees, flowing to both the
fund-of-funds managers and the underlying hedge fund managers. Protégé also
had the ability to replace underperforming funds during the decade, selecting
new "stars" as required.

The final scorecard after ten years:

| | A | B | C | D | E | S&P Index |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total gain | 21.7% | 42.3% | 87.7% | 2.8% | 27.0% | **125.8%** |
| Annual average | 2.0% | 3.6% | 6.5% | 0.3% | 2.4% | **8.5%** |

The five funds-of-funds got off to a fast start — each beating the index
fund in 2008. Then, in every one of the nine subsequent years, the
funds-of-funds as a whole trailed the index. The environment over the decade
was unremarkable: had a poll of investment experts been asked in late 2007
to forecast long-term stock-market returns, their average guess would have
been close to the 8.5% the S&P actually delivered. Making money in that
environment should not have been difficult. *"While this group prospered,
however, many of their investors experienced a lost decade."*

Buffett's three-word verdict: *"Performance comes, performance goes. Fees
never falter."*

The result is a direct empirical instantiation of the Gotrocks allegory. The
aggregate returns available to investors in the S&P were fixed by the
businesses themselves. Investor A's gain above that aggregate had to come at
the expense of investor B. The helpers — the hundreds of hedge fund managers
and five fund-of-funds managers — collectively consumed fees that far
exceeded any aggregate outperformance they generated.

The 2017 letter draws three additional lessons from the experiment. First,
markets are generally rational but occasionally do crazy things; seizing
those opportunities requires *"an ability to both disregard mob fears or
enthusiasms and to focus on a few simple fundamentals"* — not superior
intelligence or professional credentials. Second, the correct strategy is
to stick with big, easy decisions and avoid activity. The 200-plus hedge
fund managers made tens of thousands of buy and sell decisions over the
decade. Buffett and Protégé made exactly one: selling zero-coupon Treasury
bonds yielding 0.88% in late 2012 and moving the proceeds into Berkshire
shares. Third, the passive investor who simply avoids paying helpers
captures the full business return; each layer of intermediation extracts
a fraction of that return permanently.

The Jack Bogle tribute in the 2016 letter names the practical implication:
*"If 'investors' as a whole cannot hope to outperform the market (since they
collectively are the market), a diversified, low-cost index fund can beat
the great majority of investment professionals over the long run… Both large
and small investors should stick with low-cost index funds."* Bogle is
described as *"a hero to them and to me."*

## Febezzlement: when rising markets conceal the take

Munger's most original extension of the Gotrocks logic appears in Talk 2
of the Almanack, where he reaches back to John Kenneth Galbraith's 1955
work *The Great Crash* for the concept of the "bezzle."

Galbraith's insight: embezzlement creates a curious interval of inflated
happiness. During the period between when money is taken and when the
theft is discovered, the embezzler feels richer and the victim does not
yet feel poorer — both parties, simultaneously, believe they own the
same asset. Net happiness in the world is momentarily above what the
underlying reality supports. Galbraith called this surplus of illusory
wealth the "bezzle," and noted that it expands in boom times (when
controls are relaxed and opportunities multiply) and contracts sharply
in busts (when discovery arrives).

Munger's extension is named "febezzlement" — the fee-bezzle. The
mechanism is the same but the agent is different: instead of an
embezzler, the extractor is the financial intermediary industry. In a
rising market, investors feel prosperous even as their after-fee returns
are running substantially below what passive ownership would have
produced. The fees have been charged; the returns have been reduced; but
because prices are rising, the investor's account balance is going up
anyway. The extraction is invisible. Net happiness is inflated above what
the underlying return reality supports.

The illusion persists as long as markets rise. When markets fall or
flatten, the drag becomes visible: investors who were paying 2-and-20
discover that a decade of intermediary fees has consumed what would
otherwise have been a substantial fraction of their wealth. The
febezzlement is then recognised — but it cannot be recovered. Munger's
point is that the Gotrocks allegory is not merely a theoretical argument
about aggregates; the febezzlement framework explains why the damage
goes unnoticed and unchallenged for so long. Rising markets are the
embezzler's alibi.
