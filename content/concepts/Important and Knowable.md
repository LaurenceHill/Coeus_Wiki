# Important and Knowable

**Source:** [[sources/Buffett and Munger Unscripted]] — Part 10 (Economics),
drawn from multiple annual meetings across the 1994–2024 period

## Summary

The framework is a two-by-two filter for deciding where to direct analytical
attention. Any question about the world can be placed in one of four boxes:
important-and-knowable, important-but-unknowable, unimportant-and-knowable,
and unimportant-and-unknowable. Investment thinking should concentrate almost
exclusively on the first box and consciously ignore the second.

The practical target of the framework is macroeconomic forecasting. Buffett
and Munger's consistent position across three decades is that macroeconomic
variables — GDP growth, interest rate direction, inflation trajectory,
currency movements, election outcomes — are _important_ in the sense that
they affect everything, but they are _not knowable_ with sufficient precision
or consistency to be a sound basis for investment decisions. The correct
response to an important-but-unknowable question is not to try harder to
answer it. It is to design your investment approach so that the question does
not need to be answered.

## The Four-Box Filter Applied to Investing

**Important and knowable** — the target zone. These are the questions
investment analysis should be built around: whether a business has durable
competitive advantages, whether management is honest and capable, whether
the price paid is reasonable relative to intrinsic value, whether the moat
can be assessed from available evidence. Buffett's term for this zone is
[[concepts/Circle of Competence]]: "If you have doubts about whether
something is in your circle, it isn't."

**Important but unknowable** — the danger zone. Macro variables belong here.
Interest rates, exchange rates, GDP growth, election results. These things
will have enormous effect on asset prices, but they cannot be forecast
reliably enough to build a repeatable investment process around. The correct
move is explicit acknowledgement: "That's important, and I can't know it,
so I'll design around it." The worst move is to spend serious time generating
confident macro forecasts — this allocates analytical capital to the second
box under the illusion that it is the first.

**Unimportant and knowable** — a temptation to avoid. Much of what analysts
can readily calculate — near-term earnings to the penny, quarterly same-store
sales — falls here. It is knowable and precise, which makes it feel
productive, but it rarely determines long-run investment outcomes.

**Unimportant and unknowable** — ignore entirely.

## Macro as Important-but-Unknowable

Buffett's specific statements across many meetings form a consistent doctrine:

_"Macro is important, but it's not knowable. We focus on things that are both
important and knowable."_

This is not false modesty or evasion. It is a considered position held
through periods when Buffett had every incentive — social, financial,
reputational — to have macro views. His operational statement is that he
has never made a buy or sell decision based on a macroeconomic forecast, and
he does not know a single investor who has generated a sustained record from
doing so. The record of professional macro forecasters, carefully examined, is
not materially better than chance on the questions that matter most (recession
timing, rate inflection points, currency turning points).

The admission extends to Berkshire's own timing mistakes. Buffett has noted
explicitly that he bought $$16 billion of securities at terrible prices in
September 2008, missed the March 2009 bottom, and missed the March 2020
bottom — all cases where macro reading or market timing would have helped.
His conclusion is not "I should try harder to time markets" but "we have not
been good at timing, and we shouldn't pretend otherwise." See also
[[Patience as Competitive Advantage]] and [[Mr. Market]].

## Interest Rates as Gravity

One macro variable Buffett does discuss at length — not because it is
predictable but because its _current level_ is directly usable in valuation
— is the interest rate. His formulation: _"Interest rates are to asset prices
what gravity is to matter."_ The higher the rate, the greater the
gravitational pull downward on the present value of future cash flows.

This is not a forecast of where rates will go. It is a statement that the
current rate is an input to every intrinsic value calculation, and that
changes in the rate mechanically reprice all assets simultaneously. Investors
who ignore the rate level in their valuation work are omitting a material
variable. The zero-interest-rate environment of 2009–2021 was, in Buffett's
words, "the most interesting movie we've ever seen" — not because he could
predict how it would end, but because the mechanics of repricing at zero
gravity were unlike anything previously observed.

## Munger's "Currents" Formulation

Munger has a complementary way of stating the same idea, drawn from nautical
metaphor:

_"We're not predicting the currents that come. We're just figuring out how
some things will swim in whatever currents arise."_

This is the positive version of the macro agnosticism. Rather than attempting
to forecast the macro environment, the task is to find businesses and
positions whose fundamental economics are robust across a range of plausible
macro environments. See's Candies, GEICO, and Coca-Cola were not acquired
because Buffett predicted the exact macroeconomic conditions of the following
decades. They were acquired because their economics were attractive enough
that they would be good businesses in most environments.

## Inflation and the Ranking of Hedges

One application of the Important-and-Knowable framework: given that high
inflation is a real possibility (important), and given that its timing and
severity are not predictable (unknowable), what should investors do? Buffett's
ranking of inflation hedges from best to worst:

**1. Your own earning power** — the most inflation-resistant asset is
competence that is continuously in demand. Skills, particularly rare or
technical ones, reprice with inflation.

**2. A wonderful business with pricing power and low capital needs** — the
ideal is a business that can raise prices without losing customers and does
not need to reinvest at inflated capital costs to maintain its economics.
See's Candies was the teaching case: approximately $$7–9 million of tangible
assets at the time of acquisition generated $$30 million in revenues, and by
the time revenues reached $$300 million only $$40 million of tangible capital was
required. The pricing power is the key variable; capital intensity is the
killer.

**3. A mediocre business with pricing power** — better than a capital-
intensive business with no pricing power, but the capital drag still erodes
returns in high inflation.

**4. Gold** — Buffett's repeated critique: gold produces nothing. An ounce
of gold in 1900 is still an ounce of gold in 2024. The investor who held
Coca-Cola stock, or good farmland, or a productive business for the same
period captured both the inflation protection and the real return. Gold
captures only the former and only imperfectly. It is a bet on fear, not
on productivity.

**Worst: Capital-intensive businesses with weak pricing power** — airlines
pre-COVID are the canonical example. Equipment must be replaced at inflated
costs; labour reprices upward; fuel costs rise; but competitive pressure
prevents pricing-through. The inflation wipes out the economics.

See [[concepts/Great, Good and Gruesome]] for the capital-intensity
taxonomy that maps onto this inflation ranking.

## LTCM and the Limits of Confidence

The clearest case study of what happens when important-but-unknowable
questions are treated as knowable: Long-Term Capital Management in 1998.
LTCM's models said a 25-standard-deviation event was essentially impossible.
It happened. Buffett's formulation:

_"When you play Russian roulette, it works 83.3% of the time. But 83.3%
is not good enough when you get no benefit from the risk — and you can
lose everything."_

The LTCM partners — among the most quantitatively sophisticated investors
ever assembled — were treating macro correlations as knowable when they
were not. They believed their models had captured the full distribution of
possible outcomes. They had not. The lesson is not that models are useless,
but that treating a model's output as knowable when the underlying inputs
(correlation structures during stress) are unknowable produces fragility
precisely when it is most costly. See [[concepts/Leverage and Liquidity]]
for the full treatment of the LTCM case.

## Relationship to Circle of Competence

The Important-and-Knowable framework is the macro-level application of
[[concepts/Circle of Competence]]. The circle defines what an investor can
know about businesses and industries. The Important-and-Knowable filter
defines what an investor can know about the broader environment those
businesses operate in. Both lead to the same behavioural prescription:
work hard on what you can actually know, and design around what you cannot.

The investor who applies both consistently ends up with a portfolio of
businesses whose economics are well understood, purchased at prices that
do not require accurate macro forecasts to justify, structured to be robust
across a range of macro environments. This is not a strategy of ignorance —
it is a strategy of epistemic discipline.
