# Scuttlebutt

**Source:** [[Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits]] — Chapter 2, "What
Scuttlebutt Can Do"; Chapter 10, "How I Go About Finding a Growth Stock";
limitations discussed in [[Paths to Wealth Through Common Stocks]] — Part III

## Summary

Scuttlebutt is Fisher's term for the research method at the centre of his
investment approach: systematic, direct conversation with people who have
first-hand knowledge of a business and its competitive position. The
network includes customers, competitors, suppliers, former employees, trade
association executives, scientists and researchers in relevant fields, and
executives at the company itself. Fisher calls this the "business grapevine"
and treats it as the primary source of investment insight that published
analysis cannot replicate.

The core claim is that printed financial records tell you what has happened.
The grapevine tells you what is happening and what is likely to happen next.
A company's annual report will show you last year's sales and margin. A
conversation with three or four of that company's major customers will tell
you whether those customers are planning to increase or decrease their
purchases, whether they regard the company's products as superior or merely
adequate, and whether a competitor is making inroads. The first set of
information is available to every analyst on the same day; the second is
available only to someone willing to do the legwork.

## The method in practice

Fisher describes scuttlebutt as less mysterious in practice than it sounds in
description. He begins with the premise that most knowledgeable people —
executives at competing companies, academic scientists familiar with an
industry, customers with opinions about the products they buy — are
genuinely willing to share what they know, for two reasons. First, people
generally like to talk about their areas of expertise. Second, conversations
with investors are understood to be reciprocal: the analyst who asks useful
questions and shares general impressions of the industry creates a mutually
beneficial exchange.

The standard approach Fisher describes is to contact four or five competitors
of the company under investigation and arrange calls or meetings. The
central question — asked directly or worked toward through related
questions — is what they think of the company: its products, its sales force,
its management reputation, its technical capabilities. Competitors are
motivated to be critical, which means their positive assessments carry
particular weight. A competitor who says a rival's products are genuinely
superior, or that the rival's management is unusually capable, is providing
signal that is hard to fake.

Fisher then extends the inquiry to customers, to suppliers who know the
company's purchasing practices and internal culture, and to former
employees who understand the internal environment from direct experience.
The result, after five to ten such conversations, is typically a picture of the
company's real competitive position that no financial statement can convey.

Chapter 10 illustrates the method with a concrete example. Fisher describes
how he approached the evaluation of a specific company by making contact
first through a broker he trusted, who made an introduction to an executive
at the target company. That executive agreed to a plant visit. Fisher used the
visit not primarily to see the physical operation but to meet the management
team, ask questions about the research pipeline and the sales organisation,
and observe how people at different levels of the company interacted. He
then followed up with conversations with the company's major customers
and with contacts at two competitors. The picture that emerged from those
conversations, he argues, was far richer than anything he could have
obtained from the published accounts.

## What scuttlebutt is not

Fisher is careful to distinguish the scuttlebutt method from rumour-chasing
or from the kind of tip-gathering that characterises speculative trading.
Scuttlebutt is structured, not casual. The investor is not looking for advance
notice of an earnings surprise; she is building a picture of the underlying
quality of a business — its management culture, its competitive position,
its research pipeline — that will be relevant not for the next quarter but for
the next decade. The time horizon of the questions matches the time horizon
of the investment.

The method also does not depend on material non-public information. The
conversations Fisher describes involve impressions, assessments, and
competitive observations — the kind of qualitative judgements that
competitors, customers, and suppliers form from their own direct experience
of dealing with a company. None of this is secret. All of it requires effort
to obtain. The edge comes from the willingness to do the work, not from
access to information that others are prohibited from having.

## Why most investors do not do this

Fisher's implicit argument throughout the book is that the scuttlebutt
method is systematically underused because it requires effort that does not
scale easily for large institutional portfolios. An analyst covering forty
companies cannot conduct ten conversations per company per year. The
institutional advantage in capital and information access does not translate
into an advantage in the kind of qualitative primary research Fisher
describes. The individual investor who is willing to cover a small number
of companies thoroughly is, on this dimension, at no disadvantage to the
institution.

This observation anticipates Sleep's argument in the Nomad letters about
the structural advantage available to patient, focused investors who are
willing to hold a small number of positions rather than the broadly
diversified portfolios that institutional mandates tend to require (see
[[Concentration vs Diversification]] and [[Patience as Competitive Advantage]]).

## The relationship to the Fifteen Points

The [[Fifteen Points]] are the structured list of what to investigate.
Scuttlebutt is the primary method for investigating most of them. Points 7
through 9 (labour relations, executive relations, management depth) and
points 14 and 15 (management candour and integrity) are almost entirely
inaccessible from published sources; they require exactly the kind of direct
conversation Fisher describes. Points 4 (sales organisation) and 11 (industry-
specific key success factors) also benefit more from scuttlebutt than from
financial analysis. Only points 5 and 6 (profit margins and margin
maintenance) are primarily derived from the accounts, and even those are
enriched by conversations with competitors who can speak to the structural
reasons margins are high or low.

Fisher's research process is therefore not sequential — scuttlebutt first, then
financial analysis — but interleaved. The accounts identify where to probe;
the conversations explain what the accounts mean; the accounts then confirm
or challenge what the conversations suggested.

## Fisher's own critique and its resolution

In *Paths to Wealth Through Common Stocks* (1960), Fisher acknowledges what he regards as the principal weakness of the scuttlebutt method: it is too slow. Identifying the right informants, arranging introductions, conducting conversations, and cross-checking what each source says against what others have said takes months when done thoroughly. In a market where investment opportunities can be recognised and priced by others in a matter of weeks, this lag is a genuine disadvantage. A solo analyst working the method correctly may find that by the time he has assembled a full picture, the price has already moved.

Fisher's proposed solution in 1960 was a structural one: teams of geographically distributed specialists who collectively cover the full landscape of informants for a particular industry. Rather than one analyst in New York making two trips a year "around the country," Fisher envisioned a team with one member in each major geographic centre of an industry, each maintaining dense networks of contacts in their region. The collective contacts of such a team would allow the scuttlebutt picture to be assembled in days rather than months. Cross-checking across team members would also reduce the influence of any individual's biases or prejudices.

Fisher acknowledged this was a prediction rather than a description of existing practice. He saw it as the natural evolution of investment research, comparable to the shift from individual inventors to corporate research laboratories — more expensive, slower to get right initially, but ultimately vastly more productive. Whether the prediction was accurate is debatable; some large investment firms have developed something like this structure, while the best individual practitioners have tended to maintain Fisher's original solo method with the benefit of modern communications technology that reduces the geographic friction Fisher identified.

The critique itself is worth noting separately from the resolution. Fisher's acknowledgement that a method he advocated has a genuine structural limitation is an example of the intellectual honesty the wiki tries to preserve throughout. Good analytical frameworks acknowledge the conditions under which they work less well.

## Parallel in other sources

Fisher's scuttlebutt method is the research equivalent of what the Nomad
letters describe as deep understanding of a business model. Sleep does not
use the same vocabulary, but his accounts of understanding Amazon's
flywheel (see [[Flywheel]]) and Costco's operating model required
the same kind of sustained, direct engagement with the competitive reality
of those businesses that Fisher prescribes. The method is similar; the
specific application differs because Sleep was primarily evaluating
businesses from the outside while Fisher describes a process that includes
direct access to management through plant visits and one-on-one meetings.

## Buffett's application: the silver bullet and 20,000 Moody's pages

[[sources/Buffett and Munger Unscripted]] provides two specific
illustrations of scuttlebutt-style research in Buffett's own practice that
do not appear in the Chairman's Letters.

**The silver bullet method.** Buffett has described at annual meetings a
thought experiment he has applied when evaluating a company's competitive
position: *"If I could only ask one person one question to determine whether
to invest in this company, what would that question be?"* The discipline
forces analytical priorities. For a branded consumer goods company, the
question might be about pricing power. For a financial services firm,
about the consistency of underwriting discipline over a full cycle. For a
retailer, about the durability of the cost advantage relative to new entrants.

The silver bullet question is scuttlebutt concentrated to its essence: it
identifies the single most important fact that would determine whether the
investment thesis holds, and directs research energy toward verifying or
falsifying that specific fact rather than producing a comprehensive but
unfocused research report.

**The 20,000 Moody's pages.** Buffett has described beginning his investment
career by reading through the entire Moody's Manual — approximately 20,000
pages — looking for investment ideas. He found many. The exercise illustrates
a form of scuttlebutt applied to the written record: systematic coverage
of a large body of data in search of situations where the market's assessment
is obviously wrong relative to the facts available in the public record.
The method is not glamorous; it is exhaustive. But it produced actionable
ideas precisely because most investors don't do it.

**The Harrisburg story.** Among the discoveries in the Moody's Manual
was a small bond situation in an obscure municipality — the kind of
security that no analyst covered and no institution cared about, but
which offered a large discrepancy between stated value and market price.
Buffett invested and made money. The scuttlebutt lesson: the most
exploitable mispricings are in places where no one is looking, because
the attention of the market is the mechanism that corrects mispricings.
The investors who are willing to cover the ground that others won't
find the situations others can't.
