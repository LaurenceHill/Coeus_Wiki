# Investment Categories

**Source:** [[Berkshire Chairman's Letters]] — 2011 letter, "The Basic Choices for Investors and the One We Strongly Prefer"; 2024 letter, "Where Your Money Is" section

## Summary

In the 2011 letter Buffett offers his most systematic taxonomy of investment options. The framework divides every investment into one of three categories: currency-based instruments, non-productive assets, and productive assets. The argument is not neutral — Buffett states his preference plainly and argues from first principles that the third category is not just the most attractive but, over long periods, the safest.

The taxonomy is useful because it cuts across conventional asset-class boundaries. Stocks and bonds are normally treated as the primary axis of investment choice. Buffett's framework recasts the question: instead of asking which financial instrument to hold, it asks what underlying economic claim the investor is acquiring and whether that claim can compound in real terms through time.

## Currency-based instruments

The first category includes money-market funds, bonds, mortgages, bank deposits, and any other instrument denominated in a fixed unit of currency. These are widely described as "safe." Buffett argues the opposite: they are among the most dangerous assets available, because their beta is zero while their real risk is large.

The danger is inflation. Governments control the value of their currency, and history shows they periodically allow or engineer inflation that destroys the purchasing power of currency-based claims. The dollar fell 86% in purchasing-power terms between 1965 and 2011. A tax-free institution would have needed 4.3% annual interest from bonds over that period merely to stand still in real terms. A taxable investor paying 25% income tax on 5.7% Treasury bill yields — the approximate actual return over the same period — received nothing in real income: 1.4 percentage points went to visible income tax and the remaining 4.3 points to the invisible inflation tax. _"It's noteworthy that the implicit inflation 'tax' was more than triple the explicit income tax that our investor probably thought of as his main burden."_

Buffett's formulation is sharp: bonds are now _"priced to deliver return-free risk."_ The label inverts the standard description of government bonds as risk-free return. The inversion is not rhetorical; it follows directly from the arithmetic of nominal yields relative to plausible inflation paths. See [[Inflation Tax]] for the full treatment.

Currency-based instruments have a narrow legitimate use. Berkshire holds them for liquidity — specifically US Treasury bills — because they are the only instrument that can reliably be liquidated in chaotic conditions. The minimum holding at the time of the 2011 letter was $20 billion, the absolute floor $10 billion. (The formal minimum was subsequently raised to $30 billion in the 2021 letter — see [[Leverage and Liquidity]] for the current framework.) Beyond this operational requirement, Buffett declines them unless they offer the prospect of unusual gain from mispricing or from rates elevated enough to deliver real capital gains on subsequent decline. In 2011, neither condition applied.

## Non-productive assets

The second category contains assets that will never produce anything but that are purchased in the expectation that others will pay more for them later. Tulips in the seventeenth century were the historical example. Gold is the contemporary one.

The structure of the trade is identical in every non-productive asset: it requires an ever-expanding pool of buyers who also believe the pool will expand further. The buyer is not purchasing a productive claim; the asset will remain permanently inert. Its price is therefore purely a function of belief and sentiment, not of earnings or output. _"What motivates most gold purchasers is their belief that the ranks of the fearful will grow."_

The comparison Buffett constructs to illustrate gold's position is worth dwelling on. At 2011 prices, all the world's gold — some 170,000 metric tons, which would form a cube 68 feet on a side — was worth approximately $9.6 trillion. For the same sum one could have purchased all US cropland (400 million acres, producing roughly $200 billion of output annually) plus sixteen Exxon Mobils (each earning over $40 billion per year), with roughly $1 trillion left as walking-around money. A century hence, the 400 million acres will still be producing corn and wheat and cotton. The sixteen Exxon Mobils will have delivered trillions in dividends and their successors will hold assets worth trillions more. The 170,000 tonnes of gold will still be 170,000 tonnes of gold, incapable of producing anything.

This does not mean gold cannot rise in price; it clearly can, and it did over the preceding decade. But rising prices in non-productive assets are driven entirely by sentiment, not by any improvement in the underlying claim. _"As 'bandwagon' investors join any party, they create their own truth — for a while."_ Bubbles in non-productive assets — Buffett cites internet stocks and houses as recent examples — arise from the same mechanism: an initially plausible premise combined with rising prices, which attract further buyers, which further raise prices, until the feedback loop breaks. _"Bubbles blown large enough inevitably pop."_

## Productive assets

The third category — Buffett's strong preference — covers businesses, farms, and real estate. What these share is that they produce real output, and that output has value regardless of what the currency does.

The essential criterion is not that a productive asset be free of price volatility but that it deliver increased purchasing power over the holding period. A business like Coca-Cola, IBM, or See's Candy passes this test because its output — beverages, technology services, peanut brittle — will be exchanged for a portion of human labour regardless of the currency in which prices are quoted. _"Whether the currency a century from now is based on gold, seashells, shark teeth, or a piece of paper, people will be willing to exchange a couple of minutes of their daily labour for a Coca-Cola or some See's peanut brittle."_ The medium of exchange does not determine the value of the productive claim; the output does.

Within the productive category, quality varies substantially. Regulated utilities, for instance, pass the productive-asset test but fail a secondary test: in inflationary periods they require heavy additional capital investment just to earn more. Their owners must invest more to get more, which means inflation is partly passed through to shareholders as a capital call rather than a gain. Unregulated businesses with pricing power — See's Candy being Buffett's most-cited example — do not face this problem, because inflation in input costs can be passed to customers without requiring proportional additional capital. The distinction maps directly onto the [[Great, Good and Gruesome]] taxonomy of capital intensity and returns.

The practical implication for long-term investors is that productive assets are not just the most attractive category but the most conservative over multi-decade horizons. Currency-based instruments feel safe because their nominal value is stable; productive assets feel risky because their price fluctuates. Buffett argues this perception is backwards. _"A non-fluctuating asset can be laden with risk"_ while an asset with volatile prices can be genuinely safe if it is certain to deliver more purchasing power over the investor's contemplated holding period.

## The asymmetry with currency-based instruments

Buffett makes one further observation that connects the first and third categories. Both currency-based instruments and non-productive assets enjoy maximum popularity at peaks of fear: credit collapses drive investors to Treasury bills ("cash is king") and currency-collapse fears drive them to gold. Both reactions tend to peak at precisely the moments when productive assets are cheapest — when businesses are undervalued because fear has depressed prices. _"We heard 'cash is king' in late 2008, just when cash should have been deployed rather than held."_ The investor who uses currency-based instruments as a refuge from fear rather than as an operational necessity pays a double price: the return on the refuge is poor, and the productive assets available at that moment are foregone.

This asymmetry between popular perception and analytical reality is the practical consequence of the taxonomy. It does not require a view on the short-term direction of markets. It requires only a long enough holding period and a willingness to think in real rather than nominal terms.

## The definitive equity preference (2024)

The 2024 letter — "Where Your Money Is" — provides the most unambiguous statement of the productive-assets preference in the corpus. Despite commentary at the time about Berkshire's large cash position ($272 billion in marketable equities at year-end, down from $354 billion the prior year), Buffett is emphatic: _"Berkshire will never prefer ownership of cash-equivalent assets over the ownership of good businesses, whether controlled or only partially owned."_

The argument runs on two rails. The first is inflation and currency risk. _"Paper money can see its value evaporate if fiscal folly prevails. In some countries, this reckless practice has become habitual, and, in our country's short history, the U.S. has come close to the edge."_ The second is the inability of fixed-coupon instruments to provide any protection: _"Fixed-coupon bonds provide no protection against runaway currency."_ Both claims are consistent with the 2011 taxonomy but stated here with greater directness — bonds are named, not merely implied, as the instrument that fails the real-purchasing-power test.

The 2024 letter also makes a personal claim that grounds the abstract preference in autobiography: _"Lacking such assets as athletic excellence, a wonderful voice, medical or legal skills or, for that matter, any special talents, I have had to rely on equities throughout my life. In effect, I have depended on the success of American businesses and I will continue to do so."_ This is not false modesty — it is a statement that the equity preference is not an investment opinion to be revised when conditions change, but a structural commitment derived from the productive-assets logic developed in the 2011 letter and the [[American Tailwind]] essays.

## Contradictions

None within Buffett's framework. The productive-assets preference is fully consistent with Fisher's argument in [[Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits]] that equities in good businesses are the superior long-term vehicle, and with Sleep's [[Nomad Partnership Letters]] argument that returns compound from business quality rather than asset-class selection. The specific case against gold sits in tension with any gold-as-insurance argument (i.e., gold as hedge against catastrophic currency failure); Buffett acknowledges that in an extreme scenario of currency collapse, people will still rush to gold. His point is that over any plausible century-long horizon the productive-assets portfolio overwhelms the gold portfolio in return, not that gold cannot rise.
