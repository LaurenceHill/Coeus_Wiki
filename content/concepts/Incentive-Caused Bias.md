# Incentive-Caused Bias

**Source:** [[Psychology of Human Misjudgment]] — Tendency #1, "Reward and
Punishment Superresponse Tendency"; elaborated throughout the speech and
Q&A. [[sources/Poor Charlie's Almanack]] — the Federal Express overnight
example (Talk 2); the California workers' compensation case study and
Mormon community comparison (Talk 11 Q&A); the Salomon general counsel
failure and Ben Franklin principle (Talk 11 and Q&A).

## Summary

Incentive-caused bias is Munger's name for a specific and pervasive
consequence of Reward Superresponse Tendency: a person of normal decency
and good intentions, driven consciously and subconsciously by the incentive
structures around them, drifts into harmful behaviour and then rationalises
it — often without recognising that the drift has occurred at all.

The distinction between ordinary "incentives matter" economics and
incentive-caused bias is subtle but important. Standard economics says
people respond to incentives — give people higher pay for better work
and you get better work. Incentive-caused bias describes something darker:
that incentives can cause people who would never consciously choose to harm
others to do exactly that, while constructing a sincere narrative explaining
why their behaviour is actually correct or even admirable.

Munger places this tendency first not because it is the most psychologically
interesting but because it is the most consequential. *"I think I've been in
the top five percent of my age cohort almost all my adult life in understanding
the power of incentives, and yet I've always underestimated that power. Never
a year passes but I get some surprise that pushes a little further my
appreciation of incentive superpower."*

## The mechanism: rationalisation, not dishonesty

The critical feature of incentive-caused bias is that it typically does not
operate through conscious deception. Munger's surgeon example makes this
precise: a surgeon in Lincoln, Nebraska sent bushel baskets of normal gall
bladders to the pathology lab year after year — removing healthy organs from
patients who did not need the surgery. When asked whether this surgeon
thought "here is a way to make money by maiming people," a doctor who
participated in his eventual removal answered: *"Hell no, Charlie. He thought
that the gall bladder was the source of all medical evil, and, if you really
loved your patients, you couldn't get that organ out rapidly enough."*

The surgeon had rationalised his incentive-driven behaviour into a sincere
belief. This is not a story about a bad man doing bad things for money. It
is a story about a normal man whose incentive structure had silently
corrupted his professional judgement — and who believed, with genuine
conviction, that he was acting in his patients' best interests.

Munger's summary of the general case: *"cognitive drift of that surgeon is
present in every profession and in every human being."*

## Consequences for professional advice

The most immediate practical implication: professional advisors whose
income depends on a particular outcome will systematically shade their
analysis toward that outcome, even when they believe they are being
objective. Munger's list:

- Commercial real estate brokers' presentations are never, in his long
  experience, "even within hailing distance of objective truth."
- Management consultants' reports invariably conclude that the problem
  requires more management consulting.
- Investment bankers' acquisition analyses consistently support whatever
  the CEO wants to do.
- Auditors whose fees are paid by management will bless accounting
  practices that a genuinely independent analyst would not.

The general antidotes Munger proposes: (1) especially distrust professional
advice when it is especially beneficial to the advisor; (2) learn enough of
the advisor's trade to evaluate the advice yourself; (3) double-check,
disbelieve, or replace what you are told to the degree that objective thought
warrants.

This is the same insight Buffett encodes in the Berkshire culture: *"Always
tell us the bad news promptly. It is only the good news that can wait."* The
injunction is necessary precisely because the natural drift of incentive-
caused bias runs in the opposite direction — people bring good news readily
and bad news reluctantly.

## Persian Messenger Syndrome

The specific case where bad news is suppressed rather than distorted is
what Munger calls Persian Messenger Syndrome, named for the practice of
ancient Persian rulers who sometimes executed messengers bringing unwelcome
news. The modern version is non-lethal but structurally identical: it is
genuinely dangerous in many careers to be the bearer of unwelcome
information.

Munger's examples: lawyers who carry on to disaster rather than recommend
an unwelcome but wise settlement; union negotiators and employer
representatives who know this and must manage around it; the CBS chairman
William Paley, who was hostile to bad news and consequently *"lived in a
cocoon of unreality, from which he made one bad deal after another."*

The Berkshire antidote is structural: make it known that bad news is always
welcome and will never damage the messenger's standing. The injunction is
promulgated repeatedly and explicitly because the natural organisational
pressure runs the other way.

## The accounting system as moral instrument

Munger's most original observation on incentive-caused bias concerns the
design of systems rather than the behaviour of individuals. He argues that
the people who create good accounting systems, sound audit processes, and
resistant control mechanisms are among the most important moral contributors
to civilisation — because they have made harmful behaviour physically
difficult to sustain, rather than relying on individuals to resist temptation
by willpower.

The cash register is his primary example. John Patterson, owner of a small
store where employees were stealing him blind, acquired two cash registers
and immediately moved to profit. He promptly closed the store, went into the
cash register business, and created what became the National Cash Register
Company. *"Bad behaviour is intensely habit-forming when it is rewarded.
And so the cash register was a great moral instrument when it was created."*
Patterson did not lecture his employees about honesty; he made dishonesty
structurally harder.

The Westinghouse hotel-loan case is the accounting counterpart. Westinghouse
executives, envious of GE's lending profits, expanded into ninety-five-
percent-of-value construction loans on hotel developments. Sound accounting
would have required booking almost no profit — or a loss — on each loan at
origination, reflecting the high credit risk. Instead, accounting that showed
high starting income was used and blessed by internal and external auditors.
The result was billions of dollars in losses. Munger's assessment of blame:
*"Most blame on the accountants and other senior people who created the
accounting system. These people became the equivalent of an armored car
cash-carrying service that suddenly decided to dispense with vehicles and
have unarmed midgets hand-carry its customers' cash through slums in open
bushel baskets."* The problem was not the lending officer's character; it
was the accounting architecture that made self-serving behaviour easy and
invisible.

## The Federal Express example: structure beats supervision

The clearest single illustration of incentive structure primacy over
personnel quality is Munger's Federal Express example from Talk 2. FedEx
had a problem: its package-sorting operation, which had to run through
the night, was chronically slow. The packages were not getting sorted in
time for the morning flights. Management tried supervision, appeals, and
exhortation — none of it worked.

The solution was accidental in its discovery but exact in its logic. The
company had been paying package sorters by the hour. Someone changed the
compensation to pay per shift — a flat rate for the shift, with workers
free to leave once all packages were sorted. The results were immediate
and dramatic: workers began helping each other, sorted efficiently, and
were consistently done in time.

The lesson Munger draws is stark. The workers were not lazy or
indifferent to begin with; the incentive structure was wrong. Hourly
pay turned completion time into an enemy — workers were, rationally, in
no hurry to finish, because finishing meant the income stopped. Shift
pay turned completion time into a friend — every minute saved after the
work was done was a minute of free time. The manager who had found the
solution had not changed the workers; he had changed the architecture.

This is what Munger means when he calls incentive structure design the
most important single act of management: *"Perhaps the most important
rule in management is 'Get the incentives right.'"* Personnel quality,
supervision intensity, and motivational culture all matter, but they
all operate downstream of the incentive architecture.

## Incentive-caused bias in free-market capitalism

Munger is careful to note that the same tendency that causes so much damage
also makes capitalism work. Free-market capitalists survive by keeping costs
below revenues in brutal competition with other owners; they have a strong
incentive to prevent waste. *"The extreme success of free-market capitalism
as an economic system owes much to its prevention of many bad effects from
incentive-caused bias."* Replace owners with salaried state employees and
you get a substantial reduction in efficiency, because each employee's
incentive structure now pushes toward something other than eliminating waste.

The implication is not that incentives are bad, but that incentive structure
design is the most important single act of management — more important than
personnel selection, more important than strategy, more important than
culture. Culture, personnel, and strategy all operate through the incentive
structure. Get the structure wrong and the other elements cannot compensate.
*"Perhaps the most important rule in management is 'Get the incentives
right.'"*

## The moral injury of bad system design

Munger extends the system-design argument into explicit moral territory.
Creating a system that is easy to cheat is not a neutral act: it is, in
his phrase, "a great moral injury on the people who work for you." The
reasoning is precise: if you make cheating easy and financially
rewarding, you activate Reward-Superresponse Tendency in otherwise decent
people, drift them toward dishonest behaviour, and then — when the
behaviour becomes normalised — reinforce it through Social-Proof Tendency.
You have not merely failed to prevent bad behaviour; you have engineered it.

The California workers' compensation insurance system is Munger's worked
example. The system, as designed, made fraudulent claims easy to file and
difficult to disprove. The result was double-digit claims expense as a
percentage of payroll in many industries — not primarily because California
workers were less honest than others, but because the system's architecture
made fraud a low-risk, high-reward activity. The incentive structure had
done its work on the available population.

Munger's comparison: Mormon-owned businesses in California — operating
in the same legal and regulatory environment, paying the same workers'
compensation premiums in theory — had workers' compensation expense
running at roughly two percent. The Mormon community's social and
religious norms created an additional accountability layer that the state
system lacked. The difference was not the employees; it was the
architecture of accountability.

The Salomon Brothers case provides a complementary illustration of what
happens when the wrong appeal is made to the wrong motive. When the
Treasury securities trading improprieties became known internally,
Salomon's general counsel advised CEO John Gutfreund to report the
violations to regulators on the grounds that it was the right and moral
thing to do. Gutfreund did not act. Munger's critique is not of the
counsel's moral judgment but of his tactical intelligence: Ben Franklin's
maxim is "If you would persuade, appeal to interest, not to reason."
The general counsel should have framed the case in terms of Gutfreund's
self-interest — "If this comes out another way, it will destroy you and
the firm" — rather than in terms of moral obligation. The incentive
argument might have worked; the moral argument, with a man whose
incentives ran in the other direction, did not. This is itself an
illustration of incentive-caused bias in reverse: knowing that others'
decisions are dominated by their incentive structures, the effective
persuader frames every argument in incentive terms.

## Serpico Syndrome: incentives combined with social proof

When incentive-caused bias is combined with Social-Proof Tendency (Tendency
#15), the result is what Munger calls Serpico Syndrome — named for Frank
Serpico's experience in a near-totally corrupt New York police division in
the 1970s. Serpico was nearly murdered for refusing to participate in the
corruption. The mechanism: social proof communicated that taking bribes was
normal and acceptable; incentives created the financial pull; and the
combination produced near-universal corruption, self-reinforcing as each
additional participant's behaviour strengthened the social proof for the
next. This is a [[Lollapalooza Effect]]: neither incentives nor social proof
alone would have produced the result; their confluence did.

## The investing implications

For investors, incentive-caused bias has several direct applications:

**Evaluating advisors.** The investment banker whose fee is contingent on a
deal closing has an incentive structure that makes objective advice
structurally improbable — not because investment bankers are dishonest, but
because the bias operates subconsciously. Buffett's rule: *"If a CEO is
enthused about a particularly foolish acquisition, both his internal staff
and his outside advisors will come up with whatever projections are needed
to justify his stance."*

**Evaluating management.** A CEO whose compensation plan creates large
incentives for hitting quarterly earnings targets will face strong
subconscious pressure to manage reported earnings toward those targets —
through restructuring charges, reserve releases, or more creative methods.
This does not require dishonest intent. See [[Earnings Integrity]] for
the specific accounting mechanisms.

**Evaluating compensation structure.** The question to ask of any management
team is not "are these good people?" but "does the incentive structure make
good behaviour easy and bad behaviour hard?" Good people in bad incentive
structures systematically drift toward bad behaviour. Bad people in good
incentive structures are constrained by the structure. The structure dominates.

**Evaluating auditors and analysts.** Sell-side analysts are paid partly
through investment banking relationships that depend on positive coverage;
auditors are selected and paid by the management they audit. Neither can be
assumed to be fully independent, even when their individual practitioners
are decent. The bias is structural, not personal.

## Annual meeting additions: Buffett and Munger on corporate compensation (1994–2024)

[[sources/Buffett and Munger Unscripted]] adds several formulations and
case studies not captured in the *Psychology of Human Misjudgment* source.

**Stock options as incentive corruption.** The annual meetings contain
Buffett and Munger's most sustained critique of stock-option accounting.
The mechanism: when executives are awarded options and options are not
expensed at grant, the company has created an incentive to manage reported
earnings in ways that boost the stock price regardless of underlying economic
performance. The incentive bias is not toward building a better business;
it is toward *appearing* to build a better business. Munger's formulation
across multiple meetings: the accounting permission to not expense options
was one of the most corruption-producing decisions the accounting profession
ever made. See also [[concepts/Earnings Integrity]] for the full EBITDA
and options-expensing treatment.

**"Blood brother of evil."** Munger's phrase for quarterly earnings
expectations — the Wall Street practice of guiding analysts to specific
EPS targets and then being graded on whether the target is met to the penny.
The incentive it creates is unambiguous: managers who can hit the number by
legitimate means will do so; managers who cannot will find illegitimate means.
The earnings management strategies that follow — channel stuffing, reserve
manipulation, one-time-item reclassification — are each predictable outputs
of the incentive, not aberrations. Buffett explicitly does not provide quarterly
earnings guidance for this reason. The damage is not to Berkshire (which
doesn't play the game) but to the companies that do, where the practice
becomes self-reinforcing.

**Heroin metaphor.** A related formulation from the meetings: once a company
starts managing earnings upward through accounting choices, stopping becomes
progressively harder — the same dynamic as addiction. Each quarter's managed
result raises the baseline expectation for the next. The company that begins
by deferring a legitimate expense for one quarter ends, after several years,
with reserves that are structurally inadequate and a management culture where
gaming is the norm. Munger's phrase: *"earnings management is like starting
on heroin — it's easier to start than to stop."*

**GEICO compensation grid.** The annual meetings include discussion of how
GEICO aligns manager incentives with genuine performance: compensation tied
to two and only two variables — policies in force (growth) and underwriting
profit per policy (quality). Managers cannot win on volume alone (which would
incentivise writing unprofitable policies) and cannot win on margin alone
(which would incentivise cherry-picking only the easiest risks). The grid
is designed so that the incentive-caused bias pulls toward actual business
quality rather than metrics that mimic it. This is the constructive
application of the same insight — structure the incentive, and the behaviour
follows.

**NICO's no-layoff policy as incentive alignment.** National Indemnity's
willingness to shrink premium volume 85% from peak to trough was possible
partly because of a management commitment to not lay off employees during
soft markets. The standard incentive at most insurance companies is to
maintain volume in order to keep staff occupied — which means writing
business at inadequate prices just to keep the book full. NICO removed this
incentive: the staff knew their jobs were secure regardless of volume. The
result was that the underwriting decision could be made on pure economics
without the social pressure of employment consequences. The no-layoff
policy was the structural enabler of underwriting discipline.

**Wells Fargo: three mistakes compounded.** The annual meetings include
a candid account of Wells Fargo's account-opening scandal (revealed 2016)
as an incentive design failure. The incentive to open new accounts was
built into the compensation system at the branch level. When the legitimate
path to meeting targets was exhausted, the illegitimate path (opening
accounts without customer consent) was taken — predictably. Munger's comment:
Wells Fargo made three mistakes — they created the perverse incentive, they
ignored early warnings, and then they fought the regulators. The first was
the root cause; the second and third were made worse by the same human
tendency (avoiding evidence of one's own mistakes) that Tendency #4 in
the *Psychology* speech documents as denial.

## Contradictions and tensions

Incentive-caused bias appears to sit in tension with the Buffett/Munger
approach of trusting good managers with full autonomy. If incentives
systematically corrupt behaviour, how can Berkshire safely delegate to
subsidiary managers with minimal oversight?

The resolution is structural rather than psychological: Berkshire's
compensation designs are built to align incentives accurately. Subsidiary
managers are charged for capital they employ at a high internal rate; they
are credited when they return capital to Omaha; and their pay is tied
directly to results in the area they control, not to Berkshire overall.
Under this structure, incentive-caused bias pulls *toward* honest reporting
and conservative capital use, not away from it. The trust is not naive;
it is architecturally supported.
